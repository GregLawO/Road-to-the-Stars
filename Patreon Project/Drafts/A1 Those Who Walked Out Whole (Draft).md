# Those Who Walked Out Whole

*“The clinics were built around a single apparatus, the Med-Pod. Patients entered it ill or broken and, by their own accounts, left restored. The accounts do not gush. They are written in a stunned, flat register, like those of survivors of an accident who have not yet decided whether to believe they are alive. Patients crossed borders to reach the clinics. Families waited to see a cure work on a neighbor before trusting it with their own. By the 2060s the clinics stood in all the larger economies, though standing everywhere is a different matter from being accepted.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A1 · Hannah Shepard (22)*

The floor of the exit lobby was gray stone, and the cold came through the soles of Hannah Shepard's shoes. She sat in a leather chair with her dress blues folded on the chair beside her. A glass bowl of fresh flowers stood on the low table next to a coffee service with real cups. On the far wall, at the height where a license should hang, there was only paint.

She had been in the lobby since Monday. A woman in gray scrubs brought coffee every few hours and spoke in the same level voice each time. Nobody wore a name tag. A screen on the wall showed a sealed pod lid under white light, and in three days the lid had not moved.

The frosted doors stood at the end of the lobby. Behind them a passage ran back to a stair, and the stair went down to the lower building. Danny was down there. Hannah had learned the passage by watching it. Each time the doors opened, someone in scrubs came through and turned left toward a side door. She had counted forty seconds from the doors to that side door, which opened onto the east lot, away from the street. She had written the count in her notebook.

The Navy had taught her to notice what was missing. In this room she noted what was extra. Her notebook held the list: polished stone floor, deep leather chairs, fresh flowers replaced every morning, coffee in porcelain cups from a silver pot, soft lamps in place of ceiling lights, a wall of dark paneled wood, and a private entrance from the street with its own awning. A clinic with no license on its wall and no name tag on any staff member had built a room for people who could pay.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A1 · Lucía Ferreira*

### *A border clinic, 2058*

The slip of paper had gone soft in Lucía's fist, and the ink had begun to run. She had not looked at it since the clerk pressed it into her hand. The river had been shallower than the smuggler promised and colder than anything she had imagined. She had carried Mateo across it on her hip with her skirt knotted above her knees. Through two layers of wet cloth she had felt his heat, dry and papery. He was six years old.

His kidneys had failed in the spring. The doctors in the capital had told her, kindly and without meeting her eyes, to make him comfortable.

The clinic stood three streets back from the water. It was a squat building of pale concrete with a green cross painted over the door. It looked like any pharmacy she had passed without a glance. Inside there were plastic chairs bolted to the floor, a water cooler with no cups, and a television murmuring about a football match. The ordinariness unsettled her more than marble would have. A place that cured the dying ought to look as if it knew what it was.

The line ran the length of the waiting room and out the side door into a yard. Families sat on upturned crates and folded blankets. Nobody pushed and nobody called out. Lucía had seen crowds at hospitals, and they always carried a low, ragged noise of pleading. This one was quiet in a different way. Everyone watched the double doors at the back of the room.

A farmer's wife from the uplands made room for her on the floor. She had been there since the previous morning. “You don't just go in,” the woman said. “You sit and you watch the doors. That one yours?”

“Yes.”

“He's small.”

“He has not been eating.” Lucía shifted Mateo higher on her lap. “Why is everyone watching the doors?”

“My neighbor's boy went through them on Tuesday with a growth in his throat. The whole yard is waiting for him to come out.” The woman tugged her blanket straight. “A grown man will say he's well so his family can go home. A boy doesn't know how to lie yet. When that one walks out, we will know what we are looking at.”

“And if he walks out?”

“Then we watch him a week. Fast things come undone fast.” The woman's eyes went to Mateo and came away again. She said nothing more, and Lucía did not ask her to.

A board above the clerk's desk said that numbers were called in order and that the day's numbers closed at sundown. Through the side door, the light in the yard had turned the color of weak tea.

Lucía understood the woman completely. She had spent her savings on three treatments that promised everything. She had buried her mother-in-law's faith along with the second one. She knew what hope cost and how little of it one person could afford. So she sat on the floor with Mateo across her knees and the slip in her fist, and she watched the doors, because watching was the only thing left that she could do for him.

A gray-haired man came out on his own feet and wept without a sound. A girl of about twelve carried a crutch she no longer seemed to need, as if she were embarrassed by it. Each time the doors opened, every head turned together. Nobody cheered.

Mateo stirred against her collarbone and said something in his sleep. She smoothed the damp hair off his forehead. His skin was hotter than before. She told herself the doors would open for them soon. She told herself the number was only paper.

The double doors banged open.

A boy shot through.

Barefoot. Thin. Laughing.

He crossed the tiles at a run. His feet slapped. He skidded past her knee.

Iker. The neighbor's boy.

His mother screamed his name and caught him off the floor.

The farmer's wife did not move. Her hands lay flat on the blanket and her mouth was shut. Her fingers were counting. A week of days.

The clerk lifted a card from the desk.

“Forty-one,” she called.

Lucía looked down at the slip.

Her arms tightened around Mateo.

She opened her fingers.

Fifty-eight.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A1 · Anselm Roque*

### *A district clinic, 2059*

The closure order lay on Anselm Roque's knee in a cream ministry envelope, still sealed. Its flap had gone tacky in the damp heat of the waiting room. He had signed for it at seven that morning. In eleven years with the licensing department he had never carried an order that weighed so little.

The file behind it was thick. Private hospitals had complained, and the pharmacists' guild, and an insurance board whose claims had dried up in a single season. Every complaint used the same words: unlicensed practice, fraud against the sick. His instructions were plain. Inspect the premises, seal the doors, post the notice, and be home before dark.

The building told him little. It had been a parcel depot once, and the sorting racks had left pale ladders on the far wall where the paint had been shielded from the sun. Someone had hung a child's drawing over the worst of the damp. Someone else had propped the side door open with a bucket to let the afternoon in. Nothing was hidden, and that was the first thing he could not account for. Every fraud he had closed had kept very good locks.

The bench was warm from the people who had sat on it before him. Nobody in the room was in a hurry, and that was the second thing. People waiting in a hospital fidgeted and watched the clock. These people sat with their hands in their laps, and the room smelled of soap and wet wool.

His own father had died in a ward that cost more per night than Anselm earned in a month. The bill had outlived him by six years. Anselm did not think of it as a grudge. He thought of it as a figure he knew by heart, and it sat in his chest beside the envelope.

“Government,” said the old woman next to him. There was no edge in it. “You hold the letter in both hands. They all do.”

He looked down. He was holding it in both hands.

“It is a routine visit,” he said.

“Is it.” She tipped her chin at the envelope. “Then why haven't you opened it?”

He had no answer ready, and she did not wait for one. “My brother went in Tuesday. The neighbors carried him in on a door, the way you carry a man to his grave. Today they say he walks out.”

“And if they are wrong?”

“Then I carry him home on it again.” She flexed one hand against her knee. “I have the arms for it. I would rather not need them.”

The doors swung open at a quarter past four. A man came through with a door under one arm, a plain wooden door with its hinge plates still screwed to the edge. He carried it as if it weighed nothing. He looked like someone who had been ill for years and had just been told it was over, a little embarrassed and mostly hungry. The old woman put her hand over her mouth.

He stopped at the end of Anselm's bench. “Mind your feet,” he said, and turned the door sideways to pass. He smelled of soap.

Anselm drew his boots back. It was the smallest courtesy anyone had offered him in a year, and something under his ribs loosened.

The old woman did not call out to her brother. She watched him all the way to the street door. When it had closed she looked at Anselm's hands again. “Well,” she said. “You can put it down now.”

He put the envelope on the bench between them.

The order said fraud. Fraud needed a patient who failed later, and the file held not one. A man carried in on a door had carried it out under one arm. A career of paper had given Anselm a form for every lie that could be forged. It had given him none for a thing he could simply watch go by.

He slid the envelope into his bag and did not break the seal.

He took out the inspection form.

He uncapped his pen.

The form had a box marked *Findings*.

The box was an inch tall.

The nib scratched. His thumb whitened on the barrel.

Two words. A line under them. His name.

*Nothing found.*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A1 · Hannah Shepard (28)*

### *Galveston, 2059*

A name was taped to every exit door of the Galveston complex, in plain block letters, one name to a door. The staff plan had been in force for years, and Hannah Shepard read it as she walked in from the car park. At the end of each night shift the person named at a door counted the staff out through it and signed the sheet. The count at the staff door was Okafor's. She was already standing there with her clipboard.

Hannah was on leave. She had come to meet Danny for breakfast when his shift ended. Her left hand held a paper cup of coffee she had bought at the gate, and the staff door was ten steps ahead.

“Morning, Ms. Shepard,” Okafor said. “Eleven minutes to the change.”

“I'll wait out of your way.”

The grounds were quiet. From the staff door she could see the car park, the fence line, and the gate, and no one walked any of them. The guards on the lower building's side stayed at their posts inside, as they did every night. Nothing patrolled. She distrusted a place that ran this smoothly, and she watched it.

She noticed three small things, and each had a reason. The third lamp in the car park was out. It had been dead eleven days, and the maintenance sheet said a part was on order. The Thursday delivery van had not arrived. Delays happened on the coast road. The stool at the gate was empty, and the guard's radio sat on the seat, switched on, with a faint voice running through it. His coffee cup was gone.

She set her cup on the window ledge and took out her phone to call the gate in.

The window went white.

Sound came second.

The door hit her. The floor rose. Her shoulder struck tile.

Glass crossed the corridor. Alarms started. Smoke rolled in at knee height.

Her left forearm burned. She pressed it against her ribs.

She got up.

Doors opened along the ward. A nurse. Two orderlies. A technician with a cup still in her fist.

“East lot,” Hannah said. “Walk.”

They ran anyway. She ran behind them and herded them. She took the staff plan's route, the one she had walked twice on her last visit: the corridor, the side door, forty seconds. It led away from the lower building. The blast had come from that side.

The east lot lay on the far side of the main building from the break in the wall. No glass could reach it. The staff stood on the asphalt, bent over, coughing.

Behind her, from the lower building's side, came a burst of heavy steps. Many of them, fast, metal on concrete. Then nothing. She took it for machinery shifting in the wreck.

She counted.

“Okafor's door,” she said. “Call it.”

“Nine.”

“Dev's door.”

“Twelve,” a man called.

“Lund's.”

“Ten,” said the nurse beside her.

Thirty-one. The night sheet said thirty-one.

“Say it again,” Hannah said.

They said it again. Thirty-one.

Her knees went. She sat down on the asphalt. A medic was at her elbow and had her arm turned over.

“Ma'am, I need you to lie back.”

“Not until it's confirmed.”

“It's confirmed. We counted twice.”

She lay back. The sky was orange at one edge. The medic wrapped her arm and the pain came up through the wrapping.

The noise stopped dead.

She noticed it the way she would notice a missing step. No alarms, no steps, no voices but the staff's. A radio on the medic's shoulder spoke in a flat voice. “Lower building clear.”

They put her on a gurney and wheeled her in through the east doors. The corridor ceiling slid past in a row of lights. One flickered.

A door banged at the far end.

Her husband came down the corridor at a run, coatless. He slowed when he saw her. He stopped at the gurney and took the rail in both hands. His knuckles went pale.

He looked at her face. Then at the burn. Then at her face again.

She did not wait for him to speak.

“Where is my brother?”

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A1 · Maya Brooks (24)*

### *Marseille, 2062*

The bracelet on her wrist held a number, a barcode, and a strip of blank white plastic where a name should have been printed. Maya Brooks had read it so many times that the digits had stopped being digits. She had been dying in Marseille for fourteen months, and the last three of them had been spent in rooms where nobody said the word.

She counted the exits because she could not stop. Two doors, one to the corridor and one to a utility stair. A window painted shut along its bead. A ceiling vent too narrow for a child. Eleven steps from the bed to the corridor door, and she could walk two of them now. Six years of reading other countries' ministries had trained her to find the way out of a room before she found the chair. The leukemia had taken nearly everything else.

The man came in at noon. He wore a gray coat in July. The coat hung heavier on the left side than the right, and she counted that too. He carried no flowers and no folder. He sat in the visitor's chair and turned it a quarter turn, so that he could watch the door as well as her. She noticed. He noticed her noticing, and something in his face approved.

“Ms. Brooks.” Nobody in the building had used her name in a month. “Your Ankara tariff paper was the best thing your directorate put out last year. It was also the last. You stopped coming in.”

“I am not receiving visitors.”

“You are receiving one.” He said it without apology.

“Who sent you?”

“Somebody who read your paper.” He did not look away. “There is a machine under this clinic. People go in as you are. They come out as they were before. I will not ask you to believe that. I will ask you to let it be tried.”

She had heard this speech from three different men, and each had wanted her savings. Her thumb walked across her fingertips under the blanket. “What does it cost?”

“Nothing you are carrying.” He waited, and she counted the silence. Four seconds. He said it slowly and plainly. “The years it gives back are spent working with the organization. You serve for life. Your family is covered, and treatment continues for as long as you serve. There is one session after the treatment. It stops you from telling anyone outside what the organization does or who runs it. You will know it, and you will not be able to say it.”

A man who stated his terms in order, and paused to let her count, was telling her the truth about the cost. He might still be holding something back. “Whose work?” she said.

“Afterward.”

“Noted.”

She looked at the window, painted shut, and at the wedge of white sky in it. She had run out of better questions.

“Yes,” she said.

Three days later, the frosted doors opened.

She had expected the walk to hurt. It did not. A stair rose from the passage behind the doors, and she took it one step at a time and counted. Her legs held. She did not need the rail. She did not need the wall.

Twelve. Thirteen.

She kept going.

Her right hand tapped against her thigh. Two doors. One stair. One sealed window. She could not make it stop.

The passage opened into a lobby of gray stone, with leather chairs and flowers. Beyond a glass wall lay a car park and the sun. She counted the paces to the glass. She counted the cameras and the doors.

The man in the gray coat stood with his back to the glass, hands in his pockets, so that he could see her come. He had turned his stance the way he had turned his chair.

She stopped three paces short. Her heart was loud in her ears, strong and slow, a beat she had not heard in a year.

He smiled a little, without showing his teeth.

“Sixty more years, for a start,” he said. “Ms. Brooks, what would you do with them?”

Her fingers stopped tapping.

She opened her mouth.
