# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (27)*

### *A hall on the seawall, June 2058*

The name inside the ring was too small to read, so Hannah had carried it to the clinic pharmacy the night before and read it under the magnifier. HANNAH, in square capitals, each line cut clean into the steel. Nobody had asked her to check.

The hall on the seawall held sixty folding chairs in two blocks with an aisle between them. Her side was full by half past one: yard crews in good shirts, clinic nurses, the secretaries from her mother's school office, Rafa Ibarra in a borrowed jacket with the sleeves too long. The other block was empty. In April she had asked Kenji who would sit on his side. He had said he had no one to invite, and that he understood if that was a problem. She had told him it was not.

Two men in dark suits stood against the empty side with their hands folded. They were the two who followed Kenji from room to room and never sat. She nodded to them through the crack in the door, as she did every morning, and they nodded back.

A man in a gray coat stood at the back door. It was June. He watched the room and not the chairs, and she did not know him.

"Wrong coat for the weather," her father said. He stood beside her in his good suit with his steel-toed boots under the trousers, and his hand kept going to his back pocket. His own father's wrench was in it, the initials scratched into the handle. "That door hinge is loose, Darling. I'll want a screwdriver after."

"After, Dad."

Danny came in at ten to two, in a dark suit that pulled across the shoulders. He had driven from Houston that morning and had to be back at his post by eight. He stopped beside them and laid his palm flat against the doorframe.

"Dad brought the toolbox," he said.

"Mom set your place for the reception." Hannah waited. "Okay, so. Still gates and fences?"

"Work's work." He pressed the frame. "Hot out, though."

"I asked where you're posted, Danny. Not about the weather."

"Houston." He took his hand off the frame. "That's all I've got, and it's true."

She let it stand. The music started, and her father put out his arm.

They walked the aisle together. Danny had gone ahead and was already standing in the front row on her mother's side. Ruth said, "Daniel." He answered something Hannah could not hear, kissed her mother's cheek, and crossed the aisle. He sat alone in the first chair of the empty block, his back straight, a few feet from the two men in dark suits. Neither of them looked at him. Her mother sat down with one hand holding the other wrist.

Kenji stood at the front with his back to the wall. Her father put her hand in his.

"Thank you, Dad," Kenji said.

Her father opened his mouth and shut it. "Well," he said. "Mm." His hand went to his back pocket and stayed there through the vows.

At the reception her mother crossed the hall with a napkin in her hand and straightened Kenji's collar, which did not need it. "And who looks after you?" she asked.

"Do you need anything, Mom? I can find you a waiter."

Ruth's hands stopped on the collar. She did not say anything about the word. "Tea, please," she said. "I will ask the other question again another day."

She came back with the cup and turned to face the empty block. She raised her glass to it. "To Kenji's side," she said. Danny lifted his glass without standing.

Hannah had a question ready. Why was her brother sitting on her husband's side of the room? She looked at Kenji. He was looking at the glass in Danny's hand, and he did not look away when she watched him do it.

She did not ask.

At the back door the man in the gray coat raised his glass.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (39)*

### *Galveston, 2059*

**March 2059.** The handover board in the dock office had one row with no name on it. Every row gave a day, a time, a carrier, a driver, and a signature. Thursday, 5:30 to 5:35 a.m.: the carrier was filled in, and the driver's box was empty.

Marchetti stood in front of it with the annual review on a clipboard. She had told me that morning that she did not sign for places she had not stood in, and she had come to the dock in her good heels. She capped and uncapped the pen.

"Who signs this row?" she asked.

"The carrier signs for its driver."

"And the carrier changes drivers every week." She had read the log without asking me for it. "So for five minutes each Thursday morning, a person none of us has met stands at your open dock."

"Yes."

"I will not sign the review with that in it. I will sign it with a line that says the row is closed, and the date it was closed."

"I'll close it tonight. The handover moves to Friday, and the new roster goes to the gates at six tomorrow morning." I squared the board against the wall. "Is that enough, or do you need more?"

"There is a second question." She uncapped the pen again. "If someone takes this clinic, what happens?"

"Nothing leaves. A pod taken from its guards destroys its own core. The designs belong to the foundation, and the foundation is you. What's left is a building."

"And the people in the building?"

"I don't know." I had not said those words to her before. "I have droids on every pod and guards on every door. I don't have an answer for the people that I would put my name to."

She wrote nothing. "Then that is the line I will want a plan for next year." She signed the review and left her pen capped on the clipboard.

I rubbed out Thursday on the board and wrote FRIDAY across the row. Hannah was at home that evening. The roster would reach her in the morning with everyone else's.

**Thursday.** At twenty past five I sat at the monitor desk with the twelve screens in front of me and the earpiece in. Vance was in Duluth that week. Danny had the car park, on channel four.

On screen nine Hannah stood at the staff door at the end of the night shift, one hand on her stomach, watching the dark. She did that most mornings. She had filed three work orders for the third lamp in the car park. Each one had been closed as done.

"Third lamp's still out, sir," Danny said in my ear. "Eleven days. The gate stool's empty, and the cup's gone with the man. The roster says he's on."

"Tell the shift lead."

"Shift lead's inside. I'll hold my post." His breathing was slow. "Van on the service road. No lights. It's riding low."

"Nothing is due today. I moved the handover to Friday." The roster that closed the row was due at the gates at six. "Stay clear, Danny."

"Then it's not ours." A pause. "She's at the staff door, sir. That van's headed for the east wing dock."

I put my thumb under the cover of the alarm plate.

"Stay clear. That's an order."

"I'm walking to it."

His breath came quick and even in my ear.

A car door. Metal on metal. A handle.

On screen nine Hannah turned her head.

The screen went white.

Sound came second.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Helen Marchetti (60)*

### *Houston, 2060*

**February 2060.** The resignation letter lay in the second drawer of her desk, under the stapler, signed and undated. Helen Marchetti took it out, read its three lines, and put it back. She did this before any meeting she did not understand.

The note had come on Monday, by hand, to the firm's front desk. It was addressed to the trustee of the Janus Foundation and asked for one hour. It gave no reason and no name. Whitcombe's faction met on Friday, and Helen meant to carry an answer into that room.

He came at six on Wednesday, after the staff had gone home. He was about forty-five, in a plain gray suit, and when he sat down across the conference table he laid both hands on it, open and empty. For the second time in her life a man had come to bargain with her and brought nothing to hold. Two of Vance's fighters sat in the corridor with the door open.

"You have brought nothing to read," she said.

"Nothing in writing. I thought you would rather hear it first." His voice was pleasant and plain. "I speak for a group of people. We call ourselves Cerberus."

"Who signs for Cerberus?"

"Nobody signs. We are not that kind of party."

"Then you are not a party, and I do not bargain with someone who is not one." She uncapped the pen and wrote the date and the word he had said. "Tell me what you want. I will record that you declined to give your own name."

"That is accurate." He did not look at the pen. "The foundation licenses designs. Some of those licenses go to firms and ministries you choose. We would like to be told in advance which designs you will let out, and we would like some of those licenses to go to firms we name. The foundation would lose nothing it has agreed to lose."

"How did you find me?"

"Your clients. Each treated client remembers being treated, and each remembers exactly one name and nothing about where or how. We asked enough of them. The names led to Senator Whitcombe's faction, and her faction led to your signature."

Helen kept the pen moving. "Mr. Strauss refused three offers for his patents in 2058. A law firm, in January, April, and October."

"Three courteous refusals. Our firm enjoyed each one." He let the room be quiet. "I would rather not ask a fourth time through a law firm."

"What are you offering?"

"Whatever you wish. Name it."

"No." She tried to picture herself signing, as she would for any offer, to see what it cost. She could not picture it at all. She had made Himura put the clause in writing and initial it, nine years ago, and she had never expected to find out what it did.

"You have not heard what I would pay."

"I have not, and I will not. I will also tell you that I will repeat everything you have said tonight to the people it concerns."

"I expected that." He stood without hurry. "Senator Whitcombe asked me for a day this afternoon and took the request back before I reached the door. I thought you might be quicker."

He took a white card from his breast pocket, laid it on the table, and left. She picked it up. It had a telephone number on one side and no name anywhere.

**That night.** Himura's office was lit by one lamp. Vance sat by the door in his gray coat with the green book open on his knee. Helen read her notes aloud, slowly and in order, and neither man interrupted her.

When she reached the name, Vance's pen stopped. He wrote one word, drew a line under it, and turned the book so she could see it. *Cerberus.*

"Three offers," he said. "One name."

"Yes." She laid her notes on the desk. "I asked what they wanted before I asked who leads them. That was an error of order. He would not have answered, but I should have asked."

"Did he say who leads them?" Himura squared her pages against the edge of the desk.

"No. Senator Whitcombe was approached this afternoon and refused." She waited until he looked up. "The paper held because neither of us could sign. The next person he asks may not be one of us."

"Which door did he leave by?" Vance asked.

"The front. A gray car. I did not see the plate."

"Next time you will."

"There will be no next time, Mr. Vance."

"I'd still like the plate, ma'am."

She set the card on the desk. Himura turned it over. The back was blank.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (40)*

### *The Gulf coast, spring 2060*

**April 2060.** Hannah's name was on the visitor sheet at the plant gate, spelled right, and the line for her escort was blank. I had left it blank. If she came, I would be the escort. If she did not, nobody would need to sign for her.

The plant stood behind a double fence on the Gulf, finished in February. No one outside the foundation had been past that gate. I had not shown it to Hannah. Until last night I had not told her what the two men who followed me were, either.

I had begun with Danny, because she asked about Danny first. She wanted to know what her brother had been doing in the car park at half past five, outside her roster. I told her I had asked him to watch her since 2056, and that he had asked me not to tell her where he worked. I told her the name Marchetti had been given in February was Cerberus. I told her about three offers for Oyama's patents in 2058, and that I did not know who had sent the van. I told her the two men beside me were machines. She did not interrupt. When I stopped, she said, "Tomorrow. Show me the rest," and went out to her car.

Vance stood at the gate in his gray coat. He had walked the fence at five.

"Every post is covered," he said. "She's ten minutes out."

"She said nine. It's ten to."

"She'll be early. She'll count the posts." He took a slow breath through his nose. "You told her about the guards."

"Yes."

"Then she's going to want to know what else isn't what it looks like."

"I'll answer what I can. Is there something you'd have wanted me to leave out?"

"Not my call."

My two guards stood a pace apart at the gate post, as they did every day. I squared the clipboard against the edge of the sill and squared it again. Then I went into the gatehouse and washed my hands twice at the small sink.

Her car came up the access road at ten to nine.

She stopped at the gate and got out and shut the door. Her father's tape was in her jacket pocket. She looked at the two men beside me. She had nodded to them every morning for four years, and she nodded to them now, and they nodded back as they had been told to.

"In March of 2058 you said you'd remember I said yet," I said. "This is the yet."

She did not answer. She turned the car keys over once in her hand and looked at the open gate.

I did not step aside, and I did not step in front of it. She had the keys in one hand and the gate in front of her, and she had not said which she would use.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (41)*

### *The Gulf yard, September 2061*

**September 2061.** The owner plate on the starship's hull was empty. A man on a lift stood beside it with the last rivet in his glove and waited for someone to tell him what to stamp. Nobody did.

The ship stood on the long cradle where its keel had gone down four years before, three castings joined with a hull raised around them. The drive ring was set. The reactor was running at idle, a tokamak, built by Oyama's own crews. I knew it would lift. I had known that since the day I drew the ring.

Strauss waited at the foot of the gantry with a clipboard. He still read every sheet to its last line. "The first section of the yard in orbit is in the hold," he said. "A foundry that casts in zero gravity, in pieces. Gundarium Alpha gets cast up there or it doesn't get cast."

"And the second hold?"

"Crates for the Moon, and robots to unpack them." He turned the steel ring on his finger. "The weather window opens at ten, not eleven."

"Then we go at ten."

Marchetti stood a few paces behind him with a folder under her arm. She had come from Houston that morning. "I have a page for you to sign," she said. "I will hold it until the ship is up."

At ten the crane gauges dropped to zero. The hum came through the gantry steel and into my hands on the rail. The hull came off the cradle without a sound, ten meters, then forty, then a hundred, and the cradle stood empty under it. The crews along the fence held their caps and said nothing. Nobody cheered until the ship was a point of light.

Forty minutes later the radio gave the figures. The first section had released into orbit, and the ship was continuing to the Moon with the second hold. I wrote them down. The window had opened on time. I wrote that down too.

Marchetti opened the folder on the gantry rail and turned it toward me. "Janus grants Oyama the right to build and sell a Primitive grade of the drive," she said. "Mr. Strauss has yards in four countries asking him what lifts a hull. If you sign, the first thing of yours that anyone can buy and copy leaves this room. Who signs for the foundation after you?"

"You do."

"Then I will sign second, and I will read the whole of it aloud first." She did not move the folder. "They will come for it."

"They'll come for the seller," I said. "Oyama sells."

"That's what Oyama's for," Strauss said. "Skip what it does. What does it cost?"

"Time, and nobody asks who drew it. Is that enough, or do you need more?"

"It's enough. Friday is better than Monday."

Marchetti uncapped her pen and held it out. The line at the bottom of the page was the one that let Oyama sell the drive. I took the pen.

I did not sign, and I did not put it down.
