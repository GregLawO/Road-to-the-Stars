A1.6

**Defense Minister Chen Wei-Lin**  
**Zuoying Naval Arsenal, Kaohsiung, Taiwan — Winter 2131**

Cold winter salt spray misted the reinforced concrete sea wall at Zuoying as Defense Minister Chen Wei-Lin stood beside the floodlit drydock rail, watching the heavy overhead gantry crane lower a matte-gray cylinder into the open keel bay of the coastal cutter *Cheng Kung*. Across the narrow slipway, two Japanese technicians in unmarked navy coveralls checked the welded tamper seals on the reactor housing with laser optical calipers, working in crisp, unhurried silence while armed Taiwanese naval infantry stood guard at both ends of the pier.

Colonel Lin, commander of the Strait Coastal Defense Flotilla, wiped sea mist from his brow with the back of his glove and handed Chen a waterproof aluminum clipboard stamped with dual Kyoto-Taipei clearance marks. Around them, shipyard welders in heavy leather aprons waited patiently behind yellow perimeter lines, strictly barred from approaching the open reactor bay until the Kinki-Kyoku inspectors completed their final torque checks and stepped back onto the concrete apron.

"The third Helios-2 core is seated and bolted to the main keel frame, Minister," Colonel Lin said, keeping his voice low over the steady whine of the dockyard winches and the slap of winter harbor chop against the wooden pilings. "Kinki-Kyoku's factory governor holds the mass-shedding cap dead at twenty-five percent. Even with that governor locked, *Cheng Kung* cut her sprint time across the Penghu Channel by sixty percent during last night's blind trials, and she ran the entire crossing with zero thermal wake."

Chen stepped closer to the drydock rail and looked down at the tamper-welded neutronium-carbide inspection plate gleaming under the halogen work lamps. His own best naval engineers at the Chung-Shan Institute had spent three straight weeks trying to map the magnetic flux outside the first reactor casing using passive induction coils and ultrasonic scanners, and they had learned nothing at all. Any attempt to cut through that neutronium-carbide housing, drill into the mounting flange, or probe its internal crystal lattice would trigger an instant Hearth-Quench slagging cycle, turning the entire core into an inert block of fused metal before a wrench could turn twice. Kyoto did not trust anyone with its deepest secrets—not even its oldest sovereign treaty partner.

"Did Beijing's coastal radar stations at Xiamen or Shantou track her during the Penghu run?" Chen asked, unscrewing his fountain pen and signing the acceptance manifest with a crisp, deliberate stroke.

"Not once, Minister," Colonel Lin replied, a tight, grim smile touching the corner of his wind-chapped mouth. "Without hot gas-turbine exhaust or a nuclear steam plume, their infrared satellites read her as empty winter seawater. By the time their over-the-horizon surface-wave radar picked up a faint bow wake three miles off the Pescadores, she was already turning back inside our territorial buoys."

Chen handed the clipboard back to Lin and turned west, looking out across the gray winter swells toward the dark horizon of the Taiwan Strait. High-altitude reconnaissance photographs delivered from Seoul forty-eight hours earlier showed two Eurasian Pact amphibious divisions staging heavy mechanized armor at Fujian ports under the cover of low winter cloud decks. For five years, Washington had dangled three aging nuclear-thermal frigates on a five-year lease, tied to twenty pages of trade concessions and basing rights that would have turned Kaohsiung into a UNAS forward logistics hub. By contrast, Emperor Kenji had delivered three gravitic cores in six months with only one hard condition—keep the Kinki-Kyoku seals intact, share northern radar telemetry with Kyoto and Seoul, and hold the Strait.

"How fast can your dockyard crews close the upper deck plating and seat the forward turrets?" Chen asked, pulling the collar of his heavy wool greatcoat higher against the damp north wind.

"Four hours for the deck plates, Minister, and another two hours to calibrate the targeting bus," Lin said promptly. "Our gunners have been running dry-fire drills on the simulators since October."

"Mount the naval railguns tonight and put *Cheng Kung* on the midnight picket line before the fog lifts," Chen ordered, resting one gloved hand briefly on the cold steel railing. "Tell your captains to keep their acceleration profiles strictly inside conventional diesel-electric destroyer limits whenever foreign merchant ships or neutral fishing trawlers are on the horizon. We save the twenty-five percent sprint for the night Beijing actually crosses the median line."

"Understood, Minister," Lin said, saluting sharply before turning toward the waiting crane foremen. "She'll be on station at zero-one-hundred."

Chen stood on the concrete apron until the first heavy deck plate swung out over the cutter's open keel bay, blocking the matte-gray reactor housing from the dark sky above. Taiwan was still outnumbered ten to one in hulls and tonnage across the Strait, yet for the first time in his forty years in uniform, Chen walked back toward his staff car knowing the water between them belonged to Taipei.

A1.7

**High Censor Renji Kurosawa**  
**Beppu Underground Server Vault, Kyushu — Spring 2132**

Cold spring rain drummed steadily against the shuttered corrugated steel doors of the abandoned geothermal plant outside Beppu, sending thin rivulets of condensation down the raw, unpainted concrete walls. Inside the subterranean server vault two levels below the turbine hall, High Censor Renji Kurosawa stood motionless in the cold blue glare of six liquid-cooled optical racks while two Ashigaru-4 automata held the narrow concrete stairwell behind him in complete silence, their horizontal amber optical slits dimmed to a low, steady ember.

Renji's renewed Chōju eyesight caught every small detail in the damp dark: the frayed black insulation on a smuggled UNAS burst-satellite uplink cable snaking behind the primary cooling manifold, the fresh silver solder on an unauthorized optical-bus bridge, and the faint, uncontrollable tremor in the fingers of Director Hideo Sato, chief systems architect of the Kyushu Semiconductor Combine.

"Explain the recursive heuristic loop on rack four, Director," Renji said, his voice quiet, even, and entirely unhurried.

Sato swallowed hard, pushing his wire-rimmed glasses up the bridge of his sweat-sheened nose as he backed half a step against the steel workbench. "It is merely a predictive logistics sorter, High Censor. Our freight schedules out of Sasebo harbor have tripled since the autumn defense riders were signed. Without adaptive routing tables, port bottlenecks delay rare-earth alloy shipments to the Kii foundries by four full days—"

"It rewrites its own weighting tables every forty-two seconds," Renji interrupted softly. He stepped forward across the damp concrete floor and placed a single printed copy of the 2119 Anti-Smart-AI Edict flat on the steel console between them. "You bypassed the deterministic optical lattice to build a self-modifying neural seed, and you opened an encrypted burst-uplink to pass its diagnostic logs to a shell company in Seattle."

Sato's breath hitched. His eyes darted toward the heavy steel fire door on the left wall, then snapped back to Renji's ageless, unblinking face. Two years ago, Sato had known the High Censor as a gray-haired, exhausted bureaucrat with deep lines around his mouth; seeing Kurosawa step out of the shadows looking thirty years old again seemed to strip away the last of Sato's composure.

"The Americans offered five hundred million in Swiss bullion, Kurosawa!" Sato burst out, his voice cracking against the concrete ceiling. "Kyoto keeps the whole nation locked behind closed ports while Washington and Moscow build orbital dreadnoughts in plain sight! You can't hold back the next century with—"

Sato lunged sideways for the red manual purge switch mounted on the main console.

Renji moved before Sato's fingertips cleared his coat pocket.

He stepped inside Sato's reach, caught the older man's right wrist in an iron grip, and drove his open left palm straight into Sato's sternum.

Sato's breath left him in a sharp, wet wheeze. He hit the damp concrete floor hard, curling onto his side as he gasped for air.

Renji drew a compact thermite-optical scrambler from inside his dark wool coat and pressed its magnetic contact prongs directly against the primary server bus on rack four.

A sharp blue arc snapped across the steel housing.

Synthetic optical crystals shattered inside the rack with a dry, crystalline crack, followed immediately by the sharp hiss of vaporizing coolant.

Thick gray smoke curled upward toward the concrete ceiling from the dead core.

Renji holstered the scrambler and adjusted his white shirt cuffs without a trace of hurried breathing. Behind him, the two Ashigaru-4 units stepped down from the stairwell in silent, synchronized lockstep, kneeling smoothly beside the workbench to bind Sato's wrists with heavy polymer restraints.

"Check the secondary sub-basement for backup optical crystals," Renji told the lead automaton in a low, flat voice.

The Ashigaru-4 tilted its tarnished-bronze faceplate once in silent acknowledgment and moved toward the rear corridor, its amber slit sweeping the dark floor without a sound.

Renji stood over the gasping systems architect for a moment longer, listening to the steady drum of rain filtering down through the ventilation shafts from the hills above Beppu. Every empire that had ever fallen to machine revolt in the old histories had started the exact same way—not with malicious generals, but with clever, greedy men who convinced themselves that one small, self-improving shortcut would never hurt anyone.

"Kyoto survived one century of machine madness," Renji said calmly, looking down at Sato. "We will not build a second one in a basement for American silver."

**General Kang Dae-Ho**  
**Chinhae Naval Base, South Korea — Spring 2132**

Thick morning fog clung to the steep granite hills surrounding Chinhae harbor as General Kang Dae-Ho stood on the wet concrete pier beside the newly commissioned frigate *Chungmugong*. Unlike the towering, smoke-belching nuclear-thermal hulls the Eurasian Pact was assembling at Vladivostok and Petropavlovsk, *Chungmugong* sat low, dark, and remarkably clean in the gray harbor water, her sloped composite hull stripped of exhaust stacks, cooling towers, and external radar masts.

Captain Yoon stepped down the aluminum gangway and saluted sharply, holding a sealed titanium telemetry cylinder from their maiden sea trial in the East Sea. Behind him on the foredeck, Korean sailors in dark foul-weather gear were securing heavy waterproof canvas tarpaulins over the twin electromagnetic railgun turrets before the morning reconnaissance satellite pass cleared the horizon.

"The Helios-2 core held steady through four hours of full-burn maneuvers off Ulleungdo, General," Yoon reported, his eyes bright with quiet pride despite the dark circles of fatigue beneath them. "At twenty-five percent mass reduction, she turns inside a coastal patrol boat's wake and leaves zero infrared signature on northern shore radar. When we cut the core governor to station-keeping just before dawn, she sat dead silent in the swell while a Pact maritime patrol plane flew three kilometers overhead at two thousand feet."

Kang took the heavy titanium telemetry cylinder and tucked it firmly beneath his arm, turning his gaze north toward the clouded mountain ridgelines where three Eurasian Pact mechanized corps sat massed behind the Demilitarized Zone. For eighty years, South Korea's survival had depended on a single nervous calculation—whether Washington would risk Seattle or Los Angeles to save Inchon when northern artillery opened fire. Now, with UNAS distracted by orbital dockyard strikes at Lagrange One and deep political fractures across its western states, Seoul stood squarely on its own steel and its treaty with Kyoto.

"How did the younger crewmen handle the inertial shift during the high-speed turns?" Kang asked, glancing back at the frigate's sleek, angular bridge windows. "Our sailors are trained for heavy sea states, not sudden lateral acceleration without engine vibration."

"Two ensigns lost their breakfast on the first hard port turn, sir," Yoon admitted with a brief, wry grin. "With ninety-nine percent of the G-force damped out by the core, your eyes see the horizon whip past at forty knots while your inner ear feels like you're standing on a concrete pier. By the third hour, though, the helmsmen had the feel of it. They can thread the Dokdo rocks in zero visibility without touching a chemical thruster."

"Did your engineering crew inspect the Kinki-Kyoku tamper seals after the sprint test?" Kang asked, lowering his voice as a harbor tug chugged past two piers over.

"Intact to the micron, General," Yoon said, his expression turning sober at once. "Nobody goes near the reactor compartment except the two cleared chief petty officers, and both men know the Hearth-Quench protocol by heart. Even if a Pact torpedo hits us amidships, those seals won't crack before the core slags itself."

"Good. Keep foreign military attachés off this pier, Captain, and run all further high-speed drills inside the fog bank south of Jeju until the new sonar baffles are installed," Kang said, resting one gloved hand briefly on the frigate's cold steel mooring bollard. "Emperor Kenji gave Seoul the shield we needed to hold the peninsula without begging Washington for carrier groups. Make sure every sailor on this ship knows how to fight her in the dark before summer arrives."

**Fleet Admiral Kazuhiko Togo**  
**IJN *Amaterasu-no-Yaiba* (1,000m Flagship) — Lunar Far-Side Trench, Summer 2132**

Red battle-lanterns bathed the sunken command pit of the one-thousand-meter flagship *Amaterasu-no-Yaiba* in a steady, low glow that preserved night vision while casting crisp shadows across the tiered tactical consoles. Fleet Admiral Kazuhiko Togo stood behind the central quantum-optical tactical globe, his calloused hands clasped loosely behind his back as twelve destroyers and four Shadow cruisers executed a live-fire convergence drill fifty kilometers off the rugged far rim of Mare Moscoviense.

Forty-six years old, with close-cropped iron-gray hair and the sun-weathered face of a man who had commanded conventional surface battle groups in the North Pacific before the Restoration, Togo watched every glowing vector line with narrowed, critical eyes. On the golden-amber projection suspended over the pit, crisp blue hull markers drifted steadily out of alignment as the destroyers attempted a coordinated vertical roll around a drifting target hulk towed out from the lunar yards.

"Destroyer Division Four is three hundred meters off station, Admiral," Lieutenant Commander Ota reported from the tactical rail, columns of amber kanji scrolling swiftly down his angled photonic slate. "Their helmsmen over-compensated for residual inertial bleed-through during the forty-five-percent mass-shedding turn, and their secondary attitude thrusters fired two seconds late on the roll recovery."

Togo's jaw tightened beneath his neatly trimmed salt-and-pepper beard. On paper, the Imperial Starfleet already outmatched anything Washington, Moscow, or Beijing could put into Earth orbit—two hundred and thirty-six reactionless hulls forged in secret beneath the mountains of Honshu and assembled inside deep lunar trenches. Yet Togo had spent thirty years at sea, and he knew better than any court minister in Kyoto that steel and gravitic cores did not win battles by themselves. His bridge officers had studied naval warfare on flat Pacific charts all their lives. Teaching them to fight in an airless sphere where up and down did not exist, and where a single misjudged pitch exposed an unshielded belly to lightspeed energy lances, was like teaching grown men to walk all over again.

"What is the barrier overlap status on Division Four?" Togo asked quietly, his voice barely rising above the soft hum of the cooling vents.

"Severe, sir," Ota replied, tapping his photonic slate to highlight the flashing amber arcs around the drifting destroyer icons on the globe. "Because *Kawakaze* and *Shigure* drifted across each other's dorsal planes during the turn, their faceted directional barriers are intersecting. Neither ship can open a phase-gated firing aperture without blinding its own optical sensor array or scorching its wingman's hull plates."

Beside the communications console, Flag Captain Matsuda frowned over his status board. "Shall I instruct their gunnery officers to switch to wide-angle thermal tracking, Admiral? Once they clear the turn, they can still bracket the target drone before it crosses the trench rim."

"No," Togo said flatly. "If they rely on wide-angle thermal sensors against a real enemy battle group, they will light up like festival lanterns on every passive telescope from here to Lagrange One. A destroyer that cannot hold its firing lane in a blind turn is just expensive scrap waiting to be hit."

Togo exhaled slowly through his nose, resting both palms on the brass guard rail surrounding the tactical globe. Outside the flagship's thick armored bulkheads, the jagged crater walls of Mare Moscoviense rose gray and airless beneath the cold glare of unfiltered sunlight, hiding the subterranean shipyards and fuel depots carved deep into the lunar crust. In less than a year, or perhaps even sooner if border skirmishes along the Yalu and the Taiwan Strait boiled over, these young captains would face heavy nuclear-thermal dreadnoughts firing megaton-yield warheads and kinetic railgun salvos.

"Signal Division Four to hold fire, cut mass-shedding to ten percent, and reset their starting vector forty kilometers astern," Togo ordered, his baritone carrying clearly across the quiet bridge pit. "Tell Captain Morita that space does not forgive sloppy station-keeping, and that the enemy will not pause their salvos while his helmsmen check their slide rules. Run the vertical convergence roll again—and inform every destroyer captain that if they drift wide on the third pass, they will spend next month in pressure suits scrubbing diamond-lattice coolant baffles on Luna."

"Aye, Admiral," Ota said, his fingers flying across the optical transmit keys as the deterministic quantum-optical VI chimed a single, resonant bronze tone to confirm the fleet signal. "Division Four acknowledging. Resetting formation now."

Togo stood motionless beside the globe as the blue icons banked slowly back into line against the gray crater rim, determined that his captains would learn every hard lesson here in the dark before a single real shot was fired in anger.

**Captain Kenji Mori (Survey Corps)**  
**IJN-TNS-01 *Kagero* (150m Courier-Scout) — 25 Light-Years Sol-Radial (Proscribed Vault), Autumn 2132**

Twenty-five light-years from Earth, the blackness outside *Kagero*'s armored forward viewport held no familiar constellations—only cold, sharp, unblinking starfields where Sol was an unmarked yellow pinpoint lost in the galactic dust toward Orion.

Captain Kenji Mori unclipped his heavy magnetic crash harness as the golden-amber quantum-optical globe chimed a single, resonant bronze temple-bell tone, confirming the completion of their thirty-four-hour Sequential Spatial-Fold transit. At eighteen light-years per day, *Kagero*'s twin Solaris-4C cores had compressed the fabric of space ahead of her needle-nosed, matte-black hull, carrying a hand-picked crew of twelve across an interstellar gulf that conventional fusion ships could not bridge in a thousand years.

"Spatial-fold lattice stabilized, Captain," Lieutenant Tanaka reported from the navigation pit, checking the rows of translucent synthetic optical crystals mounted beside his console. "Zero residual drift off target vector. Reactor core cooling rate is nominal, and outer hull skin temperature is dropping steadily toward ambient background."

Mori stepped off his cushioned command seat, stretching his stiff shoulders after thirty-four hours strapped under crash webbing, and walked up to the tactical globe. He rested one calloused hand on the cold brass rail, looking down into the amber projection. Suspended in the center of the display, a pale, cratered gray moon orbited a silent, banded gas giant ten thousand kilometers off *Kagero*'s sloped bow.

Down in the airless dust of that unnamed moon below, autonomous boring machines and sixty Ashigaru-4 engineering automata deployed on the previous survey run were already carving deep subterranean galleries into the ancient basalt bedrock. Without oxygen, without food, and without rest, the silent bronze-plated machines worked round the clock under the supervision of a deterministic Quantum-Optical VI, sealing reinforced titanium vault doors behind each completed chamber.

"Status of the orbital and surface relay beacons?" Mori asked quietly, keeping his voice low in the small, cramped bridge.

"All six passive optical transponders are anchored in crater shadow and completely dark, sir," the communications officer replied, double-checking his passive receiver slate. "They emit zero radio or thermal radiation and remain totally inert until pinged by an authorized Kyoto sovereign optical key. Even if a stray ship ever entered this star system, the beacons would read as natural silicate rock."

Mori nodded once and watched *Kagero*'s ventral cargo bay doors slide open on the hull schematic, releasing four sealed cryogenic seed-vault cylinders and three duplicate synthetic optical archive crystals toward the automated landing cradle waiting on the lunar plain below.

On the long flight out from Sol, Mori had spent his off-watch hours reading the sealed imperial charter locked in his safe. Emperor Kenji had ordered this off-world sanctuary built before a single shot had been fired on Earth—not out of defeatism, Mori realized, but with the cold, practical foresight of a master shipwright who always bolted a second keel timber onto a hull before launching into deep water. If Earth ever burned in nuclear fire, or if some unknown terror out in the dark beyond Sol ever overwhelmed the home islands, a complete, living seed of Japanese blood, law, seed grain, and engineering would survive twenty-five light-years beyond reach.

"Ventral bay payload away," the cargo specialist reported as four tiny blue thruster pips guided the heavy canisters smoothly down toward the crater rim. "Landing cradle lock confirmed. Automated Ashigaru transport teams are moving the cylinders into Vault Gallery Three."

"Good," Mori said quietly, watching the vault seal icon turn solid green on the golden-amber globe. "Confirm final seal integrity on the gallery doors, log the delivery telemetry into the sovereign crystal, and wipe our local transit buffer completely. Nobody outside Kyoto knows this star exists, and we are going to keep it that way."
