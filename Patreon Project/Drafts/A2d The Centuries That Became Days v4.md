# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Maya Brooks (25)*

### *Marseille, 2063*

Three forms lay side by side on her desk, and each had one blank line. Maya Brooks had read them four times. They came from three ministries in three countries, and each cleared the sale of one hull. The empty line on all three read *Owner of record*.

Three stamps, three signatures, three dates. Each form had been filed on a Monday and stamped nine days later, to the day. The stamps were real. She had checked each one against its ministry's own seal.

Her office was a rented room above a ship chandler, with one window and a door that locked from the inside. She had asked for it because it had two exits. It smelled of tar and rope. Below her, a man argued the price of a coil of cable, and she heard every word.

Her small task that morning had been to match the forms to the three hulls before Kenji's call at noon. She had done it. What she had not done was stop looking at the blanks. Her thumb walked across her fingertips, one, two, three, four, and began again.

Cerberus had beaten the network with paper three times: the 2058 audit, the 2059 van, the 2061 state letter. Each time the weakness had been a box no one thought to check. She did not like an empty box that nobody had explained.

The phone rang at noon.

"What do you need?" Kenji said. He always said it first.

"For the record, three forms, nine days each, and one empty box on every one. Why is the owner line empty?"

"I told Marchetti to leave it empty." He spoke slowly. "If a ministry stamps a form with a name on it, someone can follow that name. If the line is empty, there is nothing to follow. The designs belong to the foundation, but I don't want its name on a form a ministry will file."

"That is the reason for the box. It is not the reason for the dates. The ministries did not ask who the owner was, and they should have."

"No. I thought a blank line would make a clerk ask. Three of them did not."

"Three countries, three clerks, nine days to the day." She turned the second form over, as if the back might say something else. "A stamp that lands the same number of days late three times is a process, not chance. I can see the pattern from three forms. Anyone else reading our paper can see it too."

He said nothing for a while. She waited. She was good at waiting for the second half of an answer, and she counted four seconds of it.

"Then they will see it," he said. "I don't have a way to stop that. What do you need?"

"Three forms that don't match," she said. "Different days, different clerks. I'll have a list by tonight."

When the call ended she took her notebook from the drawer and wrote one line. *Three forms. Nine days each. We left a blank, and we left a pattern.* She looked at the second sentence for a long time.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (33)*

### *A Gulf coast yard, 2064*

The checklist for the day's test ran to two pages in her own hand. Hannah went down it with a pencil, the way she had every morning for two years. Fences. Gates. The roof, where the sentries stood. The line of human guards between the hull and the road. She marked each item as she touched it.

The hull on the gantry had no maker's plate. She laid her palm on the bare place where one would go.

An engineer stood at the panel with his arms folded. The readout in front of him was a column of symbols she could not read.

"Walk me through that."

"I'm not the person to ask."

"Who is?"

"I haven't met him." He looked at the symbols as if they might answer. "They told us to watch for red. It hasn't gone red."

She wrote it down. No plate, no technician, no readout.

She had seen the same three gaps once before. Two years ago her firm had guarded its first job, a single unmanned frame on a gantry, and she had written them down then. When that frame lifted, the hum had dropped half a tone. Nobody could find the technician who knew why.

She wrote it again at the top of her note. *Hum drops half a tone at lift. Nobody has been able to explain why.*

She also had a standing instruction. If any man at the fence said the name Cerberus, she was to hear it first. In four years nobody had.

The sun was up. The guards stood where she had put them, a pace apart. She walked the line with her father's tape measure in her pocket, and at the far end of the yard she stopped and looked at the hull.

"Clear," her deputy said on the radio.

The hull began to hum. It rose off the gantry with no sound but that, a steady note she could feel in her teeth. At midpoint the note fell half a tone and stuttered.

The gantry shrieked.

A clamp sheared. A bolt whipped across the yard.

Hannah ran.

A guard stood in its path, one hand still on his radio. She hit him at the knees. They went down together.

The bolt struck the wall behind them.

She did not move. Her hand was on the guard's collar, and the tape measure was digging into her hip.

Above them, the hum came back. It rose. The note steadied.

The hull went up through the light and was gone.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Emil Varga*

### *The Lisbon docks, 2065*

The price of a day's work had been chalked on the board outside the harbor office, wiped off, and chalked again twice that week. Emil Varga read it from the gate, and chalk dust came off on his sleeve when he leaned on the board. The new price was lower than it had been in the spring. He had a crew of twelve who wanted paying, and the agent's office closed at five.

He needed the agent's signature on the crew's chits before then. A crowd stood on the quay between the gate and the office, three deep, and the police had run a rope across the middle of it.

"What's going on?" he asked a man in a dock apron.

"Mind your feet, they're moving the rope." The man did not look round. "A hull goes up from the east berth. They say it flies to the other side of the Solar System and back before dark."

"Nobody sails to the other side of the Solar System."

"This one does."

A policeman waved Emil back behind the line. He went. He was a foreman, and he had watched machines replace men on that quay for five years. First the cranes, then the arms, then the robots that tied down loads without a crew. He knew what each one cost, and he knew how many men it had taken. He had never had a reason to look up at one.

His daughter held the end of his sleeve. She was six. She had asked at breakfast whether she could come, and he had told her she could stay at the gate while he worked. She had not stayed at the gate.

"Papa, I can't see."

He looked at the crowd, and then at the agent's window. The blind was down. The agent would not sign anything until the hull had gone, and everyone on the quay knew it.

He picked her up and sat her on his shoulders. She weighed more than she had at Easter. He felt it in his back, and he had stopped being surprised.

A note began under the quay. It was a hum, low and steady, and it came up through his boots. The crowd went quiet. A gray hull lifted from the east berth with nothing under it and no flame, and rose straight up.

His daughter's hands were in his hair. "Where is it going?"

"I don't know."

"Will it come back?"

"They say before dark."

The hull was a speck, and then it was gone. Nobody cheered. People stood with their faces up and waited for the sky to say something. His own throat had gone tight.

"Papa," his daughter said. "I want to go."

"Where?"

"Wherever that was."

He laughed, once, and it hurt. He had not wanted to go anywhere in years. He held her ankles and stayed where he was, with the crew's chits folded in his pocket and the sun in his eyes, until the rope came down.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Colm Farrady*

### *Houston, 2065*

A clerk forgets a line, or a lawyer wants one hidden, and Farrady had seen both in nineteen years. Neither explained a form where every other box, down to the postal code, was filled in the same careful hand and this one was left clean.

The application on his desk was for a loan of eleven hulls. The borrower was a shipping line, and it was ordinary. The seller was eleven months old. It had no history and no offices he could find, yet the drive in its hulls had flown for two years.

His want was simple. The credit committee closed the quarter on Friday, and he needed the loan signed before it did. The loan officer from the bank down the street had already telephoned twice to ask whether Farrady was still thinking about it.

He read what each party owned. The borrower owned its docks, its lease, and its debt. The hulls would own the line's future. He turned to the seller's sheet.

*Owner of record.* The line stayed empty.

"Get me the registry," he said to the girl at the next desk.

"It's a public file, Mr. Farrady. It will be forty pages of nothing."

"Then I'll read forty pages."

She got it on the second try. He went through it line by line. The seller's incorporation was eleven months old. Its shares were held by a trust, and the trust by a foundation. The foundation's name stood on the page at last, in plain type.

*Janus Foundation.*

He had never heard of it. He searched his bank's own records and found nothing. He searched the registry for the foundation and found it on a second page. It had been filed in December of 2051.

Fourteen years ago. Two years before the first hull flew, and long before anyone knew there would be a drive to sell. Somebody had built a foundation to own the machine before the machine existed.

He looked for an address and found a filing agent's desk. He looked for a trustee and found one listed only by a law firm's mailing box. He looked for an owner, and the line stayed blank.

He wrote the foundation's name in the empty box on the loan form. Then he crossed it out. It was not his line to fill.

The telephone rang. It was the other bank again. He let it ring.

He had a name now. It led nowhere, and it was older than the machine.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (35)*

### *A Gulf coast yard, 2066*

The *Halyard*'s registration card was a small plastic rectangle with a stamp in one corner. Hannah held it up to the light on the gangway and looked at the empty line. *Owner of record.* There was nothing on it, and there had been nothing on any card she had seen in four years.

Her firm had guarded eleven departures from this yard. Tonight's was the first with a paying cargo, and the first she would follow out. She had two cutters on the water with a crew of eight on each, and a clear sky. On the pad the hull stood on its gantry with the lamps burning white. Outside the fence, a man had set up a coffee cart.

Her pre-flight note was on a clipboard. She had written it at four that morning, and she read it again. *Hum drops half a tone at lift. No technician. No plate.* She had written the same three lines on every note for four years, and she had stopped looking for someone to ask.

"Fences clear," her deputy said on the radio. "Roof clear."

"Walk me through the cargo."

"Sealed containers, and a manifest with every box filled in. The weight is on it, in the signer's hand." He paused. "The owner line is blank."

"It is on all of them."

"Yes, ma'am. I only wanted you to hear me say it."

She went down the gangway and stood at the foot of the gantry. The crew had cleared the pad. Beyond the fence a few dozen people had gathered with their faces turned up, holding paper cups from the cart. Nobody had asked them to come. They were there because the ships had stopped being a rumor.

The hum started.

It rose off the gantry on the note she knew. At the half-tone drop it fell and recovered, and this time nothing sheared. The hull climbed. She watched it through the glass of the cutter's bridge, then through the radar, then through nothing.

"Hold the follow," she said.

"Holding. We have it at ten thousand meters."

"Twenty."

"Twenty. Thirty."

The radar showed a single bright dot, climbing steadily. Her hand was on the rail.

The dot disappeared.

It did not fade. It did not drift. It was on the screen, and then it was not.

The cutter lurched. The deck came up under her feet. A wall of air struck the bridge, and every window rang. She was thrown against the rail. Someone shouted.

She got up. The screen was blank. The sky was empty.

"Find it," she said.

"There's nothing to find."

"Find it."

Her deputy looked at her. He did not say what both of them knew. She held the registration card in her left fist, and the empty line pressed into her palm. Her right thumb pressed along the scar on that forearm.
