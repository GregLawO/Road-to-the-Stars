Recap: Batch 1 (Autumn 2132)

On the morning of October 14, 2132, the Eurasian Pact and the United North American States launched coordinated surprise assaults across the Taiwan Strait, the Korean Demilitarized Zone, and high Earth orbit. Guided by Emperor Kenji Himura's calm directive from Kyoto to keep operations strictly within Solaris-4 parameters without civilian casualties, Taiwanese Helios-2 cutters crippled the Pact amphibious vanguard off Penghu while General Kang Dae-Ho's mobile railgun grids shattered three mechanized shock divisions along the Imjin River. Above Honshu, Captain Shinji Takeda's Shadow Squadron dropped Eclipse stealth to vaporize forty-eight UNAS kinetic bombardment sleds, before Fleet Admiral Kazuhiko Togo led First Battle Squadron in a forty-G vertical dive to disarm three Pact battlecruisers over the East China Sea and broadcast an ultimatum demanding full withdrawal within six hours.

A2.6

**Ambassador Arthur Pendelton**  
**U.S.S. *Abraham Lincoln* (Orbital Diplomatic Station, Lagrange One) — Autumn 2132**

Fluorescent panels buzzed faintly overhead in the zero-gravity conference module of Orbital Station *Abraham Lincoln*, where Ambassador Arthur Pendelton drifted beside the bolted aluminum table with one hand hooked loosely through a canvas thigh strap. Across the narrow, white-painted compartment, Deputy Defense Secretary Harlan Miller stared grimly at a laminated printout of high-resolution orbital radar tracks transmitted up from Cheyenne Mountain four hours earlier, his jaw clenched tight beneath his graying beard.

Out beyond the thick circular quartz viewport behind Miller's shoulder, two crippled UNAS nuclear-thermal cruisers—*Valley Forge* and *Gettysburg*—were slowly being towed into the Lagrange One repair slips by chemical maneuvering tugs, their massive magnetic fusion nozzles sheared clean away into smooth, glassy craters where invisible Japanese energy lances had struck them. Along the flank of *Valley Forge*, scorched armor plating showed where her secondary railgun turrets had been precision-melted off their barbettes without breaching a single pressurized crew compartment.

"Sixty seconds, Arthur," Miller muttered, tapping a thick forefinger hard against the timestamp printed across the bottom margin of the telemetry sheet. "Forty-eight tungsten kinetic bombardment sleds gone without a single shrapnel fragment left in low Earth orbit, four heavy cruisers disabled, and not one of our optical or S-band deep-space arrays caught an approach track until those black hulls were already inside eight hundred kilometers. Langley spent ten full years telling the White House that Kyoto was hoarding rice, rationing structural steel, and building coastal patrol boats."

Pendelton adjusted his wire-rimmed glasses and took a slow, unhurried sip from a sealed plastic coffee bulb. Sixty-four years old, with three decades spent negotiating trade tariffs and maritime treaties in Geneva, Brussels, and Beijing before his posting to the UNAS Space Directorate, Pendelton had learned long ago to separate bruised military pride from hard physical reality.

"Langley read commercial shipping manifests instead of watching the power grids, Harlan," Pendelton said evenly, drifting slightly as he turned a fresh page in his diplomatic folder. "More importantly, look at what the Japanese fleet did *not* do when they had total control of the sky. They had clear firing angles on every orbital shipyard we operate at Lagrange One, and they had forty-eight hours of total orbital supremacy over North America before we could even fuel a second cruiser squadron out of Cape Canaveral. They didn't drop a single tungsten rod on Seattle or Norfolk, they didn't touch a single one of our civilian communications or weather satellites, and they didn't even mistreat the prisoners they pulled off the Eurasian Pact drop transports over the East China Sea."

Miller frowned, crossing his arms tightly over his dark navy tunic as he pushed back against his tether strap. "You think Emperor Kenji is looking for a diplomatic off-ramp before Washington mobilizes its reserve shipyards?"

"I think Emperor Kenji just proved he owns the high ground from Earth to the far side of the Moon, and he has zero interest in governing smoking ruins," Pendelton replied, pulling an encrypted diplomatic tablet from his leather briefcase and sliding it across the magnetic tabletop. "An hour ago, the Imperial Foreign Ministry in Kyoto transmitted a three-page armistice draft simultaneously to Washington, Moscow, and Beijing through the Swiss legation. Read the articles for yourself. No territorial demands on the mainland, no punitive war indemnities, and completely open commercial transit through the Western Pacific sea lanes—provided all foreign warships stay outside a three-thousand-kilometer orbital exclusion zone around Japan, Taiwan, and South Korea."

Miller stared at the tablet screen for a long, silent moment, scrolling slowly through the plain, unornamented clauses. There were no sweeping ideological demands, no humiliating tribute clauses, and no calls for regime change—just hard, clean geographic lines backed by warships that obeyed a completely different branch of physics.

"The Joint Chiefs are screaming in the Situation Room for a retaliatory salvo from our lunar mass-drivers at Shackleton Crater," Miller said quietly.

"Tell the Joint Chiefs to ask General Williams at NORAD how long a ballistic rock or a chemical-boosted missile lasts against a ship that pulls forty gravities without a thermal plume," Pendelton said dryly, tucking his empty coffee bulb into the wall disposal slot. "We didn't lose a century-long war today, Harlan—we just discovered we were playing the wrong game with wooden pieces. Sign the armistice in Kyoto before Moscow and Beijing beat us to the table and cut their own bilateral deal behind our backs."

A2.7

**Director General Shen Ming-Hao**  
**Eurasian Pact Joint General Staff Bunker, Beijing — Autumn 2132**

Acrid smoke from half a dozen stubbed-out cigarettes hung thick beneath the reinforced concrete vault ceiling of the Beijing Joint General Staff bunker as Director General Shen Ming-Hao stood beside the illuminated East Asian strategic map. Along the Fujian coastline and across the frozen mud of the Yalu River, dozens of red unit icons pulsed with amber casualty warnings—the battered remnants of two amphibious assault groups and three mechanized shock corps that had spent thirty-six hours bleeding steel against coastal electromagnetic railguns and silent, low-slung gravitic cutters.

Marshal Volkov, senior liaison from the Moscow Strategic Rocket Forces, slammed a thick red folder of satellite damage assessments onto the steel map table, his face mottled dark red beneath his silver crew cut.

"Three *Kirov-class* orbital battlecruisers reduced to drifting, unpowered hulks above the East China Sea!" Volkov barked in heavily accented Mandarin, jabbing one thick finger at the orbital track plot. "Twelve thousand airborne shock infantry stranded in surrender pods on Kyushu! And when our strategic command at Baikonur prepared to elevate long-range ballistic missiles to launch readiness at zero-six-hundred, two unidentified black cruisers materialized inside geostationary orbit directly above our silo fields—with zero radar warning from our northern early-warning domes!"

Shen did not raise his voice or flinch at the slap of the folder against steel. At fifty-nine, having survived three politburo purges and two border crises by keeping his temper locked behind cold arithmetic, he looked calmly at the orbital telemetry prints spread beside Volkov's folder.

"They did not fire on Baikonur, Marshal," Shen pointed out quietly, resting his fingertips lightly on the edge of the plotting table. "Nor did they strike our rail hubs at Shenyang, our assembly plants at Harbin, or our naval shipyards at Dalian. Every single round fired by Kyoto, Taipei, and Seoul over the past two days fell exclusively on forces that crossed their sovereign borders or entered their orbital airspace."

Volkov exhaled a harsh, ragged breath and dropped heavily into a steel folding chair, rubbing both calloused hands over his face. "Our physicists at Akademgorodok have spent all night analyzing the spectral flash from *Amaterasu-no-Yaiba*'s weapons. Those were not chemical lasers, nuclear-pumped X-ray emitters, or conventional particle beams—they were coherent phase-gated energy lances operating at power densities we cannot replicate with a gigawatt fission reactor. Kyoto has cracked reactionless gravitic propulsion, and they have been sitting on it in complete secrecy for a decade."

"While we poured three trillion rubles and yuan into heavy nuclear-thermal hulls that burn like festival torches on every passive telescope," Shen added without bitterness.

Shen picked up the single sheet of crisp rice-paper stationery transmitted via the Swiss embassy two hours earlier—the formal text of the Armistice of Kyoto. Unlike the bloated, hundred-page treaties drafted by Geneva lawyers, it was barely eight hundred words long, free of ideological rhetoric, and backed by two hundred and thirty-six reactionless warships sitting silently in high orbit.

"What does Moscow advise?" Volkov asked, looking up from his hands with tired, bloodshot eyes.

"Moscow has already lost its entire Pacific orbital squadron, Marshal, and Beijing's amphibious fleet is sitting on the bottom of the Penghu Channel," Shen said, uncapping his fountain pen with deliberate precision. "If we refuse to sign before midnight, we lose every orbital shipyard and lunar mining outpost we have built since 2080. If we sign, our armies withdraw cleanly behind the pre-war borders, our twelve thousand prisoners return home unharmed within thirty days, and we buy the peace we need to reorganize our own science directorates. Inform the Premier and the Kremlin—our delegation flies to Kyoto at dawn."

A2.8

**Prime Minister Lin Chia-Hao**  
**Kyoto-Heian Imperial Citadel, Kyoto — Winter 2132**

Crisp winter air drifted across the raked white gravel of the dry stone garden outside the Great Hall of State in Kyoto as Prime Minister Lin Chia-Hao of Taiwan stood beside Prime Minister Han Seung-Soo of South Korea, watching the snow-dusted pine branches sway gently beneath a pale December sun. Inside the vast cedar-beamed chamber behind them, diplomatic clerks from Washington, Moscow, and Beijing had just finished affixing their wax and ink seals to the final parchment copies of the Armistice of Kyoto, formally recognizing the sovereign neutrality and three-thousand-kilometer orbital defense perimeter of the Tripartite Pacific Accord.

Five minutes later, the heavy sliding cedar doors opened on silent rollers and Emperor Kenji Himura stepped out onto the covered wooden veranda. He had already shed the heavy gold-embroidered ceremonial court robes worn for the formal signing inside, wearing instead a plain dark wool jacket over a high-collared tunic, his calloused hands tucked comfortably into his pockets as he walked up to join the two allied leaders.

"That looked genuinely painful for Ambassador Pendelton," Kenji remarked with a dry, easy smile, leaning one shoulder lightly against a thick cedar pillar. "I thought his fountain pen was going to snap in half when he signed the orbital exclusion clause."

Prime Minister Han let out a quiet, genuine laugh, relaxing his stiff diplomatic posture for the first time all morning. "Marshal Volkov looked even worse, Your Majesty. Seeing two Ashigaru-4 automata standing silent guard behind the treaty table seemed to take ten years off his life—he kept glancing over his shoulder like he expected them to march him straight out to the courtyard."

"Good," Kenji said, his tone turning practical and direct while keeping the easy warmth in his voice. "Let them remember what's standing behind the door so none of us have to repeat October's lesson anytime soon. Which brings me to why I asked the two of you to stay behind after the foreign delegations cleared out to their hotels."

Lin straightened slightly, his dark eyes attentive beneath his silver-streaked brows. "Taiwan's shipyards, railgun batteries, and patrol flotillas stand ready to honor every article of our mutual defense treaty, Your Majesty."

"I know you do—your coastal cutters proved that in the Strait when Beijing threw two amphibious groups at you," Kenji said, resting both hands comfortably on the wooden veranda rail. "October showed that twenty-five percent Helios-2 cores are enough to hold a coastline against conventional hulls. Now that the whole world knows gravitic ships exist, though, Moscow, Beijing, and Washington are going to throw every ruble, yuan, and dollar they have into reverse-engineering reactionless drives. That means a twenty-five percent cap won't be enough by 2135."

Kenji turned to face both prime ministers directly, his gaze steady, open, and completely unpretentious. "Starting next month, Kinki-Kyoku is shipping upgraded Helios-5 industrial and naval cores to Kaohsiung and Busan—thirty-five percent mass-shedding cap, upgraded inertial dampeners, and enough continuous output to power heavy orbital defense platforms and deep-water cruisers. Same rules as before: factory tamper seals stay welded, Hearth-Quench stays active, and zero foreign inspectors ever cross your dockyard gates. In exchange, Taipei and Seoul stay completely independent sovereign nations—no vassal treaties, no foreign bases on your soil, just three neighbors keeping our own house clean."

Lin bowed deeply, genuine emotion tightening his throat as he thought of the generations Taiwan had spent living under the constant shadow of blockade and invasion. "Taipei accepts without hesitation, Your Majesty. Every shipyard in Kaohsiung will treat those seals like sacred relics."

"Seoul stands with Kyoto and Taipei," Prime Minister Han added firmly, extending his hand across the rail. "Our engineers are already clearing the drydocks at Chinhae for the new hulls."

Kenji shook both men's hands with a firm, unhurried grip. "All right. Enough state business for one afternoon—come inside before the palace kitchen lets the winter hot pot go cold."

A2.9

**Lady Shizuka Himura**  
**Kii-Yamato Subterranean Foundry Complex, Wakayama — Winter 2132**

Four hundred meters beneath the snow-dusted cedar ridges of the Kii Peninsula, the massive main assembly cavern of Foundry Gallery Four thrummed with the deep, steady vibration of magnetic induction smelters and automated gantry cranes. Lady Shizuka Himura stood on the steel inspection catwalk overlooking the drydock floor, wearing her heavy dark canvas work apron over a warm wool kimono, her stubby wooden pencil tucked securely behind her right ear and a thick clipboard resting against her forearm.

Below her on the illuminated slipway floor, three hundred human master welders worked side by side with sixty silent Ashigaru-4 engineering automata, fitting curved neutronium-carbide hull plates onto the keel frame of a next-generation Solaris-7 heavy destroyer. Unlike the frantic, chaotic mobilization now gripping shipyards in Seattle, Baikonur, and Dalian, the Kii-Yamato floor moved with practiced, unhurried rhythm—every crane swing timed to the second, every alloy ingot stamped with its Kinki-Kyoku batch code.

Foreman Sato climbed the iron stairs to the catwalk, wiping sweat and soot from his forehead with a clean cotton rag. Four months after completing Stage Four Chōju rejuvenation in Kyoto, the sixty-eight-year-old master shipwright moved with the spring and stamina of a man in his late twenties, though he still kept his silver hair cropped short and refused to wear anything fancier than grease-stained coveralls.

"The first twelve Helios-5 export cores passed their full-load bench tests at dawn, Lady Shizuka," Sato reported, handing her a stamped inspection ledger. "Factory output governors are welded dead at thirty-five percent apparent mass reduction, and the dual-stage Hearth-Quench slagging charges are seated inside the neutronium-carbide jackets. Even if an American or Russian team captures one intact in deep water, the core turns into a five-ton lump of fused silicate glass the instant anyone touches a plasma torch to the casing."

Shizuka flipped open the ledger, running a scarred thumb down the thermal tolerance columns while pushing her wire-rimmed reading glasses up her nose. "Good. Make sure the crating crews at Sasebo double-check the inertial shock mounts before loading them onto the Kaohsiung and Busan freighters tomorrow night. The winter swells in the East China Sea are rough this week, and I don't want a single damper bracket bent before those hulls leave harbor."

"Already seen to, my lady," Sato said, leaning one forearm against the catwalk rail as he looked down at the glowing hull plates of the new Solaris-7 destroyer below. "What about our own line? The new optical crystal lattices from the Nara plant arrived an hour ago—they handle forty percent higher gravimetric flux without overheating the diamond sinks."

"Then put the night shift onto seating the Solaris-7 core mounts in Slipway Two," Shizuka said briskly, making a sharp check mark in her ledger with her wooden pencil. "October taught the foreigners what our pre-war hulls can do at forty-five percent mass-shedding. By the time Moscow and Washington finish reverse-engineering their first clumsy prototypes next year, I want our fleet running at sixty-five percent with full tactical fold capability. Now go get your shift down to the canteen for hot noodles—nobody welds straight on an empty stomach."

A2.10

**Emperor Kenji Himura**  
**Amaterasu Palace Private Wing, Kyoto — Winter 2132**

Snow fell in large, silent flakes outside the open wooden shutters of the private garden gallery in Amaterasu Palace, settling softly onto the stone lanterns and the dark green needles of the courtyard pines. Inside the warm, cedar-scented room, Emperor Kenji Himura sat comfortably on a thick floor cushion beside the low keyaki table, wearing a loose dark fleece pullover and plain cotton trousers while Empress Kaguya poured hot roasted barley tea into two stoneware cups.

Between them on the lacquer tray sat a freshly baked chestnut tart sent up from the palace kitchens, alongside a single sheet of thin rice paper bearing the final ratification tally of the Armistice of Kyoto.

Kenji picked up his teacup, warmed his calloused palms against the ceramic, and took a slow sip before glancing at the single sheet of paper. "Washington signed at eleven-forty tonight, right behind Beijing and Moscow," he said easily, resting his arm along his knee. "Three-thousand-kilometer orbital exclusion zone locked in, pre-war borders restored on the peninsula and the Strait, and zero war indemnities to keep their politicians screaming for revenge."

Kaguya smiled warmly, cutting two generous slices of the chestnut tart and sliding the larger piece onto his plate. "Grand Uncle Masamune sent a note from his quarters an hour ago. He says your restraint did more to secure the southern sea lanes than sinking twenty battlecruisers would have—even if Cousin Ryunosuke is still sulking because you wouldn't let him chase the UNAS cruisers back to Lagrange One."

Kenji let out a quiet, genuine laugh and took a bite of the tart, appreciating the rich, roasted sweetness before setting his fork down. "Ryunosuke wants a sweeping space opera victory. What we actually need is ten quiet years to finish upgrading the fleet to Solaris-7 cores, harden our orbital shipyards, and keep our people safe and prosperous while the rest of the world argues over budgets."

He paused, looking out at the falling snow in the quiet garden, his expression turning thoughtful yet completely steady. "I had another fragment of that dream last night, Kaguya—further out this time. Not Earth, and not the Moon. Cold red dust under an alien sky, buried ruins older than human history, and something vast waiting out beyond the edge of our solar system."

Kaguya reached across the low table and laid her warm hand steadily over his knuckles, anchoring him in the quiet room. "Then we use the peace you just won to build ships that can cross the dark," she said softly. "You don't have to carry tomorrow tonight, Kenji. Finish your tart, and let's get some sleep."

"Best order I've heard all week," Kenji said, squeezing her fingers once before picking up his fork with an easy smile.
