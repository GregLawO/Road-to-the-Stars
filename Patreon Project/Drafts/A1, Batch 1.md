### On the Quiet Decade

"*To foreign observers watching the Western Pacific during the ten years following the Kyoto Restoration, the Empire of Japan presented an enigma of quiet self-containment. Continental ministries and Western naval directorates routinely mistook reduced steel exports and closed island slipways for economic exhaustion, assuming that a nation recovering from civil realignment possessed neither the industrial capacity nor the ambition to contest high orbit. They failed to perceive that true maritime strength is not measured by the noise of public shipyards or the rhetoric of international councils, but by the discipline with which a sovereign state builds its foundations in the dark before the storm arrives.*"

— *Annals of the Chrysanthemum Restoration*


**A1.1. Emperor Kenji Himura**   *2130 — Kyoto-Heian Imperial Citadel, Kyoto*

A smear of dark blue ink sat across the second knuckle of Emperor Kenji Himura’s right hand as his thumb pressed flat against the raw weld seam of a gravitic lattice bracket. Underneath the subterranean workshop of the Kyoto-Heian Citadel, the air smelled of ozone, hot machine oil, and damp cedar floorboards. The young foundry welder stood beside the assembly jig, holding his heavy leather apron against his chest and waiting anxiously for the verdict.

Kenji traced the silver ripple of the joint down to the mounting flange, feeling the heat residual in the high-tensile steel. The bracket had been cast for a 750-meter Interceptor Cruiser's internal core framing. If the weld failed under gravimetric load, the core housing would twist three millimeters off center during a mass-shedding cycle.

"The bead is smooth across the top face," Kenji said, his voice plain, relaxed, and unhurried. "You kept your torch steady on the pass. But you pulled the flame back two millimeters too early at the heel."

The welder stepped closer, looking at the dark steel flange. "I was worried about burning through the mounting collar, Heika."

"If you burn through, we scrap the bracket and pour a fresh mold," Kenji said, wiping his thumb with a grease-stained rag. "If you leave a cold lip at the heel, the bracket takes seventy tons of lateral shear when the Solaris-4 core cycles to forty-five percent mass-shedding. The lattice snaps inside the hull where no technician can reach it. Take the afternoon off, clear your head, and sleep. We will run the torch again tomorrow morning."

The welder bowed deeply, his shoulders relaxing. "Thank you, Heika."

As the young man retreated toward the gantry stairs, Lady Shizuka stepped across the workshop threshold. Her embroidered silk sleeves were pulled back neatly and pinned with silver clips to clear the low overhead machinery beams. She held a cedar-bound ledger under one arm and tapped a wooden tally stick against the cover.

"The third slipway at the Kii foundry is running three weeks behind schedule, Heika," Lady Shizuka said, her tone crisp and practical. "The shipyard director is asking for another fifty tons of structural plate before he approves the night shift."

Kenji set the rag on the worktable and looked at the blueprint slates stacked beside the lattice bracket. "The steel is sitting in the warehouse. What is actually stuck?"

"The rivets and the crane crews," Shizuka replied. "They have worked sixteen-hour rotations since the spring equinox. Their fingers are stiff, their vision is blurring, and their hammers are dropping onto the lower gantries."

"Then give them double rations of hot rice and miso tonight, and shift the third battalion off gantry duty until Sunday," Kenji said. "Tired hands ruin good joints, Shizuka. Steel is cheap, but a trained shipwright takes twenty years to build. We do not burn out our people to beat a calendar."

Shizuka noted the instruction in her ledger with a swift stroke of her brush. "The envoys from Seoul and Taipei have arrived in the upper reception hall."

"Give me five minutes to wash the grease off my hands," Kenji said.

An hour later, Kenji sat at the head of the low cedar council table in the Citadel's Privy Chamber. Sunlight filtered through the paper shōji screens, illuminating the woven tatami mats. Across the table, Foreign Envoy Park of South Korea and the Taiwanese trade representative bowed low, laying two leather-bound mutual-defense portfolios flat on the wood.

"Our defense ministry in Seoul needs certainty, Your Majesty," Envoy Park said, leaning forward with his hands resting flat on his knees. "The Eurasian-Continental Pact is positioning kinetic-rod platforms over the Yellow Sea. If hostilities break out, we must know what cover your starfleet will provide."

Kenji picked up the drafting pen, dipped it in dark blue ink, and laid it flat across the treaty pages. "Read the cost clauses on page four before your seals touch the paper. Our hulls will cross the strait to break a blockade or engage an orbital strike line if you call for us under Article Four. But when the fighting ends, our ships lift back to sovereign waters. No Imperial flag remains planted on your soil, no Governors-General are posted, and no permanent garrisons stay behind. We offer a shield between equal neighbors, not an empire."

The Taiwanese trade representative looked at the terms, his eyes scanning the dense columns of black text. "And field command during a joint orbital engagement, Your Majesty?"

"Your admirals command your ships, and our flagships coordinate the defense grid," Kenji said simply. "We do not take your helm, and you do not order our battle lines. We operate as partner fleets."

Envoy Park met Kenji's gaze for a long moment, then picked up his wooden seal. "That is more than Moscow or Washington ever offered." Both envoys affixed their red ink seals to the parchment.

That evening, in the private living quarters of the inner palace, sixteen-year-old Heir Himura Kiyoshi sat across the low dining table, staring intently at his father’s dark coat. A lacquer tray holding grilled sea bream, pickled radishes, and steamed rice sat between them.

"Chichi-sama, the cadet company is launching its high-orbit vector maneuvers next month," Kiyoshi said, holding his chopsticks rigid. "I want my name on the flight roster."

Kenji reached across the table with his own chopsticks, plucked the largest piece of sea bream off Kiyoshi’s plate, and popped it into his mouth. "Clear your rice bowl first, Kiyoshi. An officer who cannot finish his dinner has no business managing a high-G orbital vector."

Kiyoshi flushed slightly, glancing at his stolen fish, but picked up his bowl and began eating without argument.

Later, when the paper shōji screens were drawn against the cool Kyoto night, Empress Kaguya Himura sat beside the kamidana alcove, brushing her long dark hair in the soft lamplight. She looked at Kenji as he unbuttoned his high-collared jacket and leaned his back against the cedar pillar.

"When was the last time you slept more than four hours, Kenji?" she asked softly, her dark eyes calm, attentive, and unhurried.

Kenji reached for the remaining piece of sea bream on the ceramic tray beside her pillow, chewing with quiet satisfaction. "The weld seams are holding, Kaguya. That is enough for tonight."

Kaguya watched him with a gentle smile, asking nothing more about the war councils or the fleet schedules, letting the quiet warmth of the room settle between them.

**A1.2. Audit-Marshal Sano Mitsuko**   *2131 — Hakata Port, Kyushu*

Before the first pale light of dawn broke over Hakata harbor, Audit-Marshal Sano Mitsuko stood inside the cold, sea-damp gloom of a commercial customs warehouse that the Compliance Directorate had sealed four hours earlier. Around her, six Ashigaru-4 sentinels stood motionless in the shadow of stacked iron freight crates, their dark iron-gray armor blending seamlessly into the dim concrete background. Their optical slits were dimmed to a faint crimson ember, and they held their heavy pulse carbines at low ready without making a single sound or mechanical vibration.

Sano checked her wrist slate, her thumb brushing the leather strap. High Censor Kurosawa Renji was due at zero-five-hundred to oversee the contraband inventory personally. Outside the sealed corrugated doors, harbor patrol launches engines purred softly as they maintained a continuous water sweep across the commercial slipways. Sano stepped around a puddle of oily bilge water, keeping her right hand resting comfortably near the grip of her sidearm. The warehouse smelled of rusted steel, saltwater brine, and cold diesel fumes left behind by the midnight shifts.

She checked the perimeter sensors mapped on her slate. The warehouse was completely isolated from the main harbor network. The Compliance Directorate had severed all external data lines at midnight to prevent remote data purges from corporate servers in Shanghai or Manila. Sano glanced at the six Ashigaru-4 sentinels holding their assigned positions. They stood perfectly motionless, unaffected by the cold drafts blowing through the loading dock seals. Her junior officer stood near the main gate, checking the secondary perimeter locks and confirming that all harbor surveillance feeds were redirected to Directorate channels.

Heavy boots echoed against the wet concrete outside the loading bay doors. High Censor Kurosawa stepped through the side hatch, his dark woolen cloak pinned with the silver chrysanthemum badge of the Kansatsu-In. He did not look at the sentinels or at Sano. His sharp, dark eyes went directly to the center of the warehouse floor, where eight dock workers and a ship captain stood huddled beside an open iron transport container.

"This harbor was sealed to unmonitored commercial freight at midnight," Kurosawa said, his voice flat, calm, and low. He did not raise his tone, yet the cold authority in his words made the dock workers freeze. "You have one opportunity to step away from that container and hand over the black-market self-modifying relay cores inside."

The smuggling captain’s face went pale under the overhead halogen lamp. He took half a step back, his hands opening in silent submission. But the armed crewman to his left, a broad-shouldered technician wearing a grease-stained mechanic's vest, reached inside his heavy wool jacket.

"Don't move!" Sano shouted, unholstering her sidearm and bringing the sights up.

The crewman pulled a suppressed kinetic pistol from his coat lining and fired. The slug cracked into the concrete pillar six inches beside Sano’s head, spraying sharp stone chips across her cheek and collar.

Kurosawa flicked his right index finger forward.

The six Ashigaru-4 sentinels moved in a sudden, silent blur of iron and hydraulic power.

The nearest sentinel closed six meters across the concrete in a fraction of a second. Its heavy gauntlet struck the gunman’s wrist with a sharp, sickening crunch. The pistol clattered across the wet floorboards. Sano lunged forward, grabbed the gunman’s disarmed wrist, and snapped heavy steel cuffs around his forearms, pinning his chest against the cold metal crate.

"All weapons on the deck!" Sano barked, her fingers gripping the cold steel cuffs tightly as her boots braced against the gunman's thrashing legs.

The remaining seven smugglers dropped to their knees immediately, their hands raised above their heads in complete surrender. The Ashigaru-4 sentinels resumed their static posts instantly, their crimson optical slits dimming back into the shadows as if they had never moved. Sano wiped a thin trickle of blood from her cheek where the concrete chip had grazed her skin, maintaining her grip on the cuffed suspect.

Kurosawa walked unhurriedly up to the open iron container and lifted a cedar clipboard containing the manifest. He scanned the stamped clearance codes with a practiced, steady eye. He examined the raised seal impression under his pocket flashlight, checking the serial numbers against the Directorate registry database.

"The customs seal on this container was replaced three days ago in Sasebo," Kurosawa said, his voice dropping into a razor-thin tone. "And the registry timestamp arrived four hours late on the Directorate ledger. Someone paid a harbor clerk to ignore the entry log."

One of the captured crewmen spat blood onto the concrete and glared up at Kurosawa. "What difference does a four-hour delay make? You take our cargo and lock it in your dark underground vaults anyway! What is the Directorate hiding down there under the mountains?"

At the crewman’s words, Kurosawa’s jaw clamped shut with rigid, sudden tightness. His vocal cords froze, and his left hand rose instinctively, pressing two stiffened fingers flat against his breastbone over his dark wool tunic.

Sano stood two paces away, watching his face closely. She saw the sudden rigidity in his jaw and the sharp, silent tension that locked his throat for three full seconds before his breathing smoothed out again. She knew Kurosawa was a veteran of the Restoration War, but she had never seen his face lock with such absolute, unnatural stillness.

Kurosawa lowered his hand slowly, his expression returning to cold, unreadable composure. He looked down at the manifest, turned toward Sano, and opened his mouth as if to name the origin port listed on the forged clearance seal. Then he looked at the captured crewmen still sitting in earshot on the concrete floor.

"Not here," Kurosawa said plainly. He closed the clipboard with a sharp click of the wooden latch and turned toward the exit. "Seal the warehouse and call the transport detail. We move the crate to the Kinki-Kyoku vaults before dawn."

Sano watched him walk toward the loading dock doors, noting the absolute stillness in his posture. She signaled the Ashigaru-4 sentinels to hold the perimeter, remaining at her post until the armored Directorate trucks arrived to clear the bay.

**A1.3. Capt. Han Jae-won**   *2131 — DMZ Ridge, South Korea*

Captain Han Jae-won walked along the frozen crest of the northern defense ridge, his heavy combat boots crunching into the hard-packed snow. The bitter morning wind swept down from the northern granite peaks, whipping against his collar. Beside him, a staff colonel from Seoul adjusted his fur-collared parka and pointed with a gloved finger at a long line of newly poured concrete bunker emplacements stretching across the icy hillside.

Han stopped beside a fresh gun embrasure and pressed his bare thumb against the damp concrete. The surface was cold and stiff, but under the thin skin of morning frost, the wet material yielded slightly under his weight.

"The pour is still soft," Han said, wiping his thumb on his trousers. "If Pact kinetic artillery hits this sector before Friday, these bunker walls will crumble like dry biscuits."

"The concrete will cure in time, Captain," the staff colonel said impatiently, pulling his map folder tight against his chest. "And even if the bunkers are delayed, our treaty units are already in position. The Empire supplied us with two hundred Helios-2 automata fireteams last month under our mutual defense accord. They don't need concrete walls to hold a trench line."

Han turned to look down the southern slope, where an Imperial Legion advisor in dark tactical armor stood watching a night combat drill. A fireteam of four Helios-2 automata—their sealed, hatchless gravitic torsos painted in matte olive drab—advanced steadily through a simulated artillery barrage. They walked into the live explosive blasts without flinching, their sealed internal cores absorbing the shockwaves while Korean NCOs followed twenty meters behind in absolute safety.

Behind Han, two young Korean staff officers muttered under their breath. "Look at them," one whispered. "Sending iron machines into the fire while living men walk behind. It looks like cowardice."

Han turned on his heel and glared at the officers until they dropped their eyes. "That isn't cowardice," Han said sharply. "That is armor that doesn't leave a widow in Seoul when a shell lands. Remember that before you judge how Kyoto fights."

He walked down the timber stairs to the command dugout and laid the sector defense map across the wooden trestle table. The dugout was warm, heated by an iron stove in the corner that hissed softly with burning coal. The staff colonel stood beside him, tapping the map with a wooden pencil.

"We have enough Helios-2 units to cover the central redoubts," the colonel said, tracing a red line along the primary road junction. "We can leave the left flank open until the next supply convoy arrives from Incheon."

Han looked at the left flank of the ridge—a three-kilometer stretch of rough, rocky ground where the concrete bunkers were least finished and the trench lines were barely scraped out of the frozen shale. He knew that if an enemy vanguard tested their resolve, they would probe the weakest seam first.

"No," Han said plainly, taking out his ink pen. "We keep the bunker construction going on the main ridge, but I am assigning one trial fireteam of Helios-2 automata to the left flank immediately."

He picked up his stylus and signed his name at the bottom of the deployment order, sealing the command. He set the pen down flat on the table, knowing the left flank was the most vulnerable stretch of the mountain, and he was not going to leave it unguarded.

He stepped back out onto the dugout stairs, watching the morning fog roll over the frozen valleys below. He checked his wrist watch, noted the guard shift change, and walked back along the perimeter wire to inspect the trench lines himself.

He stopped at the left flank position, watching the four Helios-2 frames take up their stations among the rocks. They stood completely still, their matte-green torsos dusted with snow and their optical sensors scanning the northern valley in silent vigilance. Han leaned against the sandbag parapet, satisfied that whatever came down from the northern passes would meet an unyielding wall of steel.

He walked back toward the command post as the sun broke over the eastern ridge, casting long shadows across the snowfields. He checked the wireless comms link with the Imperial Legion advisor, confirming that targeting data feeds from high-orbit Japanese sensor pickets were syncing directly with the Korean bunker consoles. The advisor gave a brief nod from the trench line below, confirming the telemetry lock.

Han stood by the main dugout entrance, watching his men hand out hot morning soup to the trench squads. The soldiers took their steel mess tins and sat under the lee of the reinforced parapet, their breath misting in the cold morning air. Across the valley, the northern ridge line remained silent, but Han knew that quiet borders were built on preparation, not optimism.

He walked down the trench line to check the ammunition bunkers at Sector Three. The mortar crews were stacking 120mm high-explosive crates inside the reinforced alcoves. Han inspected the moisture seals on the crate lids, ensuring that snow melt would not seep into the propellant casings during the night thaw. The gunnery sergeant gave him a sharp salute, reporting that thirty rounds per tube were pre-staged for immediate barrage fire. Han nodded approval and returned to the dugout to file his shift log.

In his office, Han sat down at his desk and pulled out his daily operational journal. He logged the completion of the left flank deployment, noting the exact coordinates of the four Helios-2 positions. He checked the wireless signal strength once more, verifying that the command feed from Seoul was clear across all frequencies.

He leaned back in his chair, taking a slow sip of hot tea from his enamel mug. Outside the dugout window, the morning sun illuminated the line of concrete bunkers stretching across the snow-covered crest. Han closed his journal, capped his pen, and rested his hands flat on the wooden desk, listening to the steady cadence of sentry boots passing along the timber catwalks outside.

**A1.4. Maj. Élodie Marchand**   *2131 — Lunar Station Copernicus*

Inside the high-altitude glass dome of UNAS-EU Joint Intelligence at Lunar Station Copernicus, Major Élodie Marchand sat at her primary console, re-keying a long column of trade figures for the third time. The artificial gravity grid hummed under her boots, sending a low, persistent vibration through her heavy steel desk. Outside the thick quartz viewing ports, the stark white landscape of the Copernicus crater stretched out toward the black horizon.

On her left monitor, the official diplomatic manifest from Kyoto listed Japanese commercial steel exports to Western markets as down by forty-two percent over the last fiscal year. On her right monitor, her own orbital freight tally showed hundreds of heavy cargo lighters lifting weekly from Yokohama and Kobe into unlisted high-orbit trajectories.

"The numbers don't match," Marchand muttered to herself, tapping her stylus against her chin. "If their domestic industrial output is falling as fast as their ministry claims, where are those high-orbit lighters actually going?"

Her directorate chief in Brussels walked past her console, holding a steaming mug of synthetic coffee and glancing down at her displays. "They don't match because the Japanese economy is stagnating, Élodie. They spent too much on domestic reconstruction after the Restoration War, and now their foundries are running out of raw coal. Brussels is drafting a formal commercial sanctions package for tomorrow's council vote."

An hour later, Marchand established a secure video link with a senior Japanese trade envoy in Kyoto. She presented the proposed steel import restrictions, fully expecting a long, aggressive round of diplomatic pushback.

Instead, the Japanese envoy listened calmly, bowed politely on the screen, and accepted the reduced steel quotas without a single word of protest or bargaining. He simply noted the date and closed his leather ledger.

Marchand sat back in her chair after the call disconnected, her brow furrowed with deep suspicion. "He agreed too fast," she thought, leaning back against her headrest. "A nation starving for steel doesn't walk away from export quotas without fighting for every single ton."

Later that night, Marchand opened an encrypted comms channel to General Williams at NORAD-Orbital Command in Cheyenne Mountain. Williams’s image appeared on her secondary display, his face shadowed by the dim red night lighting of the bunker.

"Look at these optical tracks, Major," Williams said, transferring a set of classified deep-space telescope files to her slate. "Our Maui tracking station caught a Japanese hull drifting past the lunar limb last night. Acceleration was instantaneous, with zero thermal exhaust, no chemical plume, and no detectable drive signature."

Marchand pulled up her own observatory logs from Copernicus for the exact same timestamp. She cross-referenced the orbital coordinates. The faint sun-glint captured by her lunar telescope matched Williams’s track perfectly. A 750-meter warship had covered four thousand kilometers in seven minutes without firing a single reaction thruster.

Marchand looked at the two matching logs on her screen. In eight hours, the European Council in Brussels would vote to approve economic sanctions against a nation they believed was industrially exhausted.

"The sanctions vote is going ahead tomorrow morning," Marchand said quietly into her headset mic, staring at the impossible velocity curve on her slate. "And nobody in Europe has any idea what they are actually building up there in the dark."

She powered down her secondary display screen and looked out through the dome glass at the illuminated white rim of Copernicus crater. The quiet void of space stretched across the horizon, vast and unreadable, hiding whatever fleet Kyoto was assembling behind the Moon.

She adjusted her headset, filed her intelligence report to Cheyenne Mountain, and prepared for her morning briefing with Brussels. She pulled up the orbital trajectory overlay once more, tracing the flat line where the Japanese hull had vanished off active radar. The math did not lie: Kyoto was not suffering an industrial collapse, but hiding a fleet in plain sight.

She stood up from her console and walked to the observation gallery, looking out at the Earth suspended in the black sky above the lunar horizon. The blue oceans glinted under the sun, shadowed by the orbital transit paths where Western diplomats still believed they controlled the high ground.

She returned to her desk, opened her personal log, and typed a brief memorandum to her successor on the shift rotation. She highlighted the discrepancies between declared trade figures and observed orbital launches, advising that deep-space optical monitoring remain focused on the lunar shadow. She logged off her workstation, locked her data slates in the heavy wall safe, and left the intelligence hall for the sleeping quarters.

As she stepped into the quiet corridor of the Copernicus quarters wing, Marchand paused by the observation port. She watched a commercial UNAS freight shuttle descend toward the crater landing pad, its chemical reaction thrusters flaring bright orange against the dark lunar soil. She contrasted that noisy, exhaust-heavy burn with the completely silent, reactionless track of the Japanese cruiser she had analyzed hours earlier. She knew that when the Western ministries finally recognized what Kyoto had built, the strategic balance of the entire solar system would shift in an instant.

She reached her quarters, unlocked the door with her security cipher, and sat down at her personal desk. She pulled up the raw astronomical tracking files one last time before resting, studying the clean spatial translation curve. There was no maneuvering thruster burn on record, no mass ejection, and no energy cascade across any infrared band. The Empire of Japan had developed a reactionless drive system while Western analysts were busy debating commercial steel tariffs. Marchand saved the encrypted file copy to her personal drive and turned off her desk lamp, knowing that morning would bring a briefing that Brussels was entirely unprepared to hear. She pulled her blanket up, listening to the ambient hum of Copernicus Station, and stared at the dark ceiling until sleep came.

She stood up from her desk and stepped back out into the central corridor, passing two junior watch officers who were logging routine satellite telemetry. She walked down the quiet steel walkway to the mess hall, poured a fresh cup of black coffee, and looked through the viewport at the distant starfields. The silence of the lunar base offered no answers, but Marchand knew that when the first wave of Japanese gravitic warships revealed themselves, the intelligence failure would belong to Brussels, not Copernicus.

**A1.5. Sgt. Ōtsuka Gen**   *2131 — Low-Orbit Sovereign Shipyard*

Sergeant Ōtsuka Gen stood on the narrow steel catwalk of the low-orbit Sovereign Shipyard, his magnetic boots locked firmly to the deck plates. All around him, the unpainted hull of a 750-meter Interceptor Cruiser hung suspended inside a massive scaffolding framework, illuminated by the harsh glare of construction floodlights. Below his boots, the blue marble of Earth rotated slowly in silence.

Beside the airlock hatch, four Ashigaru-4 sentinels stood motionless in their wall alcoves, their dark armor silent and their optical slits dimmed to faint crimson threads. Ōtsuka held his handheld security slate, reading badge numbers aloud as technicians passed through the checkpoint.

"Badge four-seven-two, cleared. Badge four-seven-three, cleared," Ōtsuka called out, matching each digital code against the master roster.

Down the catwalk, Lord Commander Tachibana Haruka moved with silent grace in her crimson-and-black Gin-Musha exosuit, accompanying Lady Shizuka on an inspection route. Tachibana’s eyes scanned every dark shadow beneath the hull gantries, her suit's Solaris-4M Ember core emitting a barely audible golden hum.

Suddenly, a red warning icon flashed on Ōtsuka’s slate. At the main power conduit junction fifty meters down the gantry, three men in gray yard coveralls stepped out of an unmapped maintenance bay. One of them pulled a shaped thermal breaching charge from his tool bag.

A security trigger snapped active across the yard network.

In the wall alcoves, the four Ashigaru-4 sentinels awoke instantly. Heavy magnetic clamps clacked against the deck as four pairs of bright amber optical slits flared into life. The automata surged forward in perfect unison, their armored boots pounding against the catwalk.

Tachibana kicked off the gantry rail. Her Ember core flared bright gold, launching her across fifty meters of zero-gravity void in half a second.

The lead saboteur raised a suppressed pistol and fired. Tachibana’s external shear barrier flared, deflecting the kinetic slug harmlessly into the structural steel. She closed the distance, struck the gunman’s wrist with the flat of her gauntlet, and pinned him against the steel bulkhead with a single, brutal impact.

Ōtsuka followed his fireteam into the breach, drawing his sidearm and dropping to one knee as the Ashigaru-4 sentinels tackled the second saboteur before he could ignite the thermal charge. The third man dropped his tools and raised his hands in immediate surrender.

"Area secure, Tachibana-sama!" Ōtsuka barked, his thumb pressing hard against the grip of his sidearm as his chest heaved inside his pressure suit.

Tachibana held the third saboteur against the deck plate with one gauntleted hand. With her other hand, she reached down and tore the plastic identification badge from the man’s coveralls. She pressed the badge against Ōtsuka’s slate.

A green light flashed on the screen. The magnetic badge carried a valid, high-clearance yard code issued earlier that evening.

Tachibana turned her helmet toward Ōtsuka, her voice cold and sharp over the helmet comms. "Who stood security at this hatch before your shift, Ōtsuka-san?"

Ōtsuka tapped his slate, pulling up the previous shift log. He opened the file, and his eyes widened as the roster name appeared on the screen.

He held the slate up toward Tachibana's visor, his thumb frozen against the glass as the system confirmed that the clearance had been authorized by a senior officer inside the Imperial Naval Directorate.

Tachibana looked at the name on the slate for a long moment, her golden visor reflecting the dim ambient light of the shipyard bay. She did not speak, but her gauntlet tightened around the saboteur's shoulder harness until the steel clips groaned under the pressure.

"Secure the suspects in Bay Four," Tachibana ordered, her voice completely even and dark. "Notify the Compliance Directorate that we have a breach on the high gantries."

Ōtsuka bowed his head. "Understood, Lord Commander." He signaled his squad to move the prisoners down the catwalk, knowing the conspiracy reached far beyond the shipyard floor.

He escorted the prisoners to the holding cell, double-checking the magnetic locks on the door. He stood guard outside the hatch until the Kansatsu-In Audit-Marshals arrived from orbit to take custody of the saboteurs, his mind racing as he contemplated the compromised shift log.

After the Audit-Marshals loaded the prisoners into their orbital transport, Ōtsuka returned to the gantry catwalk. The construction floodlights continued to cast long shadows across the unfinished cruiser hull. He checked his sidearm, secured the safety, and stood beside the four Ashigaru-4 sentinels at the airlock hatch. The sentinels had already returned to their static posts, their amber slits dimming back to faint crimson threads. Ōtsuka looked out at the dark expanse of low Earth orbit, knowing that even inside the Empire's most secure shipyard, hidden enemies were already at work.

He wiped cold sweat from his neck inside his suit collar, watching the blue curve of the planet pass below the gantry scaffolding. The shift log entry on his slate burned in his memory—a valid authorization code from the highest levels of the fleet command structure in Kyoto. He re-locked his security slate, attached it to his suit harness, and assumed his post beside the airlock, maintaining silent watch over the high gantries until morning.

He checked the power junction meters one final time, confirming that the main reactor conduit had suffered zero damage during the brief struggle. The gold-and-black Interceptor Cruiser hull rested peacefully in its docking cradles, its stealth skin dark against the background of space. Ōtsuka rested his gloved hand on his sidearm holster, standing straight beside the silent Ashigaru sentinels as the station completed its orbit into the Earth's shadow.

He looked up at the shipyard gantry array, watching the automated crane arms resume their silent assembly sweeps along the cruiser's spine. The golden-amber status lights of the main construction bay flickered in rhythm with the station's primary power grid. Ōtsuka adjusted his grip on his rifle harness, knowing that whoever had issued that clearance code still held an office in Kyoto, and that the true battle for the Sovereign Shipyard had only just begun. He took a deep breath of pressure-suit oxygen and settled into his watch rhythm, vigilant for any secondary breach.
