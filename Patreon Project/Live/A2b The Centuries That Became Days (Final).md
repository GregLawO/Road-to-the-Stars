# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (33)*

### *Galveston, 2053*

**June 2053.** The name on the referral list was T. SHEPARD, pancreas, advanced. Danny Shepard stood in the doorway of the monitor room for a full minute before he said why he had come. The clinic had opened two weeks earlier. Twelve screens on the wall showed the public floor, the hidden wing, the dock, and the street.

“Come in,” I said. “What do you need?”

“My father's on your list.” He put his palm flat against the doorframe. “Tom Shepard. He took me in when I was eight, after my father's gantry cable snapped. He has three weeks. I didn't name my family when I signed. I wanted them clear of this.” He did not take his eyes off me. “I've never taken anything from you I wasn't working for.”

“The terms cover your family. Name him.”

“Tom Shepard. Yes, sir. I'm naming him.”

Tom in the hidden wing would see the hidden wing, and a man who sees it asks what it is. I had counted that cost when I wrote the terms, and it had not changed. I looked at Danny's hand on the frame.

“The public floor runs a Level 1 pod,” I said. “It would clear the cancer, but he's too far gone for it to hold. He needs the Level 2 wing, and I'll move him tonight. Is that enough, or do you need more?”

He let out a breath. “That's enough, sir.” He did not take his hand off the frame. “One more thing. They already know who I work for but not where. I don't want my family to know.”

“I can't promise they won't ask you. I can promise I'll never tell you to lie to them.”

“You won't have to. I don't exactly know what you did to stop me talking about it, but it's working.”

Three days later, at ten past six in the morning, a young woman sat in the waiting room with a folded uniform on the chair beside her. She had been there most of the three days. I watched her on the fourth screen from the left. The staff had been told to say nothing, so she had spent that time looking at the bare wall behind the front desk.

I had taken the license off that wall the morning we opened. It named the Janus Foundation and its trustee, and a trustee has a home address. I had looked at the empty paint for four seconds and decided it was fine. Hannah Shepard noticed it in the first hour.

Danny stood behind my chair. He had come in without a word and laid his palm flat against the wall. Neither of us spoke while the frosted doors opened and Tom walked out on his own feet.

“He's standing straight,” Danny said. His voice had dropped almost to nothing. “Look at him. He's standing straight.”

On the screen, Tom looked at the bare wall, then at his daughter, and said something that made her cross the room and take his wrist. Danny took his hand off the wall.

“Side door,” I said. “Now, before she looks up.”

“Yes, sir.”

The card was in my pocket. At four that morning I had sat with a pen over it and found that I could not write my name on it, or a number, or anything else anyone could follow. But a blank card still says that someone stands behind the door and will not say who. I wanted Hannah to know that much.

I went out the back and around the building to the front step. Ashby sat in the car at the curb with the lobby on a handheld screen. “They're at the door,” he said in my ear. I slid the card under it and ran for the car.

**Ten days later.** They came to my office together. Tom wore a clean shirt and steel-toed boots and held his cap in his hands, and Ruth wore a cardigan with a ring of keys at her belt. Tom did not sit. He picked up my stapler, turned it over twice, and opened it to look at the empty channel, the way he checked every tool in a room before he spoke. Then he closed it and put it back exactly where it had been.

“I took the training session,” he said. “Not the rest of what your people offered. They said I could turn that down, so I did.” He looked straight at me. “Now. I've got a boy named Danny I haven't seen in five years. He sends money through Ruth and says he works for your group. Do you know where he is?”

I had decided on the stairs that I would not lie. “Danny works for a company I run. He saw your name on a list and asked me to move you to the hidden wing. He asked me not to say more, so I'd like to leave it there.”

“Is he all right?”

“As far as I know, yes. Is that enough, or do you need more?”

Tom nodded slowly. Ruth had not moved. “Mr. Himura,” she said, “who are you?”

“Kenji Himura. I build things, mostly. I run this clinic because there wasn't anyone else I'd trust with it.”

She looked at me for a long moment. “Mm-hm.” She waited, as though more might come. It did not. “I believe you. But you've told me what you do, not who you are.”

“No, ma'am. That is as much as I can say without lying to you.”

“I see.” She folded her hands. “Thank you. For my husband.”

“Is there anything you need from me, Ma'am? I have one request of my own. Please don't tell your daughter about Danny and me. She'd most likely ask questions I can't answer.”

“Won't say a word,” Tom said at once.

Ruth waited a second longer than he had. “Not yet, then.” She was giving me time, not a promise.

“Son,” Tom said, “I ran a shipyard crew for most of my life. I walked your building coming in. The pump room has a gauge reading a number nobody's looked at in two years. Show me the service log.”

I flinched at “son.” “There isn't one. Nobody has had the job.” I had no one to run the building. “The job is yours, if you want it.”

“That's what I figured.” He set his cap on the desk. “I'd have asked. I'll take it.”

“Start Monday. Tell me what you need.”

Ruth reached up and straightened my collar, which did not need it. “And who looks after you?” she said. When I did not answer, she nodded.

I walked them to the stairs.

**December 2053.** Marchetti had nine follow-up dates on her legal pad, a name beside each, and she had rewritten the order three times. I set a gray block on the pad. It was silicon carbide, five centimeters square, with a strip of tape across one face.

"What is it?" she said.

"A Dumb AI. It does one kind of work, and I have given it this one." I put the block in a reader on her desk. "Read it the nine names and dates."

She capped her pen and read them aloud, slowly and all of them, the way she read to clients. By the time she reached the ninth name, a list had filled the reader's screen.

The order was not hers. It had moved two names ahead of where she had put them, and she ran her pen down the list against the terms in her folder and checked those two twice.

"It is right," she said. "Both times." She looked at the screen and not at me. "Nobody told it which two."

"No. It sets the order itself. That is the work it has."

She turned to the service contract, found a clause, and read it to the block. Then she asked what the clause meant. The list stayed where it was. One line appeared under it saying the question was outside its task, and nothing else came.

"It will not answer outside its task," I said. "Nothing you read to it will teach it a new one, because a new task has to be given to it. Is that enough, or do you need more?"

"Not yet." She set the pen across the page. "What will it not do on its own?"

"Rebuild the whole schedule at once. If it is ever told to, it waits for a human to validate that first."

"Who?"

"You."

"Then I will want that written down." She uncapped the pen, dated a clean page, and wrote the first line of the procedure: any rebuild of the schedule is validated by Helen Marchetti, in writing.

She did not ask where the design came from, and I did not say.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Gunnery Sergeant Dominic 'Gunny' Vance*

### *The green book, 2051 to 2053*

**Summer 2051.** The hospice room had one window and one door. Vance had lain in it for weeks, learning every sound the corridor made. On a Monday a young man opened the door and asked what he could teach. Vance said he had nothing to pay with but what was in his head, and the young man said that was what he wanted.

When Vance woke from the pod, he could breathe to the bottom of his chest. His body felt the way it had not felt in decades. Years of battlefield injuries and cellular decay vanished. He stepped onto the floor without the usual stiffness, his muscles coiled with an effortless strength he hadn't felt since his twenties.

His first order was to send for Ashby, a radio technician he had found after the trenches, because someone had to hold the radio. He bought a green notebook at the hospital gift shop that afternoon, with the first cash he had carried in a year, and began to write down every site and every threat to it.

**Winter 2051 to spring 2052.** He brought in the first squad that winter. In the spring, a few months before Byron Pyrrus signed, he added a second squad.

In the spring he also brought in three intelligence staff, who read court dockets to find the people who might one day buy treatment from Janus.

**November 2052.** His rule was that someone must always watch the road beyond the fence. A wall only helps if you see what is coming up to it. A recruit named Danny Shepard read a building by where it carried its weight, so Vance posted him to Galveston as site engineer and guard lead for the hidden wing. He never had to tell Danny twice to walk a floor before standing on it. Vance liked him at once.

**Summer 2053.** Three clinics opened: Galveston first, then Marseille, then Singapore. Kenji assigned security droids to guard every pod in the hidden wings, day and night, and made it so that no pod moved without his knowing. He also told Vance that a pod taken by force would destroy its own core. The platoon was full by autumn. In 2054 Vance moved Danny to Houston, when Tom Shepard took over the Galveston building.

That same summer, the two machines Kenji had built himself were finished. They looked and moved like men, and they did not tire. Vance stopped walking behind Kenji. For a month he felt the empty space at his back.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (23 and 25)*

### *Galveston, 2054 and 2056*

**November 2054.** Hannah watched the man across the table finish everything on his plate, down to the parsley. Nobody ate like that unless they had once gone without. Her mother had set his place beside her father's, and he sat with his back to the wall and his eyes on the kitchen door.

A fifth place was laid at the end of the table, the plate turned upside down, a napkin folded beside it. Her brother Danny worked in Houston for a security contractor, doing gates and fences, and he was hardly ever home. Her mother set his place every Sunday anyway. The new man looked at the empty chair half a second longer than a stranger would. He did not ask whose it was.

Through the front window she could see a gray sedan at the curb with two men in it. They had been there since five. Her mother had gone out twice to ask them in, and neither had come. This was the first time Hannah had sat across from the man her father worked for. She had refused his clinic's offer of training the year before, and she had been polite but cold to it ever since.

“He fixed the east pump with a valve seat he made himself,” her father said, to no one in particular. “I said show me, and he did.”

“What do you do when you're not at the clinic?” Hannah asked.

“I build things.” He put his fork down and squared it against the edge of his plate.

“What things?”

“Tools, mostly. Gauges, jigs, small parts that other things get made with.”

“Tools for what?”

“Other tools.” He did not smile, but something in his face allowed that it was funny. “It's less interesting than it sounds.”

“You don't mention anybody. No wife, no brother, no town you miss. Most people who've been somewhere say so.”

“Hannah,” her mother said.

“It's all right, Ma'am.” He looked at Hannah, not at her mother. “I don't talk much about where I'm from.”

Her father picked up the gravy boat and put it down again. “He pays on time,” he said.

When the plates were empty, the man stacked them and took them to the counter without being asked. He squared the pile before he sat down. Hannah went up to bed. She did not like him. She had not caught him in a lie, but that bothered her more than a lie would have.

**March 2056.** She had left the Navy in February, and her father had talked her into the security post. Three weeks in, she walked the building with Kenji on a Thursday, her father's tape measure in her jacket pocket. She had measured every door herself. She trusted her own numbers more than anyone's floor plans.

She stopped at the front desk. “Okay, so. There's no license on this wall.”

“It's in a drawer. It's real. I took it off the wall on purpose.”

“Why?”

“It names an owner, and I'd rather not be found.”

“By who?”

“I can't tell you that yet.” He did not hurry. “Do you want the rest of your list here, or do you want to walk?”

“Walk.”

They went down the main corridor, past a nurse with a clipboard and a clerk with a lanyard turned backward. “Nobody wears a name tag,” she said.

“That's my doing too. The people who work here have been asked not to give their names to patients. I'd rather you heard that from me.”

At the east wing she stopped in front of a door she had measured twice and never opened. “I'm not allowed past this.”

“No. That door stays shut to you.”

“Two gray frames stand in the back corridor. My father calls them equipment. What are they?”

“I can't answer that.”

“The door crew is better trained than any clinic would pay for.”

“They're good because I pay for good ones.”

“And the two men who follow you from room to room and never sit down?”

“They're my guards.” He glanced toward the corridor, where they stood a pace apart, as they did every day. “That's all I can say.”

She looked at him until she saw it was true and that he would say nothing more. “Which of these can you explain?”

“None of them, to you, yet.”

“Yet.” She nodded slowly. “I'll remember you said that.”

She wrote it down. Then she asked whether she could retrain the door crew her own way, and he said yes without a pause. By Friday it was done. That evening she took a sheet of paper from the drawer, folded it in thirds, and wrote the date at the head of the first line. *Why does he stand at the edge of every room?* She had decided not to ask him.

**June 2056.** The desk's Dumb AI had compiled the front desk log for May into eleven pages, and a clerk had validated it. Hannah read it standing at the desk, the way she read every month’s.

Two hospital chains had asked for inspections, a pharmacists’ guild had filed a complaint, and an insurance board wanted patient records. A European health ministry had asked to visit. Reporters from four countries had phoned to ask who owned the clinic. Marseille and Singapore were getting the same letters, and the clerk had pinned a note on the board saying so. Every request had been answered with a form letter that the desk's Dumb AI drafted and the clerk validated, and none of them had come back.

She took the log down the back corridor. Her father was under the pipe above the east wing door, working a valve that had dripped since April. Kenji stood at the far end with one hand on the door frame, and his two quiet men stood beside him.

“Okay, so. Walk me through this,” she said, holding up the log. “Three years ago a machine that cures cancer opened in a Galveston strip lot. By now every government should have a soldier at the gate, and every drug company should have a lawyer in the lobby. I have two on the east wing door, and in three months I haven’t had to use either.”

“Every country that has a clinic has a license,” Kenji said. “Each one was negotiated before the doors opened, and the terms are in the license. The governments that don’t have one yet are in talks. Several of those are running now.”

“Who sits at those talks?”

“I can’t answer that.”

“The machine on the public floor has a plate that says Oyama. Nobody has asked Oyama how it works?”

“Many have. The design belongs to a foundation. A license lets a country run the pod, but it doesn’t give the country the design. Every government that asked got the same answer.”

“And they accepted that.”

“They signed it.”

Her father had stopped turning the wrench. He did not look round. “A man doesn’t sign that kind of paper because he’s polite,” he said. “Somebody showed him what he’d lose if he didn’t.”

“Or what he’d gain,” Kenji said. “A government that closes a clinic gets an empty building. The line outside it moves to the next country. Most ministers can count that.”

Hannah turned a page of the log. “Oyama is going to put robot arms on the market next year. I read the trade notice. Dock crews will lose jobs, and the unions will want to know why. Where are the talks for that?”

“Those started this spring, in the same countries. The terms are different, and I can’t tell you what they are.”

“Can’t, or won’t?”

“I can’t answer that.”

She looked at the log, and at the long run of form letters that had stopped at the desk. Nobody had come through the door in three years, and nobody had explained why not. There was one question left, and she knew it exactly. What had he shown those governments, that they would sign? She did not ask it.

“I’ll keep two on the door,” she said.

“Thank you.”

Kenji squared the cuff of his sleeve and walked toward the monitor room. His two quiet men followed a pace behind. Her father went back to the valve.

That evening she took a sheet of paper from the drawer, folded in thirds. She wrote under the first line. *What did he show them?*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · The Watcher*

### *The road to the Gulf, 2055 and 2056*

**April 2055.** A yellow note was stuck to the dashboard of the rental car. It held three license plates in his own handwriting and one word, underlined twice: *Texas.*

A clerk at a private airfield had sold him the plates. A flight plan had given him the word. It was all his employer's money had bought in three weeks. His employer was a rich man whose rivals had started leaving on private checkups and coming back ten years younger, and he wanted to know where they went. Texas was a state, not an address.

The watcher parked outside the hotel, left the note where it was, and went into the bar. A minister's driver sat at the far end under a television, in a good dark jacket, with his cap on the next stool. The watcher had spent two nights finding out where the man drank. He took the stool beside the cap and ordered the same beer.

The driver was talking within ten minutes.

“Nine days,” he said. “Early April. I drove him to the airfield and he told me not to wait.”

“And then?” the watcher asked, as if it hardly mattered.

“He came back without his walking stick. Never asked where it was.” The driver turned his glass on the bar. “Takes the stairs two at a time now. Sixty-eight years old. He doesn't take his pills anymore.”

“Where did the plane go?”

“Nobody tells a driver that. I asked him at breakfast where he'd been. He said he'd been looked after.”

“By who?”

“He smiled and ate his egg.” The driver's eyes went to the cap on the stool, then back to his glass. “Two other ministers' drivers say the same. Gone a week, back walking.”

The watcher bought him another beer and gave no name. The driver did not ask for one. Out in the car, he wrote the airfield and the dates under the word *Texas.*

**May 2055 to autumn 2056.** His employer lent him three men and a budget, and for seventeen months they watched hotels, airports, and the roads out of them. They worked in shifts from parked cars and from a rented flat across from a terminal. The clients were moved at night, in unmarked cars that never took the same route twice. A night clerk at one hotel told him the same car came on the first Tuesday of a month, and the next month a different car came on a Friday.

Every car he followed was lost. One went into a rainstorm on the Houston ring road and did not come out. One turned into a covered garage, and an identical car drove out of it. Once four cars left a hotel together and went four different ways. He logged each one with the plates, the time, and the place where he lost it. Most nights he slept in the car.

By the autumn the log held only fragments. Every flight plan ended within a day's drive of Houston. Four of the cars he had lost were seen again, empty, on the coast road south of the city, at an hour when nothing else used it. A station clerk sold him a fuel receipt from one of them. It was from Galveston.

He drew the fragments on a map. The cars never stopped at a hotel or a hospital. They went somewhere that took cars at night.

**November 2056.** He sat in a van in a lot across the water from the back dock of a charity clinic in Galveston. It was the one building on his map that fit. The log was due before his employer's quarterly review on Friday, and he had nothing else to put in it. His breath fogged the windshield, and twice he wiped it with his sleeve.

At ten past three a car came down the service road with its lights low. Two guards stood at the dock gate in heavy coats, stamping against the cold. One of them lifted a hand, and they waved the car through. The watcher counted two men on the dock and no one else. The car could have been a delivery. The dock could have been only a dock.

In the morning he wrote the line in the log and wrote the words he had to. *Best guess. Unconfirmed.*

His employer read the last page standing at his desk. He took a long time over that line.

“A charity,” he said. “That's your answer.”

“It's my best guess. I wrote that on the page.”

“I read what you wrote.” He signed the log and laid the pen across it. “Find out who owns it.”

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · The Director*

### *Cleveland and Toledo, January 2056*

**January 2056.** A man in a gray Gundarium suit stepped off a four-meter platform onto the concrete of the test yard and did not break stride. The fluid struts in the legs took the landing with a dull thump. He walked twelve paces to the buyers' stand and did not breathe any harder than he had before.

The director watched from the stand with his arms folded. He had sat on Oyama's board for eleven years. In three weeks the board would vote on who held the chair, and he wanted it. Anthony Strauss held it now, and had held it since the old man died more than three years ago. The director needed something to put in front of the board. So far he had a feeling and nothing else.

Strauss stood at the far end of the rail with a buyer's card pinned to his lapel, like the rest. Oyama made no suits. The director could not think why its chairman wanted one.

Military representatives from the UNAS, the European Union, and the Russian Federation stood along the rail with notebooks, and a port official from a fourth country stood among them. A vice president from Pellam Field Systems ran the demonstration from a lectern. He called the suit the Armored Exoskeleton Mechanical Suit, or AEMS.

“A hundred and fifty-five kilos of plate,” he said. “The linkages carry it, so the man inside feels nine. It stops a fifty-caliber round. It has no motor, so it runs on his legs.”

A Russian officer asked whether it could be copied.

“You could try. Take one apart and see how far you get.” There was no edge in it. “For buyers who want a motor, there is the battery variant.” He nodded at a second suit beside the platform. It rose off the concrete on two small jets and held at ten meters. “The Armored Exoskeleton Powered Suit, battery variant, the AEPS-BV. Forty-eight hours a charge. Our field charger, the CFCS, fills it in forty-five minutes from a case one man carries.”

The European officers wrote that down. At the end of the rail Strauss said, “Per AEMS, delivered. And a date for the first forty.” The vice president named a price and said mid-October.

“The first of October,” Strauss said.

“The first,” the vice president said, and wrote it down.

Across the yard, inside a roped square, Pellam's demonstration team was sparring in suits. Two pairs of them, gray gauntlets closing on gray forearms. One man was thrown onto his back with a bang that rattled the stand. He rose and went in again. They moved as a squad, with no wasted step, and the director had seen plant guards and hired security firms. He had never seen men trained like that. He wrote *not factory men* in the margin of his program.

**Nineteen days later.** The records clerk at the hospital in Toledo made him a copy of the death certificate once he showed his board card. It was one page. *Byron Pyrrus. Lung cancer. July 2052.* It gave the day and the hour. Under *Disposition* it said *Cremated*, and under *Next of kin* it said *Anthony Strauss, son (adopted).*

The director read it standing in the corridor. The date was right, and the cause was what the board had been told. It said the body no longer existed. He had never been told of a funeral, only of a family request for none. In forty years the old man had never mentioned a son.

That afternoon the board met in Cleveland. Strauss sat at the head of the table and read a supplier contract to its last page while eleven people waited. It took him eleven minutes. No one on the board had ever done that, except the old man.

The director had turned this over for nineteen days. Strauss was a big man with large hands and a heavy jaw, and he held himself very straight. He looked like Byron Pyrrus, if Byron had been thirty-five. He spoke the way Byron had, in short sentences, with a number and a day. When the supplier's lawyer asked whether the design would hold, Strauss said, “If it's a good design, it'll still be good on Friday.” Byron had said that for forty years, in the same flat voice, to anyone who asked.

The vote was recorded, the lawyers left, and the board went out in twos. The director stayed in his seat. Strauss capped his pen and sat with both hands flat on the table. Then he turned a steel ring on his finger, and the director watched the ring.

It was a plain band, worn thin on one side. Byron had worn it since his wife died. The director had sat across from it at every board meeting for eleven years.

The certificate was in his jacket. He could set it on the table. He could ask where the funeral had been held, and who had spoken, and where the ashes were. He started to ask, and stopped.

Strauss waited, still turning the ring. “Something you wanted to ask?”

“Fine ring,” the director said.
