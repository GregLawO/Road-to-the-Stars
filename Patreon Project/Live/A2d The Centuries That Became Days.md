# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (27)*

### *A hall on the seawall, June 2058*

She had worn the ring since January and never read inside it. The night before the wedding she carried it to the clinic pharmacy and read it under the magnifier. HANNAH, in square capitals, every letter cut clean into the steel. She had not asked Kenji what it said.

The hall on the seawall held sixty folding chairs in two blocks with an aisle between them. Her side was full by half past one. Yard crews sat in good shirts. The clinic nurses sat in a row, and the secretaries from her mother's school office sat behind them. Rafa Ibarra had come in a borrowed jacket with the sleeves too long.

The other block was empty. In April she had asked Kenji who would sit on his side. He had said he had no family to invite, and that the people he did have worked for him and could not sit as guests. He had said he understood if that was a problem. She had told him it was not.

Two men in dark suits stood against the empty side with their hands folded. They were the two who followed Kenji from room to room and never sat. She nodded to them from the doorway, as she did every morning, and they nodded back.

A man in a gray coat stood at the back door. It was June. He watched the room and not the chairs, and she did not know him.

"That's a heavy coat for June," her father said. He stood beside her in his good suit, with his steel-toed boots under the trousers. His hand kept going to his back pocket, where his own father's wrench rode with the initials scratched into the handle. "That door hinge is loose, Darling. I'll want a screwdriver after."

"After, Dad." She would ask Kenji about the coat.

Danny came in at ten to two, in a dark suit that pulled across the shoulders. He had driven from Houston that morning and had to be back at his post by eight. She put her arms around him. He stood still for it, then put one arm around her back and laid his other palm flat against the doorframe.

"Mom set your place for the reception," Hannah said, and stepped back. "Okay, so. Still gates and fences?"

"Work's work." He pressed the frame. "Hot out, though."

"I asked where you're posted, Danny. Not about the weather."

"Houston." He took his hand off the frame. "Music's about to start. Go take Dad's arm."

She let it stand. The music started, and her father put out his arm.

They walked the aisle together. Danny had gone ahead and stood in the front row on her mother's side. Ruth said, "Daniel." He answered something Hannah could not hear and kissed her mother's cheek. Then he crossed the aisle and sat alone in the first chair of the empty block, his back straight, a few feet from the two men in dark suits. Neither of them looked at him. Her mother sat down with one hand holding the other wrist, the way she did when she was frightened and did not mean to say so.

Kenji stood at the front with his back to the wall. Her father put her hand in his. The ring sat on her finger with her name against her skin.

"Thank you, Dad," Kenji said.

Her father opened his mouth and shut it. "Well," he said. "Mm." His hand went to his back pocket and stayed there through the vows.

At the reception her mother crossed the hall with a napkin in her hand and straightened Kenji's collar, which did not need it. "And who looks after you?" she asked.

"Do you need anything, Mom? I can find you a waiter."

Ruth's hands stopped on the collar. It was the first time he had called her Mom. "Tea, please," she said.

Kenji went to get it himself. Hannah followed him to the tea table and stood beside him, looking at the back door.

"Who is the man in the gray coat?"

He poured the cup before he answered. "He works for me. I asked him to come."

"He won't sit."

"He doesn't sit." Kenji set the pot down and squared it against the edge of the table. "He's the nearest thing to a friend I could put on my side."

She took the saucer from him. He had never offered her the second half of an answer before.

"I'm going to keep asking," she said.

"Good."

She carried the cup to her mother. Ruth turned to face the empty block and raised the cup. "To Kenji's side," she said. Danny lifted his glass without standing.

Hannah had a question ready. Why was her brother sitting on her husband's side of the room? She looked at Kenji. He was looking at the glass in Danny's hand. He saw her watching and did not look away.

She did not ask.

At the back door the man in the gray coat raised his glass.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (39)*

### *Galveston, 2059*

**March 2059.** The handover board in the dock office had one row with no name on it. Every other row gave a day, a time, a carrier, a driver, and a signature. This one said Thursday, 5:30 to 5:35 a.m. The carrier was filled in and the driver's box was blank.

Marchetti stood in front of it in her good heels, with the annual review on a clipboard. She had told me that morning that she did not sign for a place she had not stood in. Her pen came off its cap and went back on.

"Who signs this row?"

"The carrier signs for its driver. Its name is in the first box."

"The carrier sends a different driver every week. I read the log." She had not asked me for it. "So every Thursday, for five minutes, a man none of us has met stands at your open dock."

"Two guards and a dock hand stand with him. The droids are inside the east wing."

She looked at the dock door and then at the board. She did not ask what a droid could do from inside. "I will not sign the review without a line about that row. I will write that it is moved and still open, and the date it was moved."

"I'll move it tonight. The handover goes to Friday, and the new roster reaches the posts at six tomorrow morning." I squared the board against the wall. "Is that enough, or do you need more?"

"It is enough to sign against. I will not call the row closed." The pen stopped. "I have a second question. If someone takes this clinic, what happens?"

"Nothing leaves. A pod taken from its guards destroys its own core. The designs belong to the foundation, and the foundation is you. What would be left is a building."

"And the people in the building?"

"I don't know." I had never said that to her before.

She wrote nothing down. "Then that is the line I will want a plan for. Next year, with a date." She signed the review, wrote her own line above her name, and left the pen capped on the clipboard.

I rubbed out Thursday and wrote FRIDAY across the row. Then I read the Thursday posts once more: the gate guard, the lamp contractor's visit, the dock hand.

That night I gave the new roster to the shift leads at nine, one at a time, and made each of them say the new hour back to me. Nobody else heard it. I went home after midnight and slept four hours.

**Thursday.** At twenty past five I sat at the monitor desk with twelve screens in front of me and the earpiece in. Vance was in Duluth that week. Danny held the car park on channel four. He had held it since 2056, on my order, outside Hannah's roster. She did not know.

On screen nine Hannah stood at the staff door at the end of the night shift, one hand on her stomach, looking at the dark. She did this most mornings. She had filed three work orders for the third lamp in the car park. Each one had been closed as done.

"Third lamp's still out, sir," Danny said in my ear. "Eleven days. The gate stool is empty and the cup's gone. The roster says he's on."

"Tell the shift lead."

"Shift lead's inside. I'm holding my post." His breathing was slow. "Van on the service road. No lights. Riding low."

Screen two showed the service road. A van, no lights.

Nothing was due. I had moved the handover to Friday, and the new roster was still thirty-five minutes from the posts.

My hands lay flat on the desk.

"Stay clear, Danny."

"That van isn't ours." A pause. "She's at the staff door, sir. It's headed for the east dock."

I keyed the east wing droids to the dock. Forty paces.

"Stay clear. That's an order."

"I'm walking to it."

His breath came quick and even.

A boot on gravel. Metal on metal. A handle.

On screen nine, Hannah turned her head.

The screen went white.

Sound came second.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Helen Marchetti (60)*

### *Houston, 2059*

The annual review lay on her kitchen table with her signature on it. Above the signature, in her own hand, ran one line: *The row is moved, not closed.* Helen Marchetti had written it on Wednesday, and she was reading it again when the phone rang at ten past six on Thursday.

"Ms. Marchetti." It was Ashby, on the crystal line. He said her name the way he repeated a number, to be sure of it. "I need you to hear all of this before you ask anything."

"Go on."

"Galveston, east wing dock, five thirty-one this morning. A van, and an explosive in it." He stopped, and she heard him breathe. "Danny Shepard is dead. Hannah is alive and burned on one arm. She lost the child. Every one of the night staff is accounted for. She counted them out herself before she let anyone look at her."

Helen set the pen down and laid both hands flat on the review. She wrote the time in the margin, 6:11, and picked the pen up again.

"Thank you. I will ask my questions in order, and I would like short answers. Who signed for the driver of that van?"

"Nobody. It wasn't on the board. The Thursday row was moved to Friday yesterday."

"Then whoever sent it knew the old row and had not heard it was moved." She wrote that down. "Who was on the gate?"

"The stool was empty. The roster has a man on it, and his coffee cup was gone."

"A member?"

"No, ma'am. Hired gate staff."

"And the lamp over the car park?"

"Out eleven days. Three work orders, each one closed as done by the contractor."

She wrote *stool, lamp, driver* and drew a line under the three words. "Did any equipment fail?"

"No." The line hissed. "Every channel was clear until the moment it went white. I've been through the logs twice. Nothing drifted and nothing alarmed. People did this, and I don't say that as comfort."

"I did not take it as comfort. I took it as a fact, and I am grateful for it." She set the pen across the review. "Where is Mr. Himura?"

"With her."

**An hour later.** She was at her desk at the firm by half past seven, before the staff, with the door shut. She made the calls in the order of who could hurt the foundation first.

Whitcombe answered on the second ring, and the Louisiana had come back into her voice. "Well. I heard it on the television, Helen, and I was waiting for you to tell me it was not so."

"It is so. The foundation meets tonight at nine, here, every officer. I will not say more on this line."

"Mm. Then I will not either. I would like one thing before nine. I want to know which of the county's people will be asking about us, and I want to hear it from you and not from them."

"You will have it."

Strauss answered on the first ring. "How many of mine?"

"None of Oyama's. The building is the foundation's."

"Hm. Nine o'clock. I'll be there."

She dialed the last number on the officers' list in her desk. Himura answered himself, and behind him she heard a corridor and a door.

"Mr. Himura. As trustee I am calling the foundation to meet at nine tonight, in my conference room. Every officer. It is not a request, and I want it in the minutes that I called it."

"Yes."

"You do not object."

"No. I'll be there. I won't leave Hannah before eight."

"Then eight is when you leave." She wrote the time at the top of the first page of a new pad, and the date beneath it.

At nine that morning the attendant at the front desk of her firm called her. The foundation's line rang there, and a deputy from the county sheriff's office had just used it. He had asked the attendant for the employer of Daniel Shepard, deceased, for the record of death, and said the name Janus was on a pay stub in the dead man's wallet. He had left a number to call back. Helen told her to say the foundation would answer by noon on Friday, to give no name, and to write down the deputy's name.

She did not need the file. She had drafted Danny Shepard's seven-year contract herself, in the autumn of 2052, and the employer line named the Janus Foundation, as it did for Vance and every fighter he had brought in. Nobody outside the foundation knew she was its trustee. The deputy had spoken only to a front desk and asked for Janus, and Janus was about to go onto a county record.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (39)*

### *Houston, the same night*

A photograph of the handover board lay in the middle of Marchetti's conference table. FRIDAY was written across the row in my hand. Ashby had printed it from the dock camera that afternoon, and nobody had moved it since.

It was nine at night. I had left Hannah at eight with Ruth beside her bed and Tom in the corridor. Marchetti sat at the head of the table with a new pad. Whitcombe sat on her right and Strauss on her left, turning his ring. Ashby, Vance, and the man who owned two newspapers and a wire service filled the rest. Vance had flown in from Duluth that afternoon and still wore his gray coat. My two guards stood at the door.

Marchetti dated the pad aloud. "The trustee calls this meeting of the Janus Foundation. The county has asked the foundation for Daniel Shepard's employer, and I have told the county the foundation will answer by noon tomorrow. Every order given in this room must be given before then."

"I'll start with the stool." I squared the photograph. "The man on it was hired. He was never a member, and members don't sell their posts. He did. We stop hiring outsiders for posts. We bring in more members."

Vance spoke first. "Listen. Forty fighters hold nine posts, four to a post, around the clock. That leaves four for everything else. We filled the outer ring with hired hands because there was nobody else to put there, and I signed those rosters."

"I ordered it," I said. "We were short, and I would not wait for more members. That one is mine."

He took a slow breath through his nose. "Then give me the guard, the lamp man, and the driver, and I'll follow each one until he stops. He'll stop at somebody who was hired by somebody else. I'd rather say that now."

"Find the hands, and stop at the cutouts. Nobody pays anyone to guess past them. We recruit more men, starting tonight, and every hired post goes to a member as it fills."

"Understood."

Ashby did not look at me. "The equipment was sound. Every channel was clear until it went white. The failure was people." He was looking at the photograph, at FRIDAY in my handwriting. I had moved the row a day too late, and he meant me as well.

Marchetti uncapped her pen. "I have three demands. Every hired hand on every site is listed by name, with who engaged him, and I hold the list. No foundation money goes to new guards until I hold the staff plan in writing. And the death record. The employer is the Janus Foundation, and I am its trustee. Hannah will read that line."

"Write what is true. Janus paid him."

"Then she will ask what Janus is." Marchetti did not lower her voice. "I want it entered that I advised you to tell her what Danny did for us."

"Enter it. I'm telling her nothing yet."

"It is entered, with tonight's date."

Whitcombe pushed her glasses up onto her head. "Well. Before anyone says close the clinic: no. If we shut Galveston tomorrow, the man who sent that van reads about it with his breakfast and knows it worked. My committee can keep the inquiry away from the east wing. It cannot keep the inquiry from existing." She let a breath go by. "Today is the first time one of our own has died. I think we should say so aloud before anyone votes on anything."

"It stays open."

"Thank you, Mr. Himura."

Strauss turned the ring once. "Three offers for my patents last year, and I refused every one. Somebody didn't like that." He set both hands on the table. "Nine thousand people work on my floors, and not one of them is a member. And the droids were forty paces from that dock. Why were they inside the fence?"

"Droids stand inside private sites. Put one at a gate and every buyer asks whose it is." On the pad I wrote *the buyer behind the three offers.* We had no name for him, and it was a guess. "Your plants get guards, not droids, and the foundation pays."

"Then my plants get guards."

I looked down the table. "Handovers move inside the fence for good, and no door stays open for five minutes. Vance writes the staff plan before the funeral. Marchetti, is that enough, or do you need more?"

"It is enough to sign against." She signed.

They left in ones and twos. Marchetti took her pad. Vance stayed in the doorway with one hand at his coat.

"I told Danny to stay clear," I said.

"I read the channel log." He took a slow breath through his nose. "The staff plan goes to Marchetti before the funeral. Read it first."

Neither of us said more. I put both hands flat on the table.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Gunnery Sergeant Dominic 'Gunny' Vance*

### *Houston, the days after*

The gate roster lay on Vance's knee, open to the Thursday page. Beside the fourth line stood a small pencil tick. He had put it there three weeks ago, when he checked the stool and the man on it answered to his name.

He sat alone in the warehouse office east of the city, past midnight, with one lamp on the desk. Marchetti wanted the staff plan in her hands before Danny's funeral. Kenji had given him the hunt. Both started tonight.

He took a slow breath through his nose and began with the plan, because the plan could be written. One count of the night staff at every shift change. One exit for each wing, with a name posted at it. Nobody left in a building without a person whose job was to know they were there. He wrote it in pencil on one page and did not read it back.

The meeting came back in pieces while he wrote.

Ashby had said the failure was people. He had not said it to anyone's face, and Vance had watched Kenji look at the photograph with his own handwriting on it.

Strauss had asked why the droids were inside the fence. Vance had said nothing. He had watched those droids on the night rounds for six years, and they moved the way he moved. They had been forty paces from the dock, and they had moved right, and it had not mattered.

He stopped writing and put his hand in the pocket of the gray coat. The tags from his old squad lay at the bottom, where they always were. He knew how to hold a gate against men with guns. A stool, a lamp, and a van on the wrong day had been bought with paper he could not see, and he had signed the roster that put the man on the stool.

**Friday afternoon.** He started with the stool, because it was his. He rang the guard's number and it rang out. At one he sent two fighters to the man's flat. They called back at three.

"Bed's made," one of them said. "His duffel is gone. The mail on the mat hasn't been touched since Monday."

"Anything on the table?"

"A key. The key to the gate lock, Gunny. He left it where we'd see it."

"Bring it. Touch nothing else." He ended the call and wrote the flat's address on the page, and under it the words *lamp contractor* and *Thursday driver.* Marchetti would know who had paid the contractor. He wrote her name beside it.

Then he turned the roster on his knee until the tick was under the lamp. The man was gone. The tick stayed where he had made it.

The second phone on the desk buzzed. It was the guard at Hannah's door, a fighter he had trained himself.

"Gunny. She's awake. She hasn't asked for anyone. She asked me one thing."

"Go ahead."

"She asked why her brother was in her car park at half past five. I said I didn't know. She said to find someone who does."

Vance held the phone against his ear. He had known the answer since 2056. Kenji's order was to tell her nothing yet, and the guard was waiting on the line for what Vance would say.
