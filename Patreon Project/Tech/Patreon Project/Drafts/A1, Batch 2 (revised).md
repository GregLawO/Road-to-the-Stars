##### Batch 1 Recap
Emperor Himura signed mutual-defense protocols with Taiwanese and South Korean envoys in Kyoto while resisting calls for preemptive strikes against Eurasian-Continental Pact platforms. In lunar orbit, a Shadow Squadron cruiser under Eclipse stealth destroyed an intruding reconnaissance probe with a Twin Energy Lance pulse. Empress Kaguya oversaw Tier II longevity immersions at the Throneside Medical Bureau, where Admiral Togo emerged renewed while Grand Uncle Masamune declined treatment. In Cheyenne Mountain, UNAS analysts tracked Japanese hulls accelerating along flat vectors with zero thermal plumes. At a low-orbit sovereign shipyard, Lord Commander Tachibana thwarted a sabotage attempt on a Solaris-4A conduit, capturing an intruder who carried a valid yard clearance badge.

**A1.6. Sovereign Taiwanese Naval Liaison**   *2131 — Taipei, Taiwan*

For nine days, three Taiwanese engineers had searched the shield emitter for a seam. On the tenth day the commander came to the dry dock to watch them fail again.

The housing sat in the frigate's engineering bay, a single matte-black block as long as a man. It had no bolts, no hatches, and no diagnostic ports. Rain dripped from the dock roof onto the commander's cap and ran down the leather binder in his hand.

"We ran the tomographic scan across the casing again this morning," the senior engineer said. He set a hand flat on the metal. "The pulses scatter and nothing comes back, so we still have no image of the inside. If one emitter in the ring fails during a patrol, we cannot see why, and we cannot open it to repair it."

"What did the Imperial yard chief give you for maintenance?" the commander asked. "The attaché told the ministry these Helios-2 emitters would hold a frigate's bubble shield under fire. I want something in writing that says how."

"Only the treaty annex," the engineer said. "It says Kinki-Kyoku audits the seals and that we may not break them. We can switch the emitter on and watch it work. We cannot look at what makes it work."

He nodded to a technician at the wall panel. The bay lights dipped. A clear bubble rose out of the hull and stood over the deck, and the rain falling through the open roof beaded on its surface and slid away. The dock gauges settled on one steady number. The engineer wrote it in his notebook.

"That is every test we have," he said. "It works. We do not know why it works, or how long it will keep working."

The commander wiped the rain off his binder with his sleeve. Taiwan's navy had decades of coastal patrol behind it and no void combat hours. Beyond the dock gate, three patrol frigates rode at their moorings, each fitted with an emitter that no one on the island could repair.

That afternoon he stood with two staff lieutenants at a chart table in the yard office. The Taiwan Strait patrol grid lay under the lamps, crowded with a week of grease-pencil corrections. Across the table, Captain Taniguchi of the Imperial Navy studied it in a dark-indigo uniform. He had come at the ministry's request to rework the grid with them, and he had said little for an hour.

"Your third frigate squadron sits in the lee of the Central Mountain Range," Taniguchi said at last. His voice was quiet and polite. "From that position your southern coastal radar cannot see past the ridge. A Pact strike group coming up from the south would reach the squadron before you had a track on it."

"Our southern radar covers that approach," the commander said. He squared the corner of the chart against the table's edge.

"It covers it until the first kinetic rod lands on the radar site. Please look here." Taniguchi took a grease pencil and drew a circle forty kilometers southeast of the squadron's mark. "Our orbital pickets will pass your command center every track they hold, the moment they hold it. Kyoto has promised your ministry that in writing. A squadron inside this circle receives the warning. A squadron behind the ridge does not."

One of the lieutenants shifted his weight. "You are asking us to place three ships where Imperial pickets can see them," he said. "Those ships will be the first thing a Pact fleet meets. We do not know what their shields can survive."

"I am not asking you to take my word for it," Taniguchi said. "Check the ridge against your own radar coverage, and tell me whether I am wrong."

The commander opened his binder to the coverage plot. "Taiwan has patrolled this strait for decades, Captain, and in all that time nobody has fired on us," he said. "I have no combat hours to set against your plot. I also have no way into the emitters. Can you get me a maintenance cipher?"

"No," Taniguchi said. "I do not hold one. The seals belong to Kinki-Kyoku, and the treaty says so." He set the pencil down on the chart. "I can tell you what our pickets will see and when they will see it. I cannot tell you what is inside your emitters."

The lieutenants looked at the commander. He said nothing for a moment, then laid the coverage plot over the chart and ran his finger along the ridge line. Taniguchi waited with his hands behind his back.

The ridge blanked the southern approach exactly where Taniguchi had said. Taiwan's planners had drawn that squadron's mark without noticing it.

"The ridge is real," the commander said to the lieutenants. "Rework the third squadron's position and send me the revised grid tonight."

Taniguchi bowed slightly and walked back toward the yard office door. The lieutenants began rolling the old overlays.

"Log every picket track against our own radar for the first month," the commander told them. "If Kyoto's warnings arrive late even once, I want the ministry to see the timestamps. Check the emitter gauges at the same time, and record each reading."

Kyoto had been right about the chart. Whether it was right about the emitters, the commander could not learn until the Pact fired on them.

He moved the third squadron's mark into the circle. Taiwan's frigates would now sit where Kyoto drew them, under shields that no Taiwanese technician could open.

**A1.7. High Censor Renji Kurosawa**   *2131 — Off the Coast of Kyushu*

Every light on the research platform was burning. Nobody running an illegal rig lit it up in a midnight storm, and High Censor Renji Kurosawa watched it through the launch's streaked window for a full minute before he spoke.

The compliance launch rode the swells three miles off the rocky coast. Spray washed across its gunmetal deck. Six Ashigaru-4 automata stood locked in magnetic clamps near the bow, their dark-iron plates running with rain and their optical slits dimmed to a low ember. They had not made a sound since the launch left harbor.

"No radar pings, High Censor," the helm officer reported over the optical link. "Their servers are still sending data to an offshore relay, and the stream has not stopped since we picked it up."

"Then they are in a hurry," Kurosawa said. He set the photonic slate in his hand parallel to the edge of the rail and read the time on it. "Match speed with the lower gantry. Boarding team, clear the corridor to the server hall."

The six Ashigaru-4 released their clamps together with a single sharp clack. Their optical slits flared amber.

The launch bumped the landing stage.

The lead automaton crossed the gap and drove its shoulder into the corridor hatch.

The hatch folded inward.

Two mercenaries stood behind it with carbines. They fired. The rounds struck the automaton's directional barrier and flattened.

It covered the corridor in two strides at full sprint. Its fist drove the first man into the bulkhead. Bone cracked.

The second man ran. Another automaton took him at the knees and pinned him to the deck.

Kurosawa waited beside the hatch, off the line of the opening. His thumb pressed the worn fountain pen in his breast pocket until the boots stopped.

The corridor smelled of scorched insulation. He stepped over the broken hatch and walked to the server hall, where three processing towers stood in a makeshift bath of liquid nitrogen. Hot copper and ozone hung in the air. A terminal still showed a transmission running, and he understood the lights: the crew had been pushing out the last of the data when the launch arrived.

He wiped the edge of the terminal with a cloth before he touched it. Then he slotted his slate into the port and pulled the log.

The server had collected its data four hours before the transmission began. Someone had held it that long, and then released it in the final minutes. Kurosawa checked the third rack next. Its inspection seal had been cut and replaced with one that looked identical. He opened his pocket notebook, written in cipher, and compared the replacement against the series he carried. The last digit of its serial number was wrong.

"The unit was running a self-modifying neural matrix," he said to the Audit-Marshal beside him. His voice stayed quiet and flat. "That alone breaks the 2119 Anti-Smart-AI Edict. The seal and the four-hour hold mean someone who knows our procedures helped them."

The Audit-Marshal nodded and dragged the pinned mercenary upright. The man's lip was split. He looked at Kurosawa's coat and laughed through the blood.

"We sent everything the minute we got it," the man said. "Nobody held anything. You're chasing a delay that never happened."

Kurosawa tapped the edge of his slate once. "The log says four hours," he said. "Who told you to wait?"

"I don't know what you mean." The man spat on the deck. "You can burn the rig. It doesn't matter, because the buyer already has the raw code. Tell me something, Censor. What is it your Directorate keeps locked away that makes it hunt everyone who comes near? What are you hiding?"

Kurosawa opened his mouth to answer.

His jaw clamped shut.

His vocal cords froze. A hard pressure spread behind his eyes.

He pressed two fingers against his breastbone and stood without moving. The prisoner stared at him. The Audit-Marshal looked at the deck. The pressure held for a long breath, and then it let go.

"That is not a question I answer," Kurosawa said. His voice was steady again. "Take him to the launch. Bind the others and keep them apart."

The Audit-Marshal led the prisoner out. Two automata followed. He came back a few minutes later and stopped at the edge of the server hall.

"The offshore relay took the whole stream," he reported. "The last packet landed before we cleared the hatch. We can trace the relay by morning, but the buyer already holds the data. Shall I send the platform's registry to the Directorate office tonight?"

"Hold it," Kurosawa said. "Nothing leaves this launch until I have read it myself." He aligned the slate against the terminal's edge and turned back to the rack. Behind the drive slot he found a handwritten ledger page, folded twice. He opened it under the lamp. The last line gave the berth where the server chassis had been loaded, a domestic port.

He read the line a second time.

The replaced seal and the four-hour hold both pointed to someone who knew Directorate procedure from the inside. He did not know who. Until he did, he would give the port name to the Emperor alone.

The Audit-Marshal was still waiting at the edge of the hall. "High Censor," he said. "Where did the chassis ship from?"

Kurosawa folded the page into his breast pocket beside the pen. The Audit-Marshal waited for the port name.

Kurosawa did not give it.

**A1.8. Sovereign South Korean Defense Planner**   *2131 — Underground Command Bunker, Seoul*

Someone on the colonel's staff had set the Imperial advisor's chair at the foot of the map table, farther from the planners than any other seat in the room. The advisor had not moved it. He stood beside it with his hands flat on the table's edge, and nobody on the staff would look at him.

The war room smelled of stale coffee, damp concrete, and hot electronics. Surface diesel generators rumbled through the ceiling beams and shook dust from the acoustic baffles. Red markers ran along the Demilitarized Zone on the map table, in a line the colonel had drawn himself.

The advisor was an Imperial Legion major in a Gin-Musha-4 exosuit, his crested helmet resting on the chair beside him. He had come to Seoul under the East Asian Mutual Defense Accord. He had said very little since the briefing began.

"Our plan has not changed in years," the colonel told his staff. He tapped the markers one by one. "Hardened artillery bunkers, pre-sighted kill zones on every mountain pass, and infantry dug in behind concrete. The Pact has armor north of the Imjin River. If it crosses, it meets guns it cannot see until they fire."

"Concrete does not stop kinetic rods," the major said. His voice was practical and unhurried. "Pact platforms in orbit can drop a rod on any fixed position they have mapped, and the roof comes down on the crews inside. I am not saying your bunkers are badly built, Colonel. I am saying the Pact knows where every one of them is."

The colonel folded his arms. "We reinforced the roofs with four meters of steel and concrete."

"I would still not put a gun crew under them." The major took a data slate from his belt and raised a hologram above the table. Six grey-and-white Bōei-2 combat automata stood in a tight wedge on a ridge line. "These are the frames your ministry licensed under the treaty. Put one fireteam on the forward ridge. When the first rods fall, the automata take the strike, and they mark the Pact's firing points for your mobile guns while they do it. Your gun crews stay behind the ridge, in a position the Pact has not mapped."

He touched the slate. The simulated barrage fell on the ridge. The automata markers flickered red and went dark one by one. Behind them, the markers for the mobile guns stayed green.

A staff major stepped forward, his face flushed. "You want us to give our front line to machines," he said. "Korean soldiers have held these hills for eighty years. My father held a ridge like this one. We do not take orders from Kyoto."

"You take no orders from Kyoto," the Imperial major said. He met the staff major's eyes and did not raise his voice. "Imperial Japan keeps no garrison on your soil and commands none of your troops. We offer advice and licensed hardware. If your general staff refuses both, the accord still stands."

The staff major's jaw worked. "Then why are you at our table?" he asked. "If the choice is ours, you have no reason to tell us what the Pact will do."

"Because the numbers are not in dispute," the major said. "I would rather you hear them from me tonight than learn them when the roof falls."

The room went quiet. The colonel looked at the hologram, where the grey-and-white markers lay dark on the ridge line. He had trained under officers who would have put their own bodies on that ridge before they would put a machine there. None of the officers around him met his eye. The generators rumbled overhead. He thought of the conscripts on the forward ridge, who dug their own trenches each autumn and knew every rock on that slope by name.

"The doctrine is sound," he said at last. "I would be lying if I told you otherwise." He placed his palm flat on the map. "But I will not pull gun crews out of positions they have held for years because of a simulation. The bunker network stays. Every artillery position stays as planned."

"Colonel," the staff major said, "that is the plan we have had all along."

"It is, with one change." The colonel tapped the forward ridge. "A company of conscripts holds that ridge today. I will replace the company with one Helios-2 fireteam and see what it does when the rods fall. If the fireteam fails, I will have lost six machines. If the company fails, I will have lost the company."

The Imperial major inclined his head. "A reasonable trial, Colonel," he said.

The staff began redrawing the patrol logs. The Imperial major picked up his helmet and waited near the door, and nobody invited him to sit.

The colonel pulled the ridge roster toward him. It listed forty-one conscripts by name, with a bunker assignment penciled beside each one in a column he had not yet filled. He uncapped his pen.

He read each name aloud as he moved it from the ridge roster to the bunker roster, because he wanted to say them before the ridge belonged to the automata. The staff stopped talking. The generators rumbled overhead. When he reached the last name, he set the pen down on the table.

**A1.9. Fleet Admiral Kazuhiko Togo**   *2131 — IJN Amaterasu-no-Yaiba, Lunar Shadow*

Togo's tea had gone lukewarm in its lacquer cup, and he drank it anyway. Hot tea spilled in heavy turns, and the next turn was thirty seconds away.

He stood on the dais behind the obsidian-and-gold throne, feet planted shoulder-width apart, hands clasped behind his back. The Throne-Bridge of the 1,000-meter flagship *IJN Amaterasu-no-Yaiba* sat at the hull's center of mass, buried behind ten meters of sloped citadel armor, with no windows. Dark-lacquered bulkheads trimmed in gold leaf framed the sunken pit below him. Ceremonial racks of ancestral blades and the crimson Taira Banner flanked the throne. He had checked the exits and the bulkhead seals when he came in, as he did in every room.

In the pit, twenty-eight hereditary Himura officers sat at angled slate stations in dark-indigo uniforms, strapped into heavy magnetic harnesses. A golden-amber tactical globe three meters across floated before the dais. It showed a line task force turning in three dimensions behind the Moon, hidden in lunar shadow.

Cousin Ryunosuke Himura stood at the gallery rail with his arms crossed. He had asked for a place on the bridge for the exercise, and he had watched three passes without speaking.

"Hard turn in ten seconds," Togo said. "All stations, brace."

The task force pivoted. The deck plates trembled, and the 99.5 percent inertial dampening left a heavy tug in every harness. Officers in the pit gripped their consoles. A gunnery officer reached for his slate a full second late because the harness had pulled his arm back. Two stations spoke over each other on the optical link and then both stopped. On the globe, the formation's second squadron drifted wide of its slot.

"Sensor, you called that vector three seconds late," Togo said. He rubbed the bridge of his nose once with one knuckle. "Navigator, you corrected for the Moon's gravity on the wrong side and pushed the squadron out."

He did not raise his voice. He looked at the pit.

"The manual you trained from says to find the horizon before you act," he said. "There is no horizon in the void, and there never was. I taught that habit myself for thirty years. I still catch myself looking for it. The fault lies in the manual, not in your hands. Read your partner's heading before your own. Navigator, trim your correction by half on the next pass."

"Yes, Admiral," the navigator said. His voice shook slightly.

Togo watched the young man's hands settle on the console. The navigator had trained in the new doctrine from his first day, and still his eyes went to the harness each time the deck leaned. Togo had commanded decks with a down for thirty years. These officers had never known one, and they would need the same lesson shown to them slowly and more than once. He had weeks to give them, not years.

Ryunosuke uncrossed his arms. "That is the same mistake as the first pass," he said. "Every officer in that pit has the harness straps tight enough to bruise, and they still flinch at each turn. The Pact keeps probes on the outer picket line. Take the task force there and run the next pass against a live target. They will stop flinching when something shoots back."

"Something shooting back kills crews who still flinch," Togo said. "In this pit a mistake costs us a minute. At the picket line it costs a hull. We stay behind the Moon until three passes run clean, one after another."

"And if the Pact does not give you the time?" Ryunosuke asked. "You told the council the drills would be finished before any hull crossed the line. Nobody has told me how you will know when they are."

"I will know when the officers stop looking at their harnesses and start looking at the globe," Togo said. "They are looking at their harnesses now."

Ryunosuke adjusted his left cufflink and said nothing. His jaw was tight. Togo did not look at him. He tapped one knuckle twice on the dais rail, counting seconds, and finished the tea.

"Pass four," he said. "Begin."

On the globe, two 500-meter Frigates swung into a ninety-G formation turn. Each shed apparent mass to the Solaris-4 limit, fifty-five percent of its full weight. The deck hummed under Togo's boots. The tug in the harnesses grew heavier, and an officer near the front of the pit coughed and clenched her jaw.

"The turn is clean," Ryunosuke said quietly. "Both Frigates are on the line."

Togo watched the two silhouettes hold their slots. For the first time that morning, the officers in the pit were watching the globe.

A red chime sounded from the fire-control annex, a deep bronze bell tone.

"Crossing vectors," the sensor officer called. His voice cracked. "The lead Frigate and the trailing Frigate are closing on the same point."

Togo's hands came apart behind his back.

He stepped down into the pit.

The globe showed it. Two gold barrier planes, angled ahead of each bow, converging.

"Link officer," Togo said. "Lead Frigate. Cut thrust. Hold your heading."

The link officer's hands shook on the console. He keyed the order.

"Trailing Frigate," Togo said. "Bow down. Ninety degrees. Full thrust."

The globe flashed amber.

Two silhouettes slid toward each other.

The lead Frigate's barrier plane stopped dead.

The trailing hull dropped under it.

Eighty meters.

Sparks crawled across the optical monitor.

Togo's raised hand did not move.

**A1.10. Kinki-Kyoku Scout Captain**   *2132 — Deep Space / Sol System Perimeter*

The thermal gauge on the captain's armrest read five hours and ten minutes. The cruiser's solid-state sinks could hold the hull's heat for six, and the Pact satellites had not yet turned away.

The 750-meter Interceptor Cruiser *IJN-KNK-SP* drifted under full Eclipse stealth, a needle-nosed wedge of matte obsidian-charcoal plating with a chisel prow. Its skin temperature sat at 2.8 Kelvin. Inside the sunken command bridge, crimson and amber light fell across sixteen officers strapped into their harnesses. The golden-amber globe in the pit showed the sector as a sphere of dark space, and two bright points marked the hunter-killer satellites three hundred thousand kilometers off the starboard quarter.

"They are sweeping on a fixed pattern," the sensor officer said. He kept his voice low, though nothing out there could hear them. "Each ping passes over the hull and returns nothing, because the plating absorbs it. But the sinks are close to their limit, Captain. If they vent heat before we are clear, the satellites will see it."

"How long between sweeps?" the captain asked.

"Forty seconds when the two beams cross on the far side. They have done it three times since we arrived."

The captain kept one hand flat on his armrest and watched the gauge. Five hours and twelve minutes. He had planned the departure for the first crossing, and the first crossing had come and gone because one satellite had shifted its sweep toward the cruiser's heading. He had waited. Now he had one crossing left before the sinks forced his hand.

"Helm," he said. "Prime the fold pinch. Engage on the next crossing, and not before."

"Primed, Captain."

The two bright points swung slowly across the globe. A faint return flickered on the sensor officer's slate, and the officer went still.

"Port fin," he whispered. "A partial echo. The satellite is turning toward us."

"All stations, hold your positions," the captain said. "Nobody moves."

Sixteen officers froze in their harnesses. The helm officer's knuckles went white on his controls. On the globe, the satellite's beam crept across the hull's outline and slid off the stern.

The beams crossed on the far side.

"Now," the captain said.

The helm officer drove the pinch forward.

Space bent ahead of the prow.

A golden-amber shimmer rolled across the globe.

The hull shuddered once.

The satellites vanished from the pit, and the gauge stopped climbing.

The captain let his hand relax on the armrest. The fold drive carried the cruiser at eighteen light-years a day, and the run to the planetoid would take a little over a day. For the first hours the bridge officers spoke only in short reports. Then the sinks began to cool, the gauge fell, and the helm officer rubbed his eyes. The captain walked the pit once, checking each station, and said nothing about the satellites.

The cruiser dropped out of the fold in the shadow of a rogue planetoid twenty-five light-years from Sol. Its surface was black basalt, scarred and pitted, and its interior had been hollowed by Kinki-Kyoku crews in earlier years. The ventral drop-lock opened with a hydraulic hiss, and four courier shuttles slid out into the dark.

"Container manifest confirmed," the chief engineer reported. "Three sealed black-box containers, Kinki-Kyoku seals intact. Shuttles are loaded and ready."

The captain had signed the manifest at the dock. It listed container numbers and a seal pattern and nothing else. He had never asked what the containers held. He took the lift down to the drop-lock bay and followed the first shuttle through the planetoid's entry gallery on foot, his boots ringing on the basalt floor, until the crew set the first container down in a chamber cut deep into the rock.

Triple-interlocked neutronium blast doors closed behind each one. The seals engaged in turn. The chief engineer counted them aloud, and the captain laid his palm flat on the first door and felt it settle into its frame.

"Third container sealed," the chief engineer said. "The Proscribed Vault Sanctuary is seeded."

The sentinels went in last. A squad of Ashigaru-4 automata stepped from the shuttles in silence and crossed the vault corridor in single file. They did not speak and they did not slow. Each took its post beside a blast door and locked into a magnetic wall clamp. Their optical slits dimmed to a low ember.

The captain watched the last one settle.

"Pull the shuttles back," he said. "Seal the drop-lock and return to Sol. Lay the same stealth profile on the way home."

The return leg took another day. The cruiser dropped out of the fold in the outer Sol perimeter and slid into Eclipse stealth again, the sinks fresh and cold. One of the hunter-killer satellites passed at a distance, pinging, and returned nothing. The captain stayed on the bridge. When the cryptographic annex chimed with a low bronze bell tone, he was already walking toward it.

"A tight-beam array has intercepted a wide-band Pact military burst," the signal officer said. He held out a printed glass slate. "It decrypted on the second pass."

The captain read the transcript. It was brief, a string of orders and acknowledgments between Pact fleet commands, and it said that several task forces were moving to forward readiness stations. It named no date. It named no target. It was a mobilization, and the transcript was the only copy.

He read it again.

Kinki-Kyoku regulations sent every intercept to Kinki-Kyoku headquarters first. But the transcript concerned fleet readiness, and Fleet Admiral Togo commanded the fleet. The cruiser would reach home port in nine hours. Whoever met it at the dock would hold the transcript first, and the captain had to choose who that was. He could not send it by tight beam from this position, because a satellite lying along the beam would trace it back to the hull.

"Captain," the signal officer said. He waited by the annex console with the courier channel open. "Which office should I have the courier shuttle meet at the dock?"

The captain opened his mouth.

He looked at the slate in his hand. He did not answer.
