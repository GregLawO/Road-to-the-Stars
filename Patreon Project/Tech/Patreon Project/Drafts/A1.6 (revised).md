**A1.6. Sovereign Taiwanese Naval Liaison**   *2131 — Taipei, Taiwan*

For nine days, three Taiwanese engineers had searched the shield emitter for a seam. On the tenth day the commander came to the dry dock to watch them fail again.

The housing sat in the frigate's engineering bay, a single matte-black block as long as a man. It had no bolts, no hatches, and no diagnostic ports. Rain dripped from the dock roof onto the commander's cap and ran down the leather binder in his hand.

"We ran the tomographic scan across the casing again this morning," the senior engineer said. He set a hand flat on the metal. "The pulses scatter and nothing comes back, so we still have no image of the inside. If one emitter in the ring fails during a patrol, we cannot see why, and we cannot open it to repair it."

"What did the Imperial yard chief give you for maintenance?" the commander asked. "The attaché told the ministry these Helios-2 emitters would hold a frigate's bubble shield under fire. I want something in writing that says how."

"Only the treaty annex," the engineer said. "It says Kinki-Kyoku audits the seals and that we may not break them. We can switch the emitter on and watch it work. We cannot look at what makes it work."

He nodded to a technician at the wall panel. The bay lights dipped. A clear bubble rose out of the hull and stood over the deck, and the rain falling through the open roof beaded on its surface and slid away. The dock gauges settled on one steady number. The engineer wrote it in his notebook.

"That is every test we have," he said. "It works. We do not know why it works, or how long it will keep working."

The commander wiped the rain off his binder with his sleeve. Taiwan's navy had decades of coastal patrol behind it and no void combat hours. Beyond the dock gate, three patrol frigates rode at their moorings, each fitted with an emitter that no one on the island could repair.

That afternoon he stood with two staff lieutenants at a chart table in the yard office. The Taiwan Strait patrol grid lay under the lamps, crowded with a week of grease-pencil corrections. Across the table, Captain Taniguchi of the Imperial Navy studied it in a dark-indigo uniform. He had come at the ministry's request to rework the grid with them, and he had said little for an hour.

"Your third frigate squadron sits in the lee of the Central Mountain Range," Taniguchi said at last. His voice was quiet and polite. "From that position your southern coastal radar cannot see past the ridge. A Pact strike group coming up from the south would reach the squadron before you had a track on it."

"Our southern radar covers that approach," the commander said. He squared the corner of the chart against the table's edge.

"It covers it until the first kinetic rod lands on the radar site. Please look here." Taniguchi took a grease pencil and drew a circle forty kilometers southeast of the squadron's mark. "Our orbital pickets will pass your command center every track they hold, the moment they hold it. Kyoto has promised your ministry that in writing. A squadron inside this circle receives the warning. A squadron behind the ridge does not."

One of the lieutenants shifted his weight. "You are asking us to place three ships where Imperial pickets can see them," he said. "Those ships will be the first thing a Pact fleet meets. We do not know what their shields can survive."

"I am not asking you to take my word for it," Taniguchi said. "Check the ridge against your own radar coverage, and tell me whether I am wrong."

The commander opened his binder to the coverage plot. "Taiwan has patrolled this strait for decades, Captain, and in all that time nobody has fired on us," he said. "I have no combat hours to set against your plot. I also have no way into the emitters. Can you get me a maintenance cipher?"

"No," Taniguchi said. "I do not hold one. The seals belong to Kinki-Kyoku, and the treaty says so." He set the pencil down on the chart. "I can tell you what our pickets will see and when they will see it. I cannot tell you what is inside your emitters."

The lieutenants looked at the commander. He said nothing for a moment, then laid the coverage plot over the chart and ran his finger along the ridge line. Taniguchi waited with his hands behind his back.

The ridge blanked the southern approach exactly where Taniguchi had said. Taiwan's planners had drawn that squadron's mark without noticing it.

"The ridge is real," the commander said to the lieutenants. "Rework the third squadron's position and send me the revised grid tonight."

Taniguchi bowed slightly and walked back toward the yard office door. The lieutenants began rolling the old overlays.

Kyoto had been right about the chart. Whether it was right about the emitters, the commander could not learn until the Pact fired on them.

He moved the third squadron's mark into the circle. Taiwan's frigates would now sit where Kyoto drew them, under shields that no Taiwanese technician could open.
