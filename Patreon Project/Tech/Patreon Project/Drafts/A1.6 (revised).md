**A1.6. Sovereign Taiwanese Naval Liaison**   *2131 — Taipei, Taiwan*

For nine days, three Taiwanese engineers had searched the shield emitter for a seam. On the tenth day the commander came to the dry dock to watch them fail again.

The emitter housing sat in the frigate's engineering bay, a single matte-black block as long as a man. It had no bolts, no hatches, and no diagnostic ports. Rain dripped from the dock roof onto the commander's cap and ran down the leather binder in his hand.

"We ran the tomographic scan across the casing again this morning," the senior engineer said. He set a hand flat on the metal. "The pulses scatter and nothing comes back, so we still have no image of the inside. If one emitter in the ring fails during a patrol, we cannot see why, and we cannot open it to repair it."

"What did the Imperial yard chief give you for maintenance?" the commander asked. "The attaché told the ministry these Helios-2 emitters would hold a frigate's bubble shield under fire. I want something in writing that says how."

"Only the treaty annex," the engineer said. "It says Kinki-Kyoku audits the seals and that we may not break them. We can switch the emitter on and watch it work. We cannot look at what makes it work."

The commander wiped the rain off his binder with his sleeve. Taiwan's navy had decades of coastal patrol behind it and no void combat hours. Beyond the dock gate, three patrol frigates rode at their moorings, each fitted with an emitter that no one on the island could repair.

An Imperial officer crossed the dock from the yard office and stopped at the briefing board. Captain Taniguchi wore a dark-indigo Imperial Navy uniform. He studied the patrol chart pinned there for some time before he spoke.

"Your third frigate squadron sits in the lee of the Central Mountain Range," Taniguchi said. His voice was quiet and polite. "From that position your southern coastal radar cannot see past the ridge. A Pact strike group coming up from the south would reach the squadron before you had a track on it."

"Our southern radar covers that approach," the commander said. He squared the corner of the chart against the edge of the board.

"It covers it until the first kinetic rod lands on the radar site. Please look here." Taniguchi took the grease pencil from the board and drew a circle forty kilometers southeast of the squadron's mark. "Our orbital pickets will pass your command center every track they hold, the moment they hold it. Kyoto has promised your ministry that in writing. A squadron inside this circle receives the warning. A squadron behind the ridge does not."

The commander looked at the circle. "Taiwan has patrolled this strait for decades, Captain, and in all that time nobody has fired on us," he said. "I have no combat hours to set against your plot." He opened the binder to his own radar log. "I will check your ridge against our coverage."

"Please do," Taniguchi said. He bowed slightly and walked back toward the yard office.

The commander laid the coverage plot over the chart. The ridge blanked the southern approach exactly where Taniguchi had said. Taiwan's planners had drawn that squadron's mark without noticing it.

Kyoto had been right about the chart. Whether it was right about the emitters, he could not learn until the Pact fired on them.

He moved the third squadron's mark into the circle. Taiwan's frigates would now sit where Kyoto drew them, under shields that no Taiwanese technician could open.
