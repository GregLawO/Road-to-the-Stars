# On the Quiet Decade

"*To foreign observers watching the Western Pacific during the ten years following the Kyoto Restoration, the Empire of Japan presented an enigma of quiet self-containment. Continental ministries and Western naval directorates routinely mistook reduced steel exports and closed island slipways for economic exhaustion, assuming that a nation recovering from civil realignment possessed neither the industrial capacity nor the ambition to contest high orbit. They failed to perceive that true maritime strength is not measured by the noise of public shipyards or the rhetoric of international councils, but by the discipline with which a sovereign state builds its foundations in the dark before the storm arrives.*"

— *Annals of the Chrysanthemum Restoration*

---

**A1.1. Emperor Himura** *2130 — Kyoto-Heian Imperial Citadel, Kyoto*

A smear of dark blue ink sat across the second knuckle of Emperor Kenji Himura’s right hand. He had put it there an hour ago and had not looked at it since. The cedar worktable was cool under his forearm, and the tray of rice balls at his elbow had gone cold beside the telemetry slates.

Three heavy kinetic-rod platforms of the Eurasian-Continental Pact had moved into equatorial slots above the Marianas. Himura laid each slot against the Pacific picket schedule, one line at a time. Two sat inside the arc of the existing patrols. The third sat just outside it, and he circled that one with the ink brush.

A rod released from that slot would reach the Home Islands in under an hour. He wrote the figure beside the circle in small, neat characters and set the brush down.

A chime sounded at the door. Envoy Park of Seoul came in first, a thin man in a gray suit with a folder under his arm. The Taiwanese trade representative followed and bowed to the room before sitting. Both of them glanced at the ink on Himura’s hand and then politely at the wall.

“Ink,” Himura said. “That brush beats me every time. Sit, please. Tea is coming.”

Park waited until the cups were poured. “The defense ministry asked me to put one question to you directly,” he said. “If Moscow and Beijing close the Yellow Sea, how long before Japanese ships reach Korean waters? The minister wants a number. He does not want a promise.”

“Hours,” Himura said. “Your ports will take artillery fire on day one, because the guns are already there and we are not. I would rather say that in this room than have your minister hear it on a radio.” He turned his cup a quarter turn on the cedar. “Our treaty covers mutual maritime defense and licensed Helios-2 reactor cores. It does not cover an imperial garrison on your soil.”

“The minister did not ask for a garrison.”

“No. Somebody in your parliament will ask for one, the first bad week. The answer will still be no.”

Park’s mouth tightened, and he nodded once. The Taiwanese representative opened a leather-bound folder to a page of small print. “And if our patrol frigates take losses before your fleet arrives?” he asked. “Taipei wants to know what it risks by signing.”

“Your frigates may be sunk before we reach the strait, and I cannot prevent that,” Himura said. “What I can do is pass you every track our pickets hold, the moment we hold it. The cost to you is the first day of fighting. I won’t hide that from you.” He tapped the folder. “Lawyers wrote these protocols, so they are long. Read all of it before you sign.”

Both envoys read in silence. Park turned a page, then another, and the Taiwanese representative took out a pen and put it down again. When they picked up their pens together, the only sound in the room was paper. They signed the mutual-defense protocols, and Himura signed under them.

After the doors closed, Grand Uncle Masamune tapped his walking stick once against the floorboards. He had sat through the whole meeting in the corner, both palms flat on his knees, facing the north wall.

“You have promised two neighbors a shield before one of our own crews has fired a live shot,” Masamune said. “If Moscow tests that promise and our crews fail, those two nations will lose sons beside ours. I have watched that happen. You have not.”

“I know the crews are untested, Uncle. That is why they are not going anywhere yet.”

Cousin Ryunosuke straightened up from the gallery rail, where he had stood with his arms crossed. “They are already placing rods over our shipping lanes,” he said. “Every week we wait, they choose the hour more carefully. Push the vanguard squadrons to the outer picket line and force their probes back now. Let them see we are not afraid.”

“If we push their probes back, we fire the first shot,” Himura said. “Then every ministry in the world gets to describe it however it likes.” He slid the slate across the table so both men could see the circled slot. “Here is what we do. The patrol schedule stays as it is. Togo adds retraining drills behind the Moon before any hull crosses the picket line. If the Pact fires first, we answer with crews who have practiced the answer. I won’t spend crews to look bold.”

Ryunosuke adjusted his left cufflink and said nothing for a moment. “And if the drills take too long?” he asked.

“Then you can tell me so in front of the council, and I will listen,” Himura said. “Bring numbers.”

Ryunosuke gave a short nod. The council filed out. Masamune stayed by the worktable and looked at the untouched tray of rice balls.

“Your hands are stained and your tray is cold,” Masamune said. He reached into his sleeve and dropped three paper-wrapped dried plums into Himura’s coat pocket. “Eat those before you read another dispatch.”

Himura unwrapped one and put it in his mouth. It was sour enough to make his eyes narrow. He took a rice ball from the tray, ate half of it in one bite, and held the other half out.

Masamune shook his head. “I ate at noon,” he said. He pointed his stick at the slates. “Finish that yourself. Then read your dispatch.”

**A1.2. Cruiser Captain, Shadow Squadron (IJN-KGE-SP)** *2130 — Lunar Shadow, High Orbit*

Captain Sato turned his tea cup a quarter turn in its bracket on the arm of the command chair. The tea had gone cold hours ago. The bridge of the 750-meter cruiser lay in dim crimson light, and no window opened onto the dark outside. Every station sat in a pit below the dais, and every officer was strapped into a heavy magnetic harness.

Two katana racks stood on either side of the chair, one holding his sword and one his wakizashi. He had not touched either since the watch began. A low bass thrum came up from the Solaris-4C core through the deck plates and settled in his teeth, and the air smelled faintly of ozone and machine oil.

On the holographic globe in front of the dais, three small amber marks drifted along the terminator line six hundred kilometers off the port bow. They were unmarked reconnaissance probes. Passive sensors had found them four hours ago, and nothing aboard had pinged them since. The orders from Kyoto were to track and record. Nothing in them said to fire.

“Probe One holds its slow rotation,” the sensor chief said. Chief Mori was a broad man with a steady voice. “Probe Two and Probe Three drift without thrust. Wide-angle optical lenses, Captain. I can read the casing marks now. Cyrillic on Three, simplified Chinese on Two.”

“Log both.”

“Logged. Do we send them to Kyoto?”

“At the end of the watch, and not by transmission,” Sato said. “Any burst from them?”

“A burst every nine minutes, narrow beam, aimed at Earth. Nothing aimed at us.”

“Keep it that way.”

A single bronze tone sounded from the Quantum-Optical VI slate at the engineering station. The engineering officer read the line without turning his head. “Thermal sinks at eighty-eight percent, Captain. Eclipse stealth has run five hours and fifteen minutes. The limit on this hull is six.”

“Forty-five minutes,” Sato said.

“Forty-five minutes, sir.”

Sato checked the readout himself every ten minutes, though the engineering officer reported it more often. If the sinks filled, heat from the Solaris-4 core would reach those lenses within seconds. Moscow would learn that Japan kept hulls in lunar orbit.

“Helm, status,” he said.

Lieutenant Aoki sat at the helm console, young and thin, with a gray tint under his eyes. He had been on watch for eleven hours. “Station-keeping on passive trim, Captain. I am holding us on the terminator line.”

“Good. Mind the lunar vector. The pull changes as we drift toward the limb.”

“Yes, sir.”

The relief watch was due in an hour. Sato had thought about calling it early and had decided against it. A fresh helmsman would need ten minutes to find the trim by feel, and he did not want ten minutes of that with the sinks this full. Aoki was tired, but Aoki knew this hull.

For thirty minutes nothing changed. The tones came and went. The probes kept their slow rotation. Sato asked Mori to confirm the transmission schedule and got the same answer. Twelve minutes later he asked again and got the same answer again.

“Sinks at ninety-two percent,” the engineering officer said, and a little later, “Ninety-six.”

Nobody on the bridge spoke after that. The harnesses creaked when someone shifted. Mori’s hands lay flat on his slate, his eyes on the nine-minute clock. Sato looked at the three amber marks and thought about the six hundred kilometers between them and the cruiser. It was a comfortable distance. It would stop being comfortable the moment a lens turned.

At the helm, Aoki rubbed one eye with the heel of his hand and reached for the trim control. He corrected for Earth’s pull. He did not correct for the Moon.

The hull drifted three degrees off axis.

Sato saw it on the globe first. The bow marker slid toward Probe One’s amber cone, slowly, then faster.

“Helm, check your vector.”

Aoki’s eyes went to his console. He gripped the yoke and said nothing.

A red line crossed the plot.

The bow entered Probe One’s lens cone.

Sato’s hand closed on the chair arm. “Mori. Is that probe awake?”

“Optical lock, Captain. Burst spooling.”

The burst would carry the cruiser’s position to Earth. Sato stepped down into the helm pit. “Cancel the drift. Two seconds. Now.”

The hull shuddered once. It stopped sliding.

“Lock holds,” Mori said. “Burst at four seconds.”

“Open the forward Phase-Gated Firing Aperture.”

“Aperture open.”

“Twin Energy Lance. One pulse. Probe One.”

The faceted barrier parted for half a second.

Two white-gold beams crossed the dark. There was no recoil. Probe One split in half, and its pieces drifted apart in silence.

“Probe One is dead. No burst sent,” Mori said.

Sato looked at the plot. Probe Two had stopped rotating.

Its sensor mast was turning toward the cruiser.

**A1.3. Empress Kaguya Himura** *2131 — Throneside Medical Bureau, Kyoto*

Steam rose from six immersion tanks along the stone floor of the subterranean medical hall. Empress Kaguya Himura stood beside the diagnostic rail in a dark silk kimono and watched the vital traces move across the slate. The tanks had run for fourteen days. In a few minutes the first lid would lift.

The hall smelled of hot minerals and cold stone. Amber light ran along the vaulted ceiling in long strips, and each tank glowed a faint silver from within. Five of them held a sleeping figure. The fourth had been filled and warmed for Grand Uncle Masamune, and it stood open and empty.

Lady Shizuka stood at Kaguya’s shoulder with a paper clipboard and tapped a wooden pencil against it twice. She had not slept, and her reading glasses sat low on her nose. She pushed them up before she spoke.

“Medical roster first,” Shizuka said. “Five officers in Tier II immersion this cycle, five waking this afternoon, no complications on the traces. Foundry roster second. The Kii shifts are on double rotation, and the night crews have double rations. Their supervisors want to know when the double rotation ends.”

“What did you tell them?”

“That I will tell them when I know.” She tapped the pencil twice more. “I do not know yet.”

“Then that was the right answer.” Kaguya checked the nearest slate herself. The traces had settled into their final pattern, and the dead skin was already loosening in the fluid. “And the broth?”

“Hot. Two kettles in the alcove and a third in reserve. The mineral baths are drawn behind the east screen. They will want to eat before they bathe. They always do.”

“Let them eat. They wake starved, and they wake cold.”

Shizuka looked at her over the rim of her glasses. “You were here before the second bell. When did you last sleep?”

“When the traces settled yesterday.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is a true one.” Kaguya laid a wool robe along the heated mat beside the first tank and straightened a sleeve that did not need straightening. “I will sleep after they have eaten.”

Shizuka opened her mouth, closed it, and wrote something on the clipboard that Kaguya did not ask to read.

Grand Uncle Masamune stood near the far alcove, leaning on his walking stick. He had come down the stone steps an hour ago and had not sat once. He looked at the open tank beside him, and at the silver fluid moving faintly in it.

“The physicians have set the cycle for your bone density, Uncle,” Kaguya said, crossing to him. “Kenji wants you at the council table for another twenty years.”

Masamune rubbed his thumb across his knuckles. The stone floor was cold, and his joints ached in it. “I watched this house lose everything once, Kaguya. Now it has a throne and a fleet, and the work I had left is done. An old man should step aside in his own time. Give that tank to a captain who still has wars to fight.”

“Kenji will ask me why I did not insist.”

“Tell him I refused you, and that you asked twice.”

Kaguya went still. She smoothed her sleeve once. “I asked once,” she said. “I will not ask again.”

“Good.” One corner of his mouth moved. “You pour a better cup of tea than Kenji does. Do it for me later.”

She did not answer. Behind her, the first tank’s seals hissed open, and she turned back to the rail.

Fleet Admiral Togo stepped out onto the heated mats first, two senior legion commanders behind him. The lines around their eyes were gone, and their skin looked new. Their hands shook slightly as the attendants draped the wool robes over their shoulders. Two more senior officers followed from the further tanks, moving slowly, as if the floor might tilt.

None of them spoke. Togo looked at the steam, then at the floor, then at his own hands. He turned them over once, palms up. The commander behind him sat down on the mat as if his legs had been taken away, and an attendant knelt beside him with a cup of water.

Kaguya took the first ceramic bowl from Shizuka’s tray. The broth was hot enough to send up a thread of steam. She crossed the mat and placed the bowl directly into Togo’s hands, and she held them steady around it until his fingers closed.

“Drink slowly,” she said. “It will want to come back up.”

Togo drank. A little color came into his face. “Thank you, Majesty,” he said. His voice came out hoarse, and he cleared his throat and tried to rise and bow.

“Stay where you are, Admiral.”

He stayed. She served the two legion commanders next, then the two officers from the further tanks, and the attendants last. Shizuka followed with the tray and the second kettle, and ladled for any bowl that emptied. One of the commanders ate two bowls without lifting his eyes. The other held his bowl in both hands for a long time before he drank, and Kaguya knelt and waited beside him until he did.

When the officers had eaten, the attendants led them behind the east screen to the baths. Water ran there, and low voices began, then a short laugh. Kaguya listened to it for a moment. Then she walked over to the alcove table.

Masamune had settled onto the low stool beside it. He did not look up. She lifted the iron kettle and poured his cup of bitter green tea, and she set it in front of him with both hands.

Then she poured her own.

“Sit,” Masamune said.

She sat.

**A1.4. UNAS Orbital Intelligence Analyst** *2131 — NORAD-Orbital Command, Cheyenne Mountain*

Analyst Miller had checked the timestamps three times before he put the prints on the table. The first check was meant to find a clock error, and it found none. The second found none either. By the third he had stopped hoping and started writing the numbers down.

He laid three high-resolution optical prints across the steel briefing table. Behind him, the tracking wall of Cheyenne Mountain glowed in the half dark, its orbital tracks and satellite arcs drawn in pale green. The room smelled of stale coffee and warm electronics. Somewhere above the ceiling, a ventilation fan ran with a steady, low tick.

General Williams came in with a hardcopy folder, set it down, and leaned over the prints with his knuckles pressed to the table’s edge. He read the first one without speaking. He read the second one longer.

“Run me through the timestamps,” Williams said. “Maui radar lost the target at zero-two-fourteen.”

“Yes, sir.” Miller touched the first print. His finger was not quite steady, and he pulled it back. “The Japanese hull dropped off active radar behind the lunar limb. Forty seconds later, our optical telescope in Chile caught a brief sun-glint four thousand kilometers off its projected orbit.”

Williams tapped the second print once. “Four thousand kilometers in forty seconds is a hundred kilometers a second.”

“Yes, sir.”

“A chemical burn at that rate would light up every infrared satellite in the hemisphere.” Williams did not say it as a question. He waited.

“There was no infrared bloom, General. No exhaust plume, no debris trail, no drive signature of any kind.” Miller’s voice went flatter as he went on, the way it did when he was afraid of being wrong. “I had the Chile observatory check its clock against two independent time sources, and I asked Maui to rerun the radar returns. Both came back the same.”

Williams straightened. He drew a sheet of hardcopy telemetry from his folder and read it through. Then he folded it neatly in half and laid it on the table.

“Could the glint belong to a second hull already sitting at that position? Or could the radar have dropped the first one early, and we are matching two unrelated tracks?”

“We checked both, sir.” Miller turned the third print toward him. “The glint matches the first object’s brightness and its rotation rate, so it is the same hull. And the lunar limb was clean at that hour, so the radar had a clear view when it lost the target.”

“A clear view, and then nothing.”

“Yes, sir. I have the data. I do not have an explanation.”

Williams looked at him for a moment. His brown eyes were steady, and the lines at their corners looked deeper under the pale light. “That is the right answer, Miller,” he said. “I would take the data over an explanation any day. An explanation is something people want to hear, and data is what the telescope saw.”

He nodded once and let a breath out through his teeth.

“I do not want a theory in the file,” he went on. “I want every frame, and I want them raw. Nobody enhances anything until I have looked at it myself.”

“Understood, General.”

Williams drew a diplomatic wire from the corner of his folder and laid it beside the prints. “Kyoto’s trade envoy was in Washington on Tuesday,” he said. “He asked for an extension on their industrial steel quotas. His government says its domestic shipyards are shut.”

Miller looked at the wire, then at the prints. “Their commercial yards in Yokohama have been quiet, sir. I checked the yard data this morning. Nothing there matches this track.”

“Nothing there matches it,” Williams agreed. “And the wire came in the same week as the prints. We will keep them in the same file and see what the next sweeps give us.”

He crossed to the wall chart. A red grease pencil hung beside it on a string, and he took it down and drew a wide circle around the Earth-Moon Lagrange points. The pencil squeaked faintly on the plastic.

“Task every deep-space optical array we have to continuous lunar sweep,” Williams said. “I want twenty-four-hour coverage. Chile, Maui, and anything the Navy can lend us.”

“Chile and Maui are both booked for survey work through the end of the quarter,” Miller said. “I will need new schedules from both observatories.”

“Then write new schedules.” Williams let the pencil swing back on its string. “And write them tonight. Wider coverage, Miller. Not smarter guesses. We map what is there, and we decide what to call it afterward.”

“Yes, sir.”

Williams collected his folder and went to the door. He stopped there with one hand on the frame. For a moment he looked back at the wall chart, at the red circle around a patch of empty dark.

“If one of those frames shows you something you cannot account for, wake me,” he said. “Do not wait for morning.”

“I will, General.”

The door closed on its slow hydraulic arm.

Miller stayed at the table. The tracking wall hummed behind him, and the three prints lay in front of him under the dim light. He pulled the second one toward himself and put his finger on the velocity curve. It rose in a straight line from the lunar limb and ended in a straight stop. Nothing he knew of could do that.

**A1.5. Lord Commander Haruka Tachibana** *2131 — Low-Orbit Sovereign Shipyard*

Three men stood at the Solaris-4A conduit junction with no tether clips on their belts. The high gantries required them.

Haruka Tachibana saw the bare belts from twenty meters away. She had walked Lady Shizuka’s inspection route for forty minutes in her Gin-Musha exosuit, along a zero-gravity catwalk that ran between the structural ribs of an unfinished cruiser. Her magnetic boots clicked softly on the deck plates. Scaffolding crossed overhead in long diagonal shadows, and loose tether lines hung from the rails.

The cruiser’s hull stood open to the dark on three sides, its armor plates only half hung. Welding arcs flickered along the keel in short blue flashes. Each flash threw a hard-edged shadow across the catwalk that jumped once and was gone. Crews in gray coveralls worked in pairs, one holding a plate against the frame while the other sealed the seam. The air smelled of hot metal and cold coolant, and a low thrum from the yard’s forge core ran up through the soles of her boots.

Two meters ahead, Shizuka drifted along the hull with a handheld scanner, checking weld seals one plate at a time. Her reading glasses had slid down her nose, and she pushed them up with a knuckle each time she moved on. “This seam is within tolerance,” she said, without looking back. “The next one needs another pass, and that costs a full shift. The Kii crews are behind already, and I have no one to give them.” She tapped her pencil against the scanner twice. “I do not intend to lose another day on one weld.”

“Understood,” Tachibana said.

She was listening. She was also reading the catwalk. Every worker they had passed wore gloves and a tether clip, and every worker had nodded to Shizuka without breaking the rhythm of the shift. Tachibana watched their hands more than their faces. Hands showed what a person intended to do next, and these hands had been steady and busy.

Ahead, a gap opened in the scaffolding beside the power conduit junction. Three men in gray yard coveralls stood in it. Their hands were bare, and none of them was working.

Tachibana’s thumb brushed the grip of her mono-blade once.

“Lady Shizuka,” she said. “Behind the bulkhead. Now.”

Shizuka looked at her. She looked past her at the junction, and she moved at once, one hand on the rail, without asking why.

Tachibana stepped forward and put herself between the bulkhead and the gap. She did not draw the blade. She kept her hands low and open, and she measured the distance again. Twelve meters. The nearest man had a canvas tool bag hanging from his shoulder, and the bag hung too heavy for tools.

“Yard security,” she called. “Show me your tether clips.”

The nearest man did not answer. He glanced at his companions. One of them shook his head by a finger’s width, and the first man’s hand went into the bag.

Tachibana exhaled through her nose.

She kicked off the catwalk.

Her Solaris-4M Ember core flared gold.

Twelve meters closed in half a second.

The first man dragged a shaped thermal charge out of the bag. It was a gray slab the size of a book, with a dark timer panel on its face.

The second man raised a suppressed pistol.

He fired once.

The slug struck her faceted barrier and skipped off into the gantry steel with a bright, flat ring.

She struck his wrist with the flat of her gauntlet. The pistol spun away.

She drove him back against the bulkhead and pinned him there.

The man with the charge pushed off toward the conduit junction. He held the slab out in front of him with both hands.

Tachibana turned in the zero gravity. Her knee drove into his ribs.

His breath left him in one hard gasp. The charge slipped out of his fingers.

She caught it with her free hand.

The timer panel was dark. They had not set it.

The third man dropped his wrench. It floated away, turning slowly. He raised both hands, palms out, and did not move.

She held the second man to the bulkhead with her forearm and put one armored boot on the third man’s chest. Neither of them moved. The only sounds on the catwalk were her own breathing and the faint ticking of cooling metal. Down the gantry, the welding arcs had gone dark one by one, and a few workers had turned to look.

She took two magnetic restraint cuffs from her belt and locked the second and third men’s wrists to the rail. Then she checked the first man, who had gone limp against the deck plate, and cuffed him as well. Three men were now fixed to the rail where they could not push off.

“Lady Shizuka,” she said. “It is clear.”

Shizuka came back along the rail. She did not say anything about the charge. She looked once at the conduit junction, then took the cover plate from its mount and set it firmly back over the housing. Her pencil was clamped between her teeth.

“Dark panel,” Shizuka said around the pencil. She took it out. “They were going to arm it at the junction. Another half minute and they would have been there.”

“Yes.”

Tachibana slid the charge into the belt pouch at her hip and sealed it. Then she turned to the third man. He was young, thin, and sweating despite the cold of the catwalk. A yard identification badge hung from a clip at his chest pocket.

“Who gave you this badge?” she asked.

The young man looked at the rail and said nothing. His lips were pressed white, and his eyes stayed on the cuff around his wrist. Tachibana waited three seconds. Then she tore the badge free and held it out to Shizuka.

Shizuka passed the handheld scanner over it. The slate chimed once.

“Valid sovereign shipyard clearance,” she said. Her voice stayed level. She tapped the pencil against the slate. “Issued from this yard’s own office, and signed. These three never had a gate to climb. Someone inside opened the airlock for them.”

“How many people can sign a badge?”

“Everyone in the clearance office. The yard issues new badges every week, for every new hire and every visiting crew.” Shizuka pushed her glasses up the bridge of her nose. “The badge tells us it was issued here. It does not tell us by whom.”

Tachibana looked along the gantry. Workers stood at their stations in both directions, most of them watching her now, a few already looking away. Every one of them was within reach of an alarm panel. One of them might have signed the badges. Any of them might have seen who did.

She had two ways to deal with the yard, and she could use only one.

She could sound the alarm. The whole yard would stop, and the workers would line up for search, and the person who had issued the badges would hear it with the rest of them.

Or she could seal the yard in silence. Every worker would stay where they stood, no one would be told why, and she and Shizuka would work down the line until they found the one hand that had signed.

Tachibana had to choose one of them now.
