# Victor Manswell: Character Profile

First POV is A3.9 (2068). Items marked **(decision)** are invented gap-fills. He is a different man before and after the night he signs his company away in 2065; only the earlier man matters for A3.

## 1. Basic Identity & Demographics
- **Name & nicknames.** Victor Manswell. Employees say "Mr. Manswell," lenders "Victor," the press "the Captain" (which he pretends to dislike). His mother called him "Vic." **(decision)**
- **Age.** Born 2015; fifty when he announces the expedition. **(decision)**
- **Appearance.** Broad shoulders, silver at the temples, a good suit rumpled by midday, a heavy watch he keeps checking. Old rope scars across both palms, shown when he wants a room to trust him. His father's brass tide watch, no longer keeping good time, is worn under his sleeve. **(decision)**
- **Voice & mannerisms.** South-coast English smoothed by boardrooms, slipping back when excited; passable French and Portuguese. **(decision)** Generous, flowing, a little too long; reasoning then the number; calls people "friend" when he wants them on his side. Takes off his watch and sets it face down; when it is bad he leaves it there. Warm, self-deprecating humor about money or himself; never jokes about settlers on the ground.

## 2. Psychology & Internal World
- **Core goal.** Immediate: one more lender in the room before the meeting ends. Overall: be remembered as the man who took people to the stars.
- **Needs vs. wants.** He wants to own something outright again (not said aloud). He needs to stop treating debt as something to charm his way out of.
- **Internal conflict.** The settlers against his own freedom. He keeps any promise to someone who depends on him even when the price goes up, and will never abandon people he has put on the ground.
- **Flaws.** Assumes anyone who refuses an offer is holding out for a better one; reads silence as negotiation when it is often fear or principle; believes generosity creates loyalty when it only creates debt. Honesty 3 of 5: rounds optimism upward, technically true statements that omit the hard part.
- **Fears & secrets.** Fear: the phone call that ends the deal; a lender speaking very politely. Secret: he keeps his original company share certificate, now worthless, in a drawer and looks at his signature after big announcements.
- **Worldview.** Loves the age; the ships and cures are the biggest market in history.

## 3. Backstory & History
- **Upbringing & family.** Southampton, a family that worked container docks and repaired ships, always one bad season from losing everything. **(decision)**
- **Defining moment.** As a boy he watched a bank take his father's crane the morning after a lender changed his mind: the crane hauled out on a flatbed at dawn, his father in the gate with his hat in his hand. Whoever holds a debt controls the person who owes it.
- **Education & occupation.** Founder and dealmaker; second-best, marine surveyor, his father's job. **(decision)** In 2065 rich on paper but borrowed far beyond what he can repay.
- **Prior relationships.** Owes his father, whose crane he is still trying to buy back.

## 4. Story Role & Stakes
- **Function.** Main-cast POV (A3.9); the public face of the expedition.
- **External conflict.** The lenders who withdraw in coordinated fashion in 2065, and the firm that rescues him and does not explain itself. He does not know who is behind it.
- **Stakes.** The settlers; his company; his father's legacy. Finding the guard ships he was refused were never meant to protect them would break him.
- **Arc.** When offered money he asks who and what it costs, then says yes if he has no choice; when challenged in public he stays friendly and answers with a specific number or offer.
- **Rules for the text.** No fool-only or villain-only scenes, no simple gratitude to a rescuer, no sudden bravery not built up earlier. He knows only what he has seen, read, or been told.

## 5. Dialogue Voice Guide **(decision; built from this profile; sample lines are mine, not canon)**

**1. Vocabulary & Word Choice.**
- South-coast English under boardroom polish. "Right, so," "Look, friend," "Tell you what," "call it," "give or take." When excited: "mate," "proper." A rare French or Portuguese phrase.
- He rounds in his favor: "It's a bit tight, yeah. Not tight tight." Self-deprecating humor about money.
- Samples: "The number's six percent — call it seven, I'll confirm it Monday." / "Course you can, friend. It's on the ring somewhere, give me a second, or it's with the spare set, I'd have to check." / "Tell you what, I'll walk you over myself and we'll sort it on the way."

**2. Sentence Rhythm & Punctuation.**
- Long and flowing, three clauses, ending on a number or an offer. A dash for interrupting himself is his alone. He asks "Fair enough?" without waiting for an answer.
- Excited, he talks faster and the vowels go back to the docks.
- Reply length: three sentences or more as a rule. He goes short only when a lender is being polite ("Of course. Understood.").

**3. Nervous Habits & Tells.**
- Challenged in public, he gets friendlier and more specific.
- Told no, he answers with a higher offer. A lender speaking very politely makes him short, the "friend" disappears, and the watch goes face down.
- He says only true things and leaves out the hard part.

**4. Dialogue Traps (What to Avoid).**
- No "trust me," no plain "thank you, you saved me," no sudden brave speech, no admitting the debt in plain words.
- No "I'm a simple sailor."
