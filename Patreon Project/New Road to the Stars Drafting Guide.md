### Story, Chapter, and Scene Outlining and Drafting Guide: The Himura Empire

---

Part 1: The Big Picture (Story Level)

Lock down these core anchor points on a single page before planning any scenes. They act as guardrails so your story never drifts off course.

1. The Core Premise: On the eve of a global war, the Himura Imperial Heir, the Empire's Prime Minister, a senior vassal lord, and a hesitant foreign ally navigate a treacherous battlefield of tiered technology and shifting loyalties, while the enigmatic Himura Emperor orchestrates a grand strategy using secret proprietary technology that neither his allies nor his enemies fully comprehend.
2. The Non-POV Rule: see the Factions and Characters and Ranks files for who carries POV.
3. The Emperor as Primary Driver:
- Overt Will: Major military, industrial, and diplomatic moves come directly from the Emperor's decrees. POV characters react to his orders and carry out his strategy.
- Covert Actions: The Emperor runs secret operations behind the scenes. Reveal these covert moves only when they directly intersect with a POV character's scene, such as a covert unit rescuing an ally at a critical moment or an enemy realizing their supply lines were secretly sabotaged. Never explain his full plan upfront.
4. POV and tech tiers: see the Factions file (section 5) and the Characters and Ranks file (tech tier key).
5. The Major Story Milestones:
- The Beginning (Mobilization): Five years after the Imperial Restoration War. The rebuilding has hardly begun but another inevitable war is once again on the horizon. The Empire is retraining loyalists on Gen 2 variants and shipping Gen 1 gear to nervous foreign allies.
- The Midpoint (The Shift): The global coalition and traitorous vassals launch a massive offensive, thinking they know the limits of Himura tech based on stolen Gen 1 variants. Just when they push past the Gen 2 vassal lines in the Japanese home islands, the Emperor personally deploys Solaris Tech in battle, shattering their calculations and turning the tide.
- The Ending (The Climax): Leading his unified alliance into the heart of the coalition's war machine, the Emperor defeats the traitor clans and breaks the superpowers' will to fight, cementing a permanent new world order.

---

Part 2: Chapter Planning (Chapter Level)

Keep the chapter planning lightweight so it stays flexible while writing.

1. Keep Chapters Tight: Focus each chapter on 1 to 3 connected scenes. Give each chapter a clear shift in momentum from beginning to end.
2. Outline 3 to 5 Chapters Ahead: Do not outline the entire book in detail on day one. Outline the next 3 to 5 chapters (for example, A1 through A3) right before you draft them so your immediate path is clear without locking you into rigid plans.
3. Connect Chapters with Domino Logic: The ending of one chapter must either directly or indirectly affect the opening of the next chapter.

---

Part 3: The Scene Engine (Scene Level)

The story moves forward like a pendulum swinging back and forth between Action Scenes and Reaction Scenes. You never have to guess which scene comes next because the previous scene decides for you.

1. When to Use Each Scene Type:
- Use an Action Scene when a character has a clear plan and is actively trying to make something happen right now.
- Use a Reaction Scene when a character just suffered a setback, received shocking news, or landed a major win, and needs to process the fallout before making their next move.
2. Action Scene Checklist (External Push):
- POV and Setting: Who is the viewpoint character, and where are they?
- Goal: What does the POV character want right now?
- Conflict: What person, rule, or obstacle gets in their way?
- Outcome: Answer the goal using one of these paths:
  - For a Setback ("No, and furthermore..."): They fail, and a brand-new problem makes things worse.
  - For a Major Win ("Yes, but..."): They succeed and land a big hit, but the victory draws a bigger threat, reveals a new target, or comes with a heavy cost.
  - Clean Victory ("Yes"): Save a clean, uncomplicated win for the very end of the book or a major arc finale.
3. Reaction Scene Checklist (Internal Pause):
- POV and Setting: Who is the viewpoint character, and where are they?
- Reaction: How does the character emotionally process the loss, shock, or big victory?
- Dilemma: What are their remaining choices now that the board has changed?
- Decision: What new concrete goal do they commit to? This new goal immediately becomes the Goal of your next Action Scene.

---

Part 4: Simple Drafting Rules (Preventing Plot Holes and Lore Dumps)  
Follow these four habits while turning your scene checklists into actual chapters.

1. The "Therefore or But" Rule: Look at any two scenes in a row. You should be able to say, "Scene 1 happens, therefore Scene 2 happens," or "Scene 1 happens, but Scene 2 happens." If you find yourself saying "and then," cut or merge the scene.
2. Use Lore as Conflict Fuel: Never write a scene where characters just sit around explaining history or technology. Only bring up a detail from your lore book when it creates a physical obstacle, a political dilemma, or a tactical advantage in the current scene.
3. The Need-to-Know Rule: Explain a weapon, rank, or historical grudge at the exact moment a character needs it to survive or make a decision. Keep explanations brief and focused on what the object or rule does right now.
4. Track Your Promises: Keep a short running list of every new secret, sabotaged machine, or undercover agent you introduce. Check off each item as you resolve it before reaching the ending.
