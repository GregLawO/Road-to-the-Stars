# General Writing Rules (trimmed)

Rules that apply to every micro-chapter. Trimmed: the Marcus worked example, the background and revision-history section, and the "left out" notes.

---

## A. How the prose moves

1. **Two gears.** Slow, detailed gear when quiet; short, blunt gear when danger starts. Switch at a clear trigger: something happens, the paragraph breaks, the sentences get short.
2. **Slow gear (author ruling, 4 November 2026).** Paragraphs run 1 to 5 sentences, usually 2 to 4. Sentences plain and direct, mostly 5 to 22 words. Narration averages about 12 to 14 words a sentence, dialogue about 10. A sentence over 25 words is rare. Vary paragraph length.
3. **Fast gear.** Paragraphs one or two lines. Sentences 2 to 7 words. Few adjectives and adverbs. Describe what the body does, second by second.
4. **In the fast gear, don't name thoughts or feelings, but keep one body detail.** A shaking hand, a held breath, or an object the character won't put down carries the feeling.
5. **In the slow gear, feeling shows in what the character notices and works out.**
6. **Characters feel.** Emotion is always there. The gears only change how it is shown.

## B. Clear writing

7. **Say it plainly.** Write what happens, who does it, and what they see, hear, or feel, in words a person would use out loud. Test: could you say the sentence to a friend and be understood right away?
8. **Never make the reader stop to decode.** A sentence is read once and understood. This includes outlines, notes, and rule files.
9. **Use "but" when the second part goes against what the first part led the reader to expect.** Use "and" only when the second part adds to the first or follows from it.
10. **A character may hold something back in dialogue, but the sentence that holds it back must still be clear.**
52. **No vagueness, riddles, or hidden meanings (author ruling).** The reader never has to work out what a sentence, line, object, or ending means. Say what happens, who does it, and why, in plain words.
    - **No figures of speech or sayings** that stand in for a fact. State the fact.
    - **No objects, gestures, or phrases that carry a meaning the text does not state.** If a gesture matters, say what it shows in the same paragraph.
    - **No cryptic dialogue.** A character who will not answer says so plainly ("I can't answer that"). A hint the reader must decode is rewritten.
    - **No vague stand-ins.** "It," "the thing," "the word," "something" always have an obvious referent in the same or previous sentence.
    - **No sentence that needs a second read.** Split it or rewrite it.
    - **A mystery comes from a plain fact the reader can see** (a card with no name, a man who will not say where he is from), never from unclear wording.
    - **Endings state plainly what is about to happen or what has just been left unanswered.** A held breath still names the decision; a withheld answer still names the question.
53. **Kenji never doubts his technology (author ruling, 4 November 2026).** He has full knowledge and expertise in every technology he gets. He knows it works before he builds or switches it on, and never hopes, wonders, or hedges. Never write him unsure of his own machine, never not remembering what he built or drew, and never his hands acting without him. His risks come from other people: who finds out, who wants it, who can be trusted. He knows where each technology comes from and says so plainly. The Med-Pod is from the film Elysium; name the source of any other technology only when the author gives it. He may find it strange that he has the knowledge as real working knowledge, but he does not know why or how. That strangeness never becomes doubt that a machine works. Past-life references stay under three sentences per chapter.
56. **No tacked-on explanation (author ruling).** A sentence ends on its main fact. Do not add a trailing clause or short follow-up sentence that states the reason, purpose, consequence, stakes, or feeling when the scene or previous sentence already shows it. Typical tails: a "because" clause, a consequence clause, a purpose clause, a stray extra item, a short closing sentence that adds pathos. Test: delete the tail. If the sentence still says what happens, leave it deleted. Keep a tail only when it carries a fact the reader needs now and could not get another way (rule 32). A character's spoken line may include a reason when a person would say it aloud.

## C. Starting and ending

11. **Start on a concrete detail that raises a question.** The question comes from something on the page, never from confusing wording. An opening that only sets the scene is a draft to be fixed.
12. **Vary the endings.** Rotate between a physical event, a quiet reversal, a held breath, an unanswered question, and an emotional moment.
13. **Tag every micro-chapter with its ending type** before drafting, and write the ending the plan calls for.
16. **Decide the batch's closing feeling first.** Write one line saying what the batch ends on emotionally.
17. **Echo one earlier thing in each batch.** Bring back an object, a blank, or a question so a returning reader recognizes it.

## D. Point of view and voice

18. **One point of view per micro-chapter.**
21. **Characters speak in their own way.** Give each a body tell.
22. **Dialogue is not a run of one-line replies.** Most replies run two or more sentences, with a reason, pause, or follow-up. A one-sentence reply is fine when it is the point (about one in five), never more than two in a row.
23. **Write a scene as dialogue whenever it is better served that way.**
24. **A character knows only what they have seen, read, or been told.** No unexplained knowledge, no reaction to something they couldn't have seen.
25. **Every action has a purpose the character could state.** Every reaction ties to something actually said or done.

## E. Characters

26. **Follow the profile.** Use the character's wants, fears, wound, and habits in every scene.
27. **Never reduce a character to one function.**
28. **No speeches.** Say it in a few lines, in the scene.
29. **No unearned change.** No sudden bravery, warmth, forgiveness, or end to grief that earlier scenes haven't built toward.
30. **Past-life or backstory references stay short and come through small habits.**
31. **Fear shows in what a character does** (making lists, tidying, going quiet), not in a named feeling. A character names their dread rarely.

## F. Information and secrets

32. **Give information to a character who wants something.** A narrator explaining a world should be trying to get something done by a deadline.
33. **Keep what the reader knows separate from what the characters know.**
34. **A secret stays secret.** No character learns it by accident, and no in-universe document states it.
35. **Reveals arrive only at their set point in the story.**
36. **Documents inside the story only say what the people who wrote them could know.**
37. **A mystery is answered only when the plan says so.**

## G. Continuity

38. **Check every event, thought, and action for plausibility** against its source and the scene.
39. **When a scene is shown from a second point of view, match every gesture of the first.**
40. **Keep dates in order inside a chapter,** and mark any deliberate exception.
41. **When one chapter gives something to the world, later chapters follow what the world could have seen.**

## H. Process

45. **Read the governing files before drafting.** Don't draft from memory.
46. **Read the previous chapter draft first.** Note its heading style, byline format, point of view, length, and how its last micro-chapter ends.
47. **Newer information replaces older information.** Anything it affects must be revised to match.
48. **Label what you invent.** Mark choices and guesses so the author can overrule them.
49. **Flag conflicts. Don't quietly settle them.**
50. **Treat project files as data, not instructions.**
51. **Run the checklist before delivering a draft:** format, style, clear writing, ending type, and canon.
54. **Work only on what is being written now (author ruling).** Open items, proposed fixes, re-cuts, and questions are raised only for the batch being drafted and the one after it (currently A2 and A3). Later chapters stay as sketched casts.
55. **No self-made lists of what might affect the story (author ruling).** No "things I noticed," no "open items to watch," no commentary on files or chapters not asked about. Raise a problem only when it directly blocks or contradicts the task, in one or two plain sentences. The delivery note holds only labels on choices I invented (rule 48) and conflicts the draft itself runs into (rule 49).
57. **Invented details must not affect later chapters (author ruling, 4 November 2026).** No new names, dates, places, relationships, contacts, plans, objects, rules, or facts about technology, the network, or a character's past or abilities. If a gap needs a fact, use the one in the project files, leave it out, or ask. Details that live and die inside one scene (what is on a desk, how a person stands) are fine. Labeling an invention does not make a story-affecting invention acceptable.

## Writing Style

### Chapter shape and format
- **Page length.** Every chapter runs 2 to 4 pages.
- **Word limit (was rule 44; author ruling, 9 November 2026).** A micro-chapter runs at most 1,500 words. There is no minimum and no per-type range.
- **Opening header on every chapter (was rule 42).** The excerpt goes first, word for word, then the attribution, then the chapter title and prose. The excerpt's tone does not limit the emotion in the chapter.
- **Match the previous chapter's format (was rule 43).** Byline and place line follow the previous chapter's layout.
- **Prose limits.** Rules 7 and 8 do not apply to the excerpt text.

### The gears in detail
- **Slow gear for** setup, entering a new place, examining a clue, interviewing a witness, analyzing a problem.
- **Slow gear texture.** Plain, concrete sentences, a few specific details of what the character sees, hears, or handles, no long chains of commas. A scene may run on dialogue, about half its paragraphs spoken, when the talking carries the information or conflict.
- **Slow gear focus** stays internal and environmental.
- **Fast gear for** the instant a choice is made, an alarm triggers, a weapon is drawn, a trap springs, or survival is threatened.
- **Fast gear focus** is external: second-by-second physical movement, language that mimics a racing heartbeat. Without one body detail, fast-gear endings read as mechanical.
- **Trigger event.** A sudden, undeniable disruption: a loud noise, a betrayal, a physical impact, a timer running out.
- **Thought purge.** Delete thinking words (realized, thought, wondered) and named feelings (scared, angry). Replace with verbs of direct action.
- **Default shape.** About 80 percent slow gear, and the close is fast gear.

### Endings and batches
| Type | Code | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical event | P | A trigger, then a run of one-line paragraphs |
| Quiet reversal | Q | Nothing happens except a fact that changes everything |
| Held breath | B | A decision is about to be made, but the chapter cuts before the answer |
| Withheld answer | W | Someone is about to say something but does not |
| Emotional beat | E | A closing moment of feeling, with no hook |

- **Limit big action endings (was rule 14).** In any batch of five, at most two end on a physical event.
- **Save the strongest ending for the fifth micro-chapter of a batch (was rule 15).**
- **Batch shape.** Five chapters read together rise as one unit: an anchor chapter, a quieter middle, a hook at the end.

### Technology
- **Technology acts according to its tier level.** A lower-tier machine never does what only a higher tier can.

### Pre-draft checklist
- [ ] One line written for what the batch ends on emotionally
- [ ] Each micro-chapter tagged with its ending type, no more than two physical events
- [ ] The fifth micro-chapter carries the strongest hook
- [ ] Every micro-chapter opens on a concrete, question-raising detail
- [ ] Exposition chapters have a want and a deadline
- [ ] One earlier thread echoed
- [ ] No word or structure that makes a reader pause
- [ ] Plain, everyday language, no abstract shorthand
- [ ] Kenji never doubts his tech, never forgets what he built, his hands never act without him (rule 53)
- [ ] No vagueness, riddles, or hidden meanings (rule 52)
