**Chapter 4 — Establishing Akatsuki**

Gloria Castellanos had been in the loft for eleven minutes without sitting down.

She was a small, precise woman in a gray coat who had walked up the stairs, looked at the clean bench, the gantry frame, and the cluster humming against the back wall, and asked to see my notebooks before she asked to see the machine. I'd handed her the project log and the bound lab book. She was reading standing up, turning pages with one finger and saying nothing.

"Your dates are consistent," she said at last.

"I write them down."

"Most inventors write them down after they've been told to." She turned back to the first page. "These are dated from the spring. The scanner architecture, the fusion approach, the registration method, the treatment path planning. All in your handwriting, all in sequence, with the dead ends included." She glanced up. "You left the dead ends in."

"I wanted to know what I'd already tried."

"That is going to be very useful to me, and very annoying to anyone who tries to challenge us." She closed the book. "Mr. Himura, Walter Pruitt called me and said a client's son had built something I should see. I told him I do pharmaceutical and device filings for companies with lawyers, not for twenty-year-olds in garages. He said it wasn't a garage."

"It's a loft over a garage."

"That's the part he left out." She finally looked at the machine. "Show me."

I ran the demonstration without commentary, because people believed what they watched more than what they were told. Gloria stood beside the screen with her arms folded while the carriage stepped and settled through its thirteen-minute pass over a fresh phantom. I'd cut it myself this time, with no surprises in it. Dolores's test had shown me what a blind run looked like, and I wasn't going to waste Gloria's time on a repeat. When the head laid its blue bead down the seam, she leaned in until her nose was almost over the phantom.

"That's a surrogate gel," I said. "Not medicine. This unit proves the scanning and the path planning. It doesn't prove anything clinical."

"Good. Keep saying that, to everyone, in those words." She straightened. "Now tell me what you want protected."

"Everything."

"That's not an answer. That's a wish." She said it mildly. "Patents are a trade. You get exclusive rights for a fixed number of years, and in return the invention is published for anyone to read. Patent the wrong thing and you hand a competitor a manual. Patent nothing and anyone who can lawfully work out how your product functions is free to copy it. So we choose."

She pulled the lab book back open and started marking it with a pencil from her coat pocket. "The sensor-fusion architecture. Three modalities combined into one tissue map with an honest uncertainty estimate. That's novel, and it's visible in the product the moment a competitor opens the case. Patent it. The registration method for aligning the scanner to the treatment head, the same. The treatment-path planning is probably patentable too, but I want to read your notes before I commit."

"And the software framework? The system that runs the calculations and keeps the knowledge base."

"What does that do for the customer?"

"Nothing directly. It's how I design the machines. It's why I can iterate in days instead of months."

"Then that's a trade secret, and you protect it by never letting it leave the building." She tapped the page. "A patent would publish exactly how you work. Same for the calibration procedures and the validation data. If it isn't visible in the product, it stays in the vault."

That matched what I'd have chosen. "What do we file first?"

"Provisional applications, within the week. They're cheap and they establish your filing date. You then have twelve months to file the nonprovisionals, and that's when the real money goes out." She named the figure for the first round. It was less than I'd feared and more than I wanted. "The full filings will cost several times that. Plan for it."

"I'll plan for it."

"I'd also like to file on the treatment head, today, before the planning is finished."

"The head is a surrogate. It lays dyed silicone. I can't tell you yet whether the real version will look anything like it."

"Which is exactly why I'd file now. A provisional lets you describe what you have and amend what you learn." She looked at me over the lab book. "You're reluctant. Why?"

"Because every filing is money, and I'd be spending it on a design I expect to change."

"That's a fair concern, and I'll tell you what I tell everyone." She didn't soften it. "If you wait and someone files first on something close, the cost of being wrong isn't a provisional's fee. It's the claim. I'd rather you overspend by a few thousand than underprotect the one thing you own that can't be rebuilt in a weekend."

I weighed it against a budget I had not yet seen written down. "Fusion and registration this week. The treatment head after you've read the notes, and I'll hold you to the date."

"I'll take that, though I want it on record that I advised otherwise." She wrote something down. "One more thing." She reached into her bag and set a short stack of paper on the bench. "Before anyone else sets foot in this loft, you sign nothing, say nothing, and show nothing without these. A nondisclosure agreement for visitors. An invention-assignment agreement for anyone who works for you, so that what they build here belongs to the company and not to them. And before you hire a single person, the company has to exist, because right now you personally own everything, and you personally can be sued for everything."

"I'd already put 'business structure' on my list."

"Then put it at the top."

---

Walter Pruitt had done my mother's taxes for twenty-two years, and he wasn't about to be impressed by me now.

"Sit," he said, "and tell me what you think a corporation is."

His office was a converted dentist's suite above a tax-preparation franchise, with a waiting room full of other people's shoeboxes of receipts. I sat. "A legal person that owns the assets and takes the liability so I don't have to."

"That's adequate. Understand what it won't do." He folded his hands on a desk that had been cleared for the occasion. "If you sign a loan personally, it's personal. If you're negligent, it's personal. Courts pierce corporations that are run like wallets. So the company's money stays in the company's account, and you never again pay a project bill from your own pocket without writing it down."

"I've been paying for everything from savings since the spring."

"I know. I've seen the receipts you sent. We'll sort out which of those the company reimburses and which become your contribution. Some of it will be neither, and I'll have to make an unpopular call." He ticked the decisions off on thick fingers. "One. A corporation, not an LLC. Gloria agrees. Investors will expect one, and converting later costs more than starting right. Two. The shop stays out of it completely. Himura Auto remains its own entity, with its own insurance, books, and customers. If the new company fails, the shop does not fail with it."

"That's how I wanted it."

"Then you'll like three less. The company needs a place to work, and the only place you have is the loft, which belongs to the shop. So the company leases it, at a fair rent, in writing. Not free. If the shop gives the company a free room, a creditor will call it a disguised transfer."

I read the lease template. "And the equipment?"

"You contribute what you bought for the project. Bill of sale for the workstations, the blades, the arm, the printers, the clean-bench kit. Not the mill and the lathe."

"The company will need the mill. The whole gantry came off it."

"Then the company pays a use fee. They're your mother's machines, they belong to the shop, and the shop is not a donor." He peered at me. "I know it's inconvenient. But if the company owns the mill and the company fails, the mill goes to its creditors. If the shop owns it, it's protected. I'll draft a fee schedule that's fair to both sides, and the company will pay it on time."

"A use fee to myself."

"To the shop, which pays your living. Same pocket, different books, and the difference is the whole point." He didn't smile. "Now. You'll also put in cash. How much?"

I'd worked it out the night before. "Forty thousand from savings. That leaves my cushion intact and a little over."

"It's a large share of what you have." He put his pen down, which I had learned was a bad sign. "I'm going to say this once. If this takes longer than nine months, which it will, you'll be tempted to go back to savings. Don't. The first time a founder reaches past the number he chose, the number stops meaning anything."

"I won't."

"You say that now." He picked the pen back up. "Then you hold one hundred percent of the stock. We reserve a block for employees and advisers so you're not diluting yourself in a hurry. And a vesting schedule on yourself, because the first thing any serious investor asks is whether the founder can walk away with the company in his pocket."

"I'd rather propose it myself. Four years, with a short cliff."

That made him stop and look at me properly. "Why?"

"If I'm going to ask other people to accept vesting, I should accept it first."

"Hm." He wrote it down with a faint dry satisfaction. "All right. The name. The state needs one by Friday."

I'd been turning it over since Gloria's visit. "Akatsuki."

He tried it twice. "Japanese?"

"It means dawn. Daybreak."

"Optimistic, for a company that hasn't sold anything yet," Pruitt said, in the tone of a man who'd spent his life with ledgers. "It's not taken. I checked three spellings this morning." He wrote it on the form. "Akatsuki Technologies, Incorporated. Medical devices to begin with?"

"Medical technology. I'd like the charter written broadly enough that we're not amending it in two years."

"Then I'll tell the attorney to say 'technology.' You can be as vague as you like in the charter and as specific as you like in the business plan." He looked up. "Which brings us to the question you haven't asked me yet."

"How long the money lasts."

He pulled a legal pad toward him and did the arithmetic out loud, which I appreciated, because it let me check him. Patent filings, formation, the insurance policy he insisted on, parts, a part-time bookkeeper, the rent, the use fee, and a salary or two if I was serious about hiring. Against that, the forty thousand and whatever I could safely pull from the repair business's monthly surplus without starving it.

"Nine months," he said. "Ten if you hire no one and the transducer vendor doesn't raise prices. And that's before the full patent filings and before you pay a physician for a day of her time."

"Then I need money before month nine."

"You need it sooner. Raising money takes four to six months, and no one gives you any if you walk in with three weeks of runway." He capped his pen. "I know a man. He'll want to look at you. Don't take that personally."

Leonard Brandt owned four dealerships and a small portfolio of companies that had made him richer than his dealerships had, and he came to the loft in a wool overcoat and a flat cap, looking like a man who'd decided to be pleasantly surprised.

He didn't touch the machine. He asked to watch it twice and asked me to explain nothing. After the second run, he sat down on one of the shop stools, looked at the phantom for a while, and said, "I've been pitched a lot of things by young men. Usually the machine doesn't work."

"It works on a phantom."

"Walter said you'd say that. He said you'd volunteer what doesn't work, which is either honesty or tactics, and he claims you're not smart enough yet for tactics. I think he's wrong, but let's talk."

We talked at the steel table with the lamp pulled over it. His offer was three hundred and fifty thousand dollars for forty percent of the company, a board seat, and a first look at any future financing. It was a serious offer from a serious man. It would have covered the full patent filings, a lab lease, and two years of salary.

"It's generous," I said. "And I'm not going to take it."

He didn't blink. "Tell me why."

"At forty percent I'm giving up a large share of a company that doesn't have a validated product yet. If the evaluation goes well, that share is worth several times more. If it goes badly, I'd have taken your money to learn something I could have learned for a quarter of the cost. I'd rather get the evaluation done on a smaller budget and come back with data."

"Data costs money."

"Which is what the grant is for." I'd read the program's guidelines twice. The state ran a biomedical seed program funding early device work in amounts that wouldn't buy a car but would buy an evaluation protocol and a rented bench. "A hundred and fifty thousand over a year, no equity. It's competitive, and I have a physician writing a support letter."

"I'll push on that." He leaned back. "A grant isn't a plan. It's a lottery ticket with paperwork. Applications like yours get declined for reasons that have nothing to do with merit: a reviewer who doesn't like the category, a budget line that doesn't fit their form. You'd be betting the company's first year on a committee."

"The nine months don't depend on it."

"They depend on you not hiring, and you've told me you intend to." He tilted his head. "And the decision takes four months, by your own account. By then you'll have spent nearly half your runway waiting for a letter. Have you priced what happens to a twenty-year-old's nerve at month six, with a payroll and no answer?"

I hadn't, not honestly, and I could tell from his face that he knew it. "If the grant fails, I freeze hiring and cut the second position. The cushion stays out of it, and so does the shop. If the numbers get too thin, I come to you."

"And I'd be negotiating with you in month seven, with your back against a wall, instead of today, with your back against nothing." He let that sit. "I'm not saying you're wrong to refuse me. At forty percent, with that board seat, I'd have wanted a say in whether the scanner ever touched a patient, and I'd have wanted it for the wrong reasons. But I'm telling you the price of refusing is real, and you've priced it lower than it is."

"That's fair," I said. It didn't feel good.

"It's what you're paying me for, whether you take the money or not." He laughed, short and genuine, and shook his head. "Walter told me you'd be stubborn. He didn't say you'd be right about it, and I'm still not sure you are." He pulled out a card and set it on the table. "I'm not insulted. I'll tell you what I tell all of them: the first money is the most expensive money, and you've worked that out sooner than most. If you have data, call me before anyone else. If you don't have data and you're running out of room, call me anyway. I'll pay more for a company that said no to me, but I'd rather not be the last person you try."

"That seems backwards."

"It's the only way I've found to tell the good ones." He stood and buttoned the overcoat. "Don't let it kill anyone."

"That's why it's not going near a person yet."

"Keep it that way."

After he left, I stood with his card in my hand. Then I took the pad and wrote the funding plan out as a sequence. Savings and shop surplus for the first nine months. The grant application in by the end of the month. A hospital partner and a clinical evaluation as the milestone. Outside equity only after the evaluation, at a valuation I could defend. A smaller loan against the company's equipment as a fallback, if the grant failed. I numbered each step and wrote the date I wanted it done, and next to the grant decision I added a line of my own that I hadn't planned to write: _Month 6 check. Decide then, not later._

Then I went downstairs and fixed a transmission, because that was how the rent got paid.

Mr. Okafor's neighbor was named Naomi Reyes, and she was an emergency physician at the county hospital, and she'd given me exactly forty-five minutes on a Sunday afternoon.

She spent the first ten of them not talking. She was a tall woman in her early forties, still in scrubs under a jacket because she'd come straight from a shift, and she moved around the clean bench the way I imagined she moved around a trauma bay: looking at everything, touching nothing, asking short questions.

"What does it do when it can't see something?"

"It says so. There's a confidence value on every region. Anything below a threshold gets flagged and not interpreted."

"And who sets the threshold?"

"Right now, me."

"That's the wrong answer." She said it without heat. "Not unreasonable. Wrong, as in you shouldn't be the one who decides it. A clinician sets that, and a regulator checks it. Write that down."

I wrote it down.

She watched a full scan and the treatment pass. When the head finished, she lifted the phantom out herself and flexed the seam, and I thought of Dolores doing the same thing. Her expression gave nothing away. "This is a bench demo of one function on a block of silicone."

"Yes."

"And you're excited about the closure."

"It's the part that looks like a product."

"It's the part I'm least interested in." She set the phantom down. "Closing a clean laceration is the easy thing. In my department the closure is rarely the hard part. The hard part is knowing what's under it, and whether the wound is clean in the first place. Dirt, a tendon nicked, a foreign body, a deeper bleed. If your machine seals a contaminated wound, you haven't saved a nurse forty minutes. You've hidden an infection under a tidy line. I'd put your effort into the scanner and treat the closure as a research toy until someone has shown me it can't make things worse."

I hadn't expected that. I'd been proud of the blue bead. "The scanner was my priority anyway."

"Then say that with more conviction, because your demo says the opposite." She turned to the oximetry module. "You mentioned this one isn't calibrated."

"Uncalibrated. Gray cards prove the sensor sees light. They don't prove it measures blood. It's flagged in the log and in the software, and the output is disabled until it's been calibrated against proper references."

She looked at me for a moment. "Most people who build something like this hide the part that doesn't work until I find it myself."

"You'd find it in the first five minutes."

"I would." The corner of her mouth moved, barely. "I'll tell you what I see, and you tell me whether I'm wrong. Every ER in this country is overcrowded. The scarcest thing we have is not drugs and not beds. It's attention. Somebody with a gunshot wound, somebody with a sprained ankle, and somebody who looks fine but has a bleed nobody's found, all in one room, and three of us. If a machine could scan the quiet ones and flag what we'd miss, that changes my night."

"That's the idea."

"The idea is cheap. Evidence isn't." She faced me. "I won't sign anything that says this works. I don't know that it works. What I'll do is tell you what I'd need to see before I'd let it near a patient, review your evaluation protocol, and write a letter for your grant that describes what I actually saw today and nothing more. I'll want to be paid for my time, at a rate that doesn't make me your employee. If I think you're moving too fast, I'll say so, loudly, and if you don't listen, I'll walk."

"Agreed on the independence. On the rate, I'd like to see what you have in mind."

She raised an eyebrow, and named a figure. It was higher than I'd budgeted. I said so.

"Then you budgeted wrong. I'm giving up shifts to be here, and there are other hospitals that would pay me more to review less." She waited.

I did the arithmetic. "That's a quarter more than the number Walter gave me for outside consultants."

"Then Walter gave you a number for someone who bills by the page." She didn't move. "My time is the thing you can't automate. If that bothers you, find someone cheaper. I'll understand."

"I don't want cheaper. I want to afford it." I thought about it. "A retainer for a fixed number of hours a month, with additional hours billed as used. Capped until the grant decision."

"Cap it at what?"

I named a number. She countered. We settled on a figure between them, closer to hers. It came out of a budget that was already thin, and I wrote it in the column with the others.

"Good," she said. "You negotiated. That's the first thing you've done that I believe completely." She picked up her bag. "I'll draft the protocol outline this week. Have your attorney send me whatever I'm supposed to sign. And Mr. Himura, I want to see your safety design. Not the screen. The wiring."

I took her to the enclosure and opened the back. She traced the hardwired chain with a finger, from the door switch to the relay, and asked me twice whether any software could override it. Twice, I said no.

"Good," she said. "Hold on to that. It's what a review board will ask first."

Marcus Hale's résumé was two pages, and the second page had a gap in it.

He sat across from me at the steel table the following Thursday, a lean man of about thirty-nine with a patient face and a canvas bag he kept by his foot. He'd spent eight years designing control electronics for a medical-device manufacturer, and four months ago the company had closed the division. He told me this before I could ask.

"I'm not going to pretend I'm not looking for stability," he said. "I have a mortgage and a daughter. A startup is the opposite of stability. So I'll be direct about what I'd need."

"Please be."

"A salary. A real one, not a founder's promise. Health insurance, which a company this size will find irritating. Written terms that say what I own and what I don't." He glanced at the machine. "And I need to be able to say no."

"To what?"

"To you. On safety." He said it steadily. "I've seen what happens when the person who owns the company is also the person who signs off on its interlocks. Somebody has a deadline and a shortcut gets reasoned into existence. I'd want to review and approve the safety design on anything that touches a patient. Not advise. Approve. Your word wouldn't override mine."

I'd been about to offer something like it. It was better to hear him ask. "Dr. Reyes asked to see the wiring too. I'd rather have two people checking it than one. You'd have the authority."

"That was easier than I expected."

"I'm not interested in hiring someone who agrees with me." I pulled the offer out of the folder. Pruitt had drafted it, Gloria had reviewed it, and I'd put the numbers in myself. "Salary is a little under what you'd get at an established company. I can't match that yet, and I won't pretend I can. In exchange, you get stock options vesting over four years, from the pool Walter set aside, and a title that means something. Lead engineer."

He read it, slowly, all the way to the end, including the invention-assignment clause. "This is fair," he said.

"It's tight."

"It's fair and tight." He tapped the page. "The salary is eleven percent below what I'd ask elsewhere. I'd want it at six. And the vesting should start with a three-month cliff, not six. If you're going to insist on safety, I'll be working hard in the first six months and I'd like that to count."

I'd already worked out where I could move. "Six percent below, and a three-month cliff. Walter will say that's short, and he'll have a point. But if we can't tell by month three whether this works for both of us, a longer cliff wouldn't help either of us."

"Fair." He thought for exactly as long as it took to be sure. "And I want the safety-approval clause in the agreement itself, not in an email."

"It goes in the agreement."

"Then done."

We shook on it, and then he asked if he could see the safety circuit before he signed, which struck me as the most sensible request anyone had made all month. I opened the enclosure. He crouched in front of it with a flashlight in his teeth and went through the chain a wire at a time, and it took twenty minutes.

"It's good," he said finally, around the flashlight. "The relay's rated higher than it needs to be. The logic is what it should be. You've got an unlabeled terminal block on the left side that I'd want tagged before anyone else touches it." He took the light out of his mouth. "And the emergency stop cable is too long. Someone could trip on it. I'd shorten it and put a strain relief on it."

"I put it on a long cable so I could carry it."

"You can carry a shorter one. Please do." He stood, and his eyes went to the scanner carriage. "One more thing, and you won't like it. The scan routine stops between measurements to keep the motor noise off the sensor. Good. But the patient-side surfaces of that carriage are printed plastic with layer seams. You can't disinfect a seam. Any reviewer who has handled a hospital device will fail it on sight, and no amount of software fixes that."

I hadn't thought about the seams once. "That's a redesign."

"It's a redesign you'd rather find now than in a review." He shrugged. "I'm not saying stop. I'm saying put it on the list."

I wrote both items down, and a third.

By the first week of December, the corporate charter was filed, the provisional applications for the fusion architecture and the registration method were lodged, the lease was signed with me on both sides, and the grant application had gone out on the last permitted day. I stood in the loft at five in the evening with two people, a whiteboard, and a folder of invoices that had arrived before the company had a desk to put them on.

Marcus had been an employee for six days. He'd already taken apart the safety chain, relabeled the terminal block, and shortened my cable. He'd also found two places in the motor-driver wiring where I'd skipped a strain relief, and had said mildly that he assumed I'd intended to fix them. I had. Dr. Reyes sat on a stool by the clean bench with a mug of the shop's terrible coffee, which she'd tasted once and drunk anyway out of professional stubbornness. She had come with the first draft of an evaluation protocol, eleven pages long, and I'd found three things in it I didn't know how to do.

"Item four," she said. "You can't evaluate against a reference instrument you don't have."

"I know. I'm working on a rental."

"Work faster. Without it, the oximetry channel stays off, and so does anything that depends on it."

"Then the schedule slips," Marcus said from the bench. "Reliability runs on the scanner, a carriage redesign, and optics calibration, in parallel, with a team of two."

"Three. Ines starts Tuesday."

"Ines doesn't solder."

"Ines keeps the money straight, which is what keeps us alive."

Marcus shrugged, conceding it without conceding the point, and went back to his meter.

Downstairs, Dolores was arguing with a vendor about a pallet, and the sound of it came up through the floor. Pruitt's invoice sat on my desk, beside the first rent payment, due on the fifteenth, and the shop's use-fee schedule, clipped to the top of the folder. The insurance binder had arrived that morning with a premium notice stapled to it. Everything Akatsuki did now cost money in a column I hadn't had to keep a month ago.

Ines had left a neat folder on the shop counter and a note telling me the new company's account needed another signature. She'd also flagged three receipts from the spring with a question mark beside each. They were purchases I'd made personally for the project, before the company existed, and she wanted to know whether they were the company's debt, my contribution, or nobody's. I looked at them for a long time. Two I could document. The third was a transducer cable I'd paid cash for and couldn't prove, and the company wasn't going to reimburse me for it. It was a small sum, and it was the first time paperwork had told me that something I'd spent didn't count.

I looked at the whiteboard. I'd erased the old list and written a new one with Marcus and Dr. Reyes arguing over each line, which had improved it.

_1. Reliability testing: Marcus. Target: one thousand scans, logged faults._

_2. Independent safety review: Marcus and outside reviewer._

_3. Carriage redesign for cleanable surfaces: Marcus. No printed seams on patient-side faces._

_4. Evaluation protocol: Dr. Reyes reviewing. Still to do: secure a hospital partner, prepare review board submission._

_5. Oximetry calibration: need reference materials and instrument._

_6. Nonprovisional patent applications: Gloria. Twelve-month deadline. Treatment-head provisional: due on her read of the notes._

_7. Grant decision: four months. Month 6 check._

_8. A real lab._

The last line stood alone under the others. Everything above it could happen in the loft, more or less. That one couldn't.

"Where do you want to be in a year?" Marcus asked, capping his marker.

"In a building with a sterile room and a door a regulator would respect. Running the evaluation. Maybe six people."

"Six." He considered it. "That's about right. Any more and you'd lose track of what everyone's doing."

"Any fewer and I'd still be the one doing it."

Dr. Reyes smiled into her coffee and said nothing.

When they'd gone, I stayed behind and turned off the lights one bank at a time until only the lamp over the clean bench was lit. On the wall by the stairs, next to the lockable door I'd hung in the fall, there was a small brass plate that Pruitt had insisted I order, because a company that received mail needed a place to receive it. It was engraved with the name and a registered address.

I read it once. _Akatsuki Technologies, Inc._

Three months ago I'd been a man with a mill, a lathe, and a good idea, and nobody to answer to but the bank. Now there was a charter, a lease, an insurance policy, an adviser who could walk, an engineer who could say no, and a bookkeeper with a note on the counter. If the grant failed, three people would feel it before I did. The machine on the bench was the same machine. Everything around it had changed.

I turned off the lamp and went downstairs to see about the pallet.
