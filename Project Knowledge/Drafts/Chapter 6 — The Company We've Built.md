**Chapter 6 — The Company We've Built**

The Executive Assistant woke me at 5:52, eight minutes before my alarm, which meant something had moved.

"The Chief of Staff has revised today's schedule," it said from the ceiling. "Three changes. She asked that you read them before leaving the residence."

"Read them to me."

"The Callisto review moves from 0800 to 1300, to align with the settlement's shift change. The afternoon engineering block is cut from three hours to ninety minutes. The 1900 working dinner is cancelled."

I sat up. "Cancelled by whom?"

"By the Chief of Staff. She gave a reason. It is flagged for delivery in person."

That was the tell. Helena Vasquez didn't leave reasons in writing when she expected me to argue with them.

I got up and went downstairs. The house was still my mother's house, if you ignored the second story, the rebuilt garage, and the foundation work that had cost more than the original lot. The kitchen I'd left alone. The stove still warmed itself when I opened the refrigerator, and the cast-iron pan still sat on its camp burner beside it, out of principle. I made coffee the slow way, in the pot she'd used. Somewhere in the last few years I'd stopped noticing that I needed to be careful with the old ceramic mugs. I'd cracked two before I learned to hold things the way other people did, and replaced them with identical ones from a restoration shop. A bruise I'd earned on a loading dock Tuesday was already gone from my forearm. Dr. Sen would want to talk about that.

Which, I suspected, was the reason in person.

The car was at the curb at 6:30, and so was Helena, standing beside it with a tablet under her arm and the expression of someone who'd rehearsed. She was in her late forties, ran my schedule like a hospital administrator, which she had been before I hired her, and had a talent for being right in a flat voice that made it difficult to resent her.

"You moved my dinner," I said.

"I cancelled your dinner." She got in the back beside me. The driver, Okonkwo, pulled out without comment. He'd learned years ago which conversations were none of his business, and which to listen to anyway. "The delegation from the procurement office is an hour of pleasantries and a signature. Sofia can host. She knows the program and she likes them."

"They asked for me."

"They asked for the founder. Sofia will tell them you send regrets." She turned the tablet toward me. "Dr. Sen has requested your annual review three times. You have declined twice. It's now eleven weeks overdue, and the last scan flagged a bone-density figure that he'd like to look at again."

"It was within tolerance."

"Within the tolerance he set for you, which he'd like to revisit." She didn't raise her voice. "You've been up before six for nineteen days running. Your enhancements recover you from a lot, and I don't know what they recover you from when you stop letting them."

"I'm fine."

"Probably. I'd like it confirmed by someone who isn't you." She waited until we were through the residential gate. "I'm not asking permission. The dinner's cancelled and the appointment is at seven. I'm telling you so you can be annoyed in advance, and it'll be done before you finish being annoyed."

"That's your job?"

"That's the part of my job you hired me for." She handed me the tablet. "Read the briefing on the way in. You've got eleven minutes."

I read it. She'd cut it to a page and a half, which was how I knew she'd spent the morning on it. Somewhere past the second paragraph I noticed I wasn't annoyed anymore, and that was annoying in a different way.

---

Akatsuki's campus began where the old industrial park used to end. Eleven years ago it had been a few rented units and a chalk outline on a concrete floor. Now the gate read the car's credentials and opened before Okonkwo had finished braking, and the buildings ran back toward the hills in a long gradual spread of low halls, glass, and logistics lanes. A convoy of cargo droids, squat and four-wheeled, crossed the access road in front of us in single file, each carrying a sealed pallet. They stopped for us without being asked. Then they stopped again when a human technician in a yellow vest stepped into the lane to inspect something on the last one's chassis, and the whole line waited with the patience of machines that cost nothing to keep idle.

"Eleven-forty yesterday," Okonkwo said. "Same spot. Same vest."

"Is that a problem?"

"No, sir. It's the only time I see anyone walk."

The operations center was on the second floor of the central hall, a long room with a window onto the production floor and a wall of status boards that nobody needed to read. The systems managed themselves. The people in the room were there because something had come up the chain.

Ibrahim Nasser was waiting at the end of the room with his sleeves rolled and a cup of tea he'd forgotten about. He ran operations. He'd come to us from a shipping conglomerate nine years ago with a reputation for refusing to take a machine's word for something he could check, and he'd earned the title of the first human who'd ever made me redo a forecast. "We've got a conflict," he said. "Two of them, really, and they're not the same conflict."

He pulled it up on the wall. A lot of precision actuator sets, four hundred in all, had arrived from a supplier in Lyon two days late and eleven percent short of the certified count. Two orders were drawing on that lot. One was an installation schedule for a regional hospital network, treatment pods for four sites with surgical teams booked around the dates. The other was a set of stabilized sensor gimbals for a defense customer, with a delivery clause attached to a penalty.

"Logistics Intelligence recommends the defense order," Nasser said. "The penalty exposure is real, and the gimbals ship first because they're simpler to assemble. Medical Operations Intelligence recommends the hospitals, because the clinical schedule is the harder constraint to rebuild. Both recommendations are correct for their objectives. Neither one is allowed to weigh the other's."

"And the delegated limit?"

"The exposure on the contract clears it. And the other side of the ledger is patient scheduling, which neither of them is authorized to trade against money." He turned the wall to the four hospital sites. "They sent it up. That's the system working."

I studied the two columns for a moment. My instinct arrived first, and I trusted it about as far as I trusted instincts about contracts. "Hospitals first. A rescheduled surgical team costs more than a late gimbal shipment, and it costs patients."

"I'd have said that too," Nasser said. "Before I called the program office." He didn't look pleased with himself. "The gimbals aren't the problem. Their test range window is. They've booked a live-fire exercise with eleven other units, and if the gimbals miss it, their next window is five months out. That's not a penalty. That's a customer whose program slips by half a year, and they'll remember who did that to them."

"You called a customer's program office at seven in the morning."

"I called a woman I've worked with for six years. She picked up on the second ring and told me the truth." He sipped the tea, discovered it was cold, and put it down. "What I'd propose is that we split the lot. The hospitals get their full allocation, because nobody can trade that. The defense order gets seventy percent on the original date, enough to put the gimbals on the range, and the rest nine days later when Lyon catches up. I've already asked the supplier. They said six days if we pay for the express run, nine if we don't."

"Pay for the express run."

"It's not a small number."

"It's less than the penalty and less than five months of somebody's resentment." I looked at the board. "Who tells the customer?"

"I do, with Gloria on the line for the contract language. I wanted your approval before I made a promise she'd have to write down."

"You have it." I paused. "Tell the AI recommendations what we decided and why. If it's logged, next time it'll route the conflict faster."

"It doesn't learn from that. The cores are fixed." He caught himself, because he'd told me this before. "But the reference data does. I'll add the exercise window to the customer profile. Next time it'll ask the right question."

"Do that."

On the way out I realized I'd just spent twenty minutes on a decision that two specialized systems had refused to make, and that a man I'd never have found on my own had made it better. It had been a good twenty minutes. I tried not to count how many others like it I'd missed.

---

Marcus Hale had moved out of the engineering building eighteen months ago and into a test hall that smelled of ozone and machine oil. He said he got more done within thirty meters of the hardware. He was fifty now, gray through the beard, still moving like a man who'd lived in too many labs. He didn't look up from the stand until I was beside him.

"Two minutes," he said. "I'm watching it ripple."

The stand sat in a steel cradle in the middle of the hall: a ring of coils the size of a car tire, a damped platform at its center, and a four-kilogram test mass inside. A screen beside it showed the effective inertia of the mass, a green line that had dropped to about half its nominal value and held there. The line shivered, then began to wobble, and the stand shut itself down with a sound like a long exhale. The mass settled.

"Fifty-two percent for ninety seconds," Marcus said. "Then the field loses coherence at the edge of the coil. Same place as last month."

"Better than last month."

"Eleven seconds better." He scratched his beard. "It's a long way from the inertial system we want in a hull. I'd like you to remember that when you read the press office's summary."

"I'll remember."

"You won't. But I'll say it anyway." He wiped his hands. "You wanted something."

I'd brought a drive. Private work went across on physical media, hand-carried, never over the corporate network, and Marcus had never asked me why. I'd spent most of the previous month in my own lab behind two locked doors, with the Engineering Assistant running models against a bench rig that nobody but me had seen. The Knowledge Management AI had logged every step, tagging each result: _remembered, derived, measured, verified._ This package had cleared all four.

"A revised damping model for the coil edge," I said. "It should address the coherence loss. I've tested it against a scale rig, and the data's on the drive with the provenance."

Marcus took it and turned it over in his fingers. "Where does it come from?"

"My bench."

"Your bench is the bench where you build things on your own." He set it on the cradle's rail. "I'm not saying don't. I'm saying the last time I took a package from you that I couldn't trace, it took my team six weeks to find the one assumption you'd made without telling anyone. A bench isn't a test. It's a place where a test might have happened."

"The scale rig ran four hundred cycles. Every one is in there."

"Four hundred on your rig. I'll run four hundred on mine, and I'll run them blind, without looking at your predictions first." He looked at me with the patient irritation of a man who'd been right about me for a decade. "If it holds, you've saved us a quarter. If it doesn't, I need to know that before it goes anywhere near a ship."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's engineering." But he was almost smiling. "Naomi's waiting in medical. She wants fab time and she's going to ask you for it. I'll save you the trouble. I want it too."

"For what?"

"Coil winding. The bottleneck is the clean-line schedule." He shrugged. "I'm not asking you to decide in the hall. But you should know I'll argue for it."

---

The medical wing was the quietest building on the campus, and in a way the oldest. Everything else had grown from it. Naomi Reyes ran it from an office on the third floor with a window onto a courtyard full of the plants she insisted on watering herself. She was a chief medical officer who still took call at the county hospital one night a month, to keep, she said, her hands honest.

"Walk with me," she said. "There's something you should see before I ask you for anything."

We took the stairs. On the second floor, behind a window, a technician stood beside a treatment pod, a long white enclosure that had the same ancestry as a bench-scale unit in a loft but not a single component in common with it. A patient lay inside, awake and bored, with a clinician at the console. The clinician spoke the closing attestation aloud, with her name and the time, and the system waited for the second confirmation from a different person before it moved.

"Burn graft," Naomi said. "The scanner maps the wound bed. The pod prepares it. Then it waits for two humans to say they've looked." She watched through the glass. "Adeyemi still reviews the gate protocol every year. He says it's the only part of the device he trusts completely, and he's never forgiven you for it."

"He asked for it."

"He asked for a clinician gate. The two-person rule was Priya's." She smiled slightly. "He says she's the only person he's ever lost an argument to in writing."

Down the hall, a smaller room held a row of incubators and a bioreactor line, glass and pale light. "Engineered skin," Naomi said. "Grown on Med-Pod tissue scaffolds. We have enough for twelve full-thickness burn patients a month if the line runs clean, and it hasn't run clean in six weeks because the fab schedule keeps bumping our cell-culture cycles for coil winding."

"Marcus told me."

"Of course he did." She folded her arms. "I'm not going to pretend that's about politics. A burn patient who waits three weeks for a graft has a different infection risk than one who waits five days. I can show you the numbers."

"I believe you."

"Then you don't need the numbers. You need an answer." She raised an eyebrow. "Which I don't think you have."

I didn't. Marcus's stand was a year from a result that might matter. Naomi's patients were here, in rooms, with names. But the stand was the part of the company that might make the difference years from now, if anything did, and I'd learned the hard way not to starve a long project to feed a short one.

"Can the clean line run two shifts?" I asked.

"It could. We don't have the staff."

"Marcus said the bottleneck was scheduling, not capacity." I thought about it. "What if the coil winding moves to nights, with the maintenance droids, and your cell-culture cycles keep the day line? Neither of you gets everything. But you both get most of what you need."

Naomi considered it for a few seconds longer than I liked. "Nights are when my contamination rate goes up," she said. "Fewer people checking the line. If I take the day shift and Marcus takes the night, I want a human supervisor on his shift, someone who answers to me for the clean-line protocol, not to him."

"He'll object."

"Then he can say so." She looked almost amused. "He's going to say it was my idea."

"It's yours. I only did the arithmetic."

"Put that in writing before he sees it." She walked me to the stairs. "Thank you. I'll still ask next quarter."

"I know."

ASID's offices occupied the only building on the campus without windows on the ground floor. Director Kateryna Hrytsenko received me in a briefing room that had been swept for devices that morning and again that hour. She was a compact woman in her fifties who'd run counterintelligence for a national agency before she joined us, and she gave her briefings the way Nasser gave his forecasts: without decoration.

"Three items," she said. "Two will bore you. One won't."

The first was a recruiter. A European firm had been approaching mid-level engineers in the sensor division, offering salaries about thirty percent above ours. "Legal, as far as it goes," Hrytsenko said. "But the firm is a shell. It's funded through two holding companies that trace to a defense integrator we've competed with. We have no evidence of theft. We have a pattern, and I'd like permission to talk to the engineers who were approached. Not to accuse them. To let them know we know."

"Do it. And make sure they hear it from a person, not a memo."

"They will."

The second was a minor matter involving a cleaning contractor's maintenance droid on the third floor of the sensor building, which had spent forty minutes in a corridor it wasn't scheduled to enter. "Compartmentalization caught it," she said. "The droid tried to open a door it had no authorization for, the door refused, and the anomaly monitor flagged the attempt within three seconds. It was a vendor fault, not a deliberate act. But we're auditing the vendor's firmware anyway."

"And the third?"

She slid a photograph across the table. A man in a gray jacket, standing at a bus stop across from the north gate. "He's been there four days. Different times, same position. Our cameras have him pointing a phone at the campus entrance. A second man was seen near Okonkwo's route to the residence last Thursday. We haven't confirmed they're connected."

I looked at it for a while. "Kidnapping?"

"Possibly surveillance for a later attempt. Possibly a competitor's investigator who thinks he's being clever." She said it flatly. "We've had both. The last two attempts were amateurs. I'd like to assume the next one won't be."

"What do you need?"

"Two additional drivers for your routes and a rotation of the pickup times. The protective detail wants to vary your arrivals at the residence, which means Okonkwo drives different roads at different hours, and you'll be inconvenienced." She paused. "I'd also like to review your calendar for the next sixty days. Not your private research. I know where that line is, and I've never asked you to cross it. But your public movements."

"Helena will arrange it."

"I've already talked to her." Hrytsenko's expression didn't change. "She said you'd say yes. She also said you'd try to negotiate the inconvenience down to nothing, and that I should tell you not to."

I laughed. It surprised both of us. "I'm that predictable?"

"You're a client with a history of declining protection you don't want." She collected the photograph. "The people who work for you would prefer you lived. I'm one of them."

On the way out I thought about the man at the bus stop, and about a conversation a decade ago when Gloria Castellanos had told me that a company was only as safe as its worst assumption. The assumption I'd made then was that nobody would care enough to try. I'd been wrong in the spring of the second year and wrong again after that. ASID existed because I'd kept being wrong, and had been wise enough, eventually, to hire people who knew how.

At 1300 the Callisto review came in on a thirty-nine minute lag, so it wasn't a conversation. It was a recorded briefing from Lucia Ferreira, the settlement's administrator, followed by an empty slot where my reply would go, and then another thirty-nine minutes before she'd hear it. Callisto had taught us all to say what we meant in a single pass.

She appeared on the wall in her office, a tall woman in work fatigues with a window behind her showing the pale curve of Jupiter's shadow on the ice. She'd been administrator for six years and had stopped asking me for permission to say what she thought.

"Kenji. Three items, in order of urgency. First, the second residential ring. The current ring is at ninety-four percent occupancy and we have a waiting list. I know you deferred the expansion in March to prioritize the yard's power upgrade. I understand why. But the upgrade came in under budget and ahead of schedule, and I'm asking you to release the ring funds now, not in the next cycle."

She paused. A school bell, tinny and distant, rang somewhere behind her.

"Second, the water-ice processing plant has a recurring seal fault. It's not dangerous. My engineers have a fix, and it needs a four-day shutdown, which I'd prefer to schedule in the next two weeks. I'm not asking permission. I'm informing you so you don't read about it in a log.

"Third. We have four hundred and twelve children in the settlement school. When I started, there were ninety. They need more room, and I need you to understand that this isn't a figure on a spreadsheet to me." She leaned toward the camera. "That's all. I'll wait for your answer. Take whatever time you need. I'll be here."

I played it twice. Then I pulled up the funding request and read the figures.

It was a number I would once have needed a decade to earn, and it covered a ring section with sixty family units, a second school wing, and the life-support redundancy to keep both from becoming a problem in ten years. Most of it would be built by droids, supervised by a few dozen human engineers who'd chosen to live a half-billion kilometers from the nearest ocean.

Nasser's cost model said the ring would pay for itself in recruitment and retention inside six years. Helena's note, clipped to the margin, said only: _They're not asking you to be generous. They're asking you to keep a promise._

I recorded my reply in one take.

"Lucia. The ring is approved. Release the funds today and tell Ibrahim what you need from the supply chain. The seal fix goes ahead on your schedule, and I'd like a report afterward, not a request. And on the school: thank you for telling me the number. It's the one I'll remember."

I stopped. There was a pause in the recording where I should have said something else.

"Hug the kids for me," I added, and felt like an idiot, and sent it anyway.

Dr. Sen's office was a converted conference room on the fourth floor of the medical wing, and the review took ninety minutes, which was eighty longer than I'd planned.

He was a thin, precise man in his sixties who had left a research hospital to take a job with a single patient, and he conducted every visit as though he suspected the patient of sabotage. "Your bone density is within range," he said finally, studying a scan. "It's a different range than the one we started with, because the treatments change the baseline. I'd like to revise it. I don't think there's a problem. I think there's a measurement I used to understand and now I have to relearn."

"That's the reason I've been avoiding you."

"That's the reason I've been calling you." He closed the display. "Your recovery times are excellent. Your sleep is not. I'd like it to be, and I don't have a treatment for nineteen days of five a.m. starts."

"Helena told you."

"Helena told me nothing. Your sleep monitor told me." He gave the faintest smile. "She told me you were going to claim you were fine."

By the time I got out it was past 8:30, the sun had gone, and Helena had sent a message to the car that said only: _Home, or the shop. Your choice. Not the office._

I told Okonkwo the shop.

---

Himura Auto still sat on its corner lot at the edge of town, with the faded red sign and three lifts and a waiting room full of mismatched chairs. I'd never sold it. It had a manager now, a sharp woman named Ayesha who ran it with five human mechanics and not one droid, at their request and mine. The shop turned a small profit, and I'd stopped caring whether it did.

The lights were on in the back. The bay door was half open and a truck sat on the center lift with its hood up, and beside it, perched on a stool, sat a broad woman in her late sixties with a clipboard on her knee.

"You're late," Dolores said.

"I didn't know I was expected."

"You weren't. Ayesha called Helena. Helena said you'd come." She put the clipboard down. "I've been retired three years and I still come in Fridays. Nobody's stopped me yet."

"It's Monday."

"I know what day it is." She looked me over the way she had when I was twenty, with her head tilted. "You look tired."

"I had a medical review."

"That'll do it." She got up, one hand on the lift for balance, and walked to the shelf beside the parts-room door. I knew what she was reaching for before she took it down: the green cloth-bound ledger, worn soft at the corners, with my mother's handwriting at the top of every page and mine, in a different ink, somewhere near the back. I'd stopped writing in it six or seven years ago, when the numbers got too large to fit in a column.

"Ayesha keeps it current for the shop," Dolores said. "I told her she should. Your mother would have." She opened it to a page near the end and turned it toward me. "Look at that."

It was a column of figures, ordinary ones, for a repair shop with five mechanics. Parts, wages, a payment on a new lift. At the bottom, in Ayesha's neat hand, a total that was almost exactly what my mother's had been in her best year.

"That's all it makes," I said.

"That's all it needs to." Dolores closed the ledger. "You know what I think when I walk onto that campus? Fourteen thousand people. I used to deliver a box of crystals up those stairs, and a woman looked at my face and said they listened to the insides of things."

"That was me."

"It was you holding a box I told you not to drop." She set the ledger on the shelf. "I'm not going to tell you it's wonderful. I'm too old, and you'd know I was lying. But I'll tell you this. When you started, you could carry the whole thing in your two hands. You can't anymore. You've got people who carry it for you, and some of them are carrying more than you know."

"I do know."

"Then act like it." She didn't look at me while she said it. "And eat something. There's a sandwich in the office. It's not good, but it's food."

I ate it standing at my mother's old workbench, under the pegboard where each tool still hung inside its marker outline. The truck on the lift ticked as it cooled. Somewhere in the back, a radio was playing old rock, turned down low, on a station I hadn't changed.

_Who's going to buy it,_ Dolores had asked me once, with a clipboard under her arm, in a loft above this room.

Nobody had needed to answer her. The answer was fourteen thousand people and a school on Callisto. It was a woman in a burn ward telling the truth about a closing attestation and a man in a gray jacket at a bus stop. It was a stack of invoices that no one person could have read in a year, most of them for work I'd never see.

I finished the sandwich. Then I took the stool next to the truck's open hood, picked up a flashlight from the bench, and looked at the harness, because it had been a long day and it was the one job I still knew how to do.

"Alternator?" I asked.

"Ground strap," said Dolores, from the doorway. "Don't tell Ayesha I told you. She wanted to find it herself."

I put my hand on the loom and felt for the thing that was rubbing.
