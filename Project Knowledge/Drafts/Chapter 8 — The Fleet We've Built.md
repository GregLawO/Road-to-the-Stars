**Chapter 8 — The Fleet We've Built**

"Cradle Two is holding at forty percent," the yard controller said. "Hull cleared for the ring, but the inspection droids found a weld they don't like on the starboard cargo door. They've held the work and sent it up."

"Whose weld?" I asked.

"Ours." Captain Isabel Okonjo-Reyes didn't look up from the tablet she was reading as she walked. "The door was refitted after the Tethys run. A boarding charge buckled the frame and we straightened it. Somebody signed it off, and the system is politely asking whether that somebody was right."

"Was it?"

"The somebody was me." She handed the tablet to a yard technician, who took it with a nod and a look at me. "So I'd like to see the weld before anyone else decides."

I'd come to Callisto's orbital yard for the trials, not the cargo door, but I followed her anyway. That was how I'd learned to spend time with the fleet: you went where the people who knew things went, and you didn't announce your own errand.

The yard hung in the lee of the moon's night side, a skeletal spread of gantries, tool arms, and floodlit cradles strung across a kilometer of vacuum. Four of the six corvettes were in port. Cradle Two held the _Tern_, her long gray hull stripped of its outer plating along one flank, with maintenance droids moving over the exposed frames in slow, deliberate lines like a team of surgeons who didn't need to talk to each other. Cradle Three held the _Kestrel_, whose crew was loading consumables through a lock the size of a garage door. Their breath fogged in the pressurized cargo bay, and someone was complaining loudly about the pallet order.

Beyond them, in the biggest cradle the yard owned, with a framework around it that nobody had bothered to disguise, sat the frigate.

I didn't look at her yet. I'd found it was better to save her for last.

---

The _Tern_'s cargo door was a steel slab four meters wide, and the weld in question ran along its lower frame in a ridge the color of old pewter. Okonjo-Reyes crouched in front of it with a handlamp. The inspection droid stood beside her, an articulated platform with a sensor mast, waiting without impatience.

"Show me what it flagged," she said.

The droid projected an overlay on the door. A faint orange band lit up along the weld. "Ultrasonic reading shows a lack of fusion at the root over a length of eleven centimeters," it said. "Probability of defect is moderate. Existing inspection record reports no indication. The discrepancy is escalated for human review."

"Because the record is mine."

"Because the record and the measurement disagree. I am not authorized to resolve disagreements between a signed inspection and a current measurement."

"Good." She ran a gloved thumb along the ridge. "I signed it off after a dye-penetrant check, in the middle of a run, with a hull that needed to be somewhere in thirty hours. It looked sound. It might not be." She turned to me. "I'd like to cut it out and redo it. It's six hours, maybe eight, and the _Tern_ misses her escort slot."

"What does the slot cost?"

"A convoy gets a different escort. The _Kestrel_ can cover it, with a thin margin." She stood, and her knees cracked. "I'm not asking for permission. I'm telling you what I'm going to do, because the schedule is yours and I'd rather you heard it from me."

I'd heard a version of that sentence from Baek two weeks ago and Lucia Ferreira two weeks before that. The people I'd hired had developed a pattern of informing me of their decisions with enough courtesy that I could pretend they were requests.

"Cut it out," I said.

"Thank you." She was already walking. "And get the structural team to look at the frame on the port side. If one door took a charge, the other one took the same shock."

---

She'd come to us from a national navy eight years ago, a decorated commander with a bad knee and a sharp opinion of civilian shipbuilders. She'd taken the first corvette out on her own commissioning run, and she'd commanded the _Tern_ through everything that had happened since. When we'd given her a fleet command in everything but title, she'd asked for one condition: that nobody above her rank, my own included, would issue orders to a ship's captain except through her.

It was the most sensible thing anyone had ever asked of me, and I'd agreed before she'd finished the sentence.

We walked along the yard's service gallery, a long, windowed corridor looking out over the cradles. A pair of engineers in company coveralls passed us, arguing about thermal margins in the cheerful tone of colleagues who'd done it before. A droid trailed them with a cart of tools.

"Walk me through the six," I said. "Not the specifications. The history."

"You've read the history."

"I've read the reports. I'd like it from you."

She considered that. "The _Tern_ and the _Kestrel_ are the oldest. We built them for convoy work, in the days when we still thought a corvette would be a floating sensor platform with a few guns. Then a mining outpost in the belt called for help at four in the morning and the nearest military ship was nine days away." She paused at a window. "We got there in two. The raiders had already breached the main habitat. We took forty-one people off a station designed for sixty, and we lost two of our own."

"Marcus Dell and Priya Anand."

She glanced at me. "You remember the names."

"I signed the letters."

"Most people read them and forget." She started walking again. "After that we stopped treating the fleet as an asset. It's a crew. Every ship in it has a captain who'd rather burn the hull than lose the people in it, and I'd like you to remember that when someone asks you to put numbers on it."

"Which numbers?"

"Cost per hull. Cost per hour. You're going to hear a lot about efficiency, and I'm telling you now that I'll fight any metric that treats a crew as a variable."

"That's not a position I'd argue with."

"You'd be surprised how many people do." She gave me a half-smile that vanished almost immediately. "The other four came later and were built better. _Osprey_, _Harrier_, _Merlin_, and _Shrike_. They've done convoy duty on the outer routes and escorted ore haulers through three pirate seasons. Two of them took hits last year. _Merlin_ lost a point-defense mount and a gunner to a lucky railgun round off a hijacker's tug. We've fixed the mount. The gunner's family is still in the settlement." She said it flatly. "I visited them last month."

I'd have said something, if I could have found anything worth saying.

---

The operations room on the yard's command tier was warmer than the gallery, and smaller than I remembered. Four human controllers sat at consoles beside a larger bank where the yard's logistics intelligence managed berths, supply schedules, and the droid teams in the cradles. A short, broad man in his fifties was arguing with one of the consoles, which was not a thing a human could win.

"I understand the supply forecast," he said, to no one in particular. "I'm telling you I need the replacement coil by Thursday, not Monday."

"The forecast is based on freight availability," the console said. "Thursday delivery requires reassigning a hauler from the Ganymede route. That reassignment exceeds my delegated authority."

"Then escalate it."

"I have. It is waiting for review."

"By whom?"

"By you, Chief Engineer."

The man looked at the console for a moment, then at us, and began to laugh. "I've been asking myself for permission to do something I've already decided to do," he said. "Sorry, Isabel. Mr. Himura."

"Chief," I said. "I think you'd better approve it."

"I did." He tapped the screen, which chimed, and the console reorganized itself without commentary. "Hakon Strand. Chief of Yard Engineering, which means I'm responsible for everything that breaks and nobody thanks me when it doesn't." He had a voice like gravel in a barrel. "You're here for the frigate."

"I'm here for the day."

"Then you're here for the frigate." He picked up a mug from a console, read the label, and put it down again. "Trial Block Four starts at fourteen hundred. The review's at thirteen. Isabel and I need twenty minutes before that, because we disagree about something and I'd rather have it out in front of you than behind your back."

Okonjo-Reyes sat on the edge of a console. "I don't think it's a disagreement. I think it's two people worrying about different things."

"That's what a disagreement is."

---

It was about the shutdown procedure.

Strand laid it out on the wall, a sequence of eleven steps that took the frigate's drive from full operation to a safe state. The engineering team had designed it around a single principle: stop the field cleanly, in the right order, so no part of the hull saw an uncontrolled change. "Done properly," he said, "it takes ninety seconds. It protects the generators, the coil structure, and the hull. If we cut a step, we risk the equipment."

"And if there's a fire in the drive compartment?" Okonjo-Reyes asked.

"Then the fire suppression runs in parallel, and the crew isolates the compartment."

"And ninety seconds is how long the drive keeps running while that's happening."

"It's the safe way to—"

"I know it's the safe way. I'm asking what a captain is supposed to do with ninety seconds when something is burning." She said it without rancor. "I've lost a ship's worth of friends to ninety seconds. You're an engineer, Hakon. You protect the equipment first because that's what you're trained to do. I'm trained to protect the people. I need a way to kill the field in under ten, even if it breaks every coil in the ring."

Strand's face went still. "That would destroy the generators."

"Would it destroy the ship?"

"No. Probably not." He rubbed his jaw. "It would cost us eight months and several million, and if we do it during a trial, we lose the data."

"Then we lose the data." She folded her arms. "I'm not asking to use it. I'm asking for it to exist."

I'd stayed quiet through most of this. It wasn't a decision I could make. Neither of them was wrong, and I had no standing to tell a ship's captain how long she was permitted to wait. But I also had a question.

"Is a ten-second cut survivable for the hull?"

Strand glanced at me. "Survivable," he said slowly. "The field collapses asymmetrically. There'd be a structural shock. It depends on the field strength at the time. At low power, probably fine. At trial power..." He shrugged. "Nobody's tested it."

"Then test it at low power first," I said. "Build the fast cut as an emergency option with its own interlock, so it can't be triggered by accident. Hakon, you design the safe version of the dangerous thing. Isabel, you tell him what the crew can realistically do in the time they have."

Neither of them said anything.

"I'm not deciding," I added. "I'm suggesting. If you both think it's wrong, ignore me."

"It's not wrong." Strand said it grudgingly. "It's what I'd have proposed in a week if I'd had the nerve. Putting a guaranteed-damage switch on a ship nobody's flown at full power isn't a request I make lightly."

"You didn't have to make it," Okonjo-Reyes said. "I did."

"Then I'll build it." He pulled a note up on the console. "With a keyed lock and two-person authorization. And Isabel, if you ever use it, I want to be the first to know."

"You'll be the first to hear the screaming."

He almost smiled. "That's fair."

---

I saw the frigate properly at last from the observation lounge, a glass-walled room on the yard's upper tier with a view straight across to the main cradle. They'd named her _Akebono_, for the first light before dawn, and it had been the engineers' choice, not mine.

She was five hundred meters of dull gray hull, longer than anything else in the yard and slimmer than any ship I'd have called a warship in my previous life. Her bow came to a blunt point. Amidships, a broad ring of dark composite circled her like a belt, thicker than the hull around it. That ring was the drive, the first Gravitic Spacetime Drive anyone had ever built, or had ever tried to build, and it carried more of Akatsuki's money than everything else in the yard combined.

There were no weapons mounted. There wouldn't be for a long time. This was a testbed, and every cubic meter of her interior was packed with instruments, cabling, and a crew of ninety-three, thirty of them engineers and thirty more military personnel on loan from two national services and the Systems Alliance Navy, there to find out whether a human crew could actually operate what we'd built.

"Looks different from here, doesn't it?" said a voice behind me.

I turned. Dr. Amara Nwosu, the drive program's chief scientist, had come in with a coffee in each hand and gave me one without asking. She was in her early forties, wore her hair in a plain braid, and had a habit of looking at a machine as if it owed her an explanation.

"She looked finished in the simulations," I said.

"Everything looks finished in simulations." She leaned on the rail. "Trial Block Four is the first time we run the ring at forty percent for more than ten minutes. If it holds, we go to fifty next week. If it doesn't, we find out why." She said it lightly, but she hadn't slept; I could see it in her face. "I've told my team the same thing every day for three months. A good trial is one where we learn something, not one where everything goes right."

"And how many have gone right?"

"Almost none." She grinned. "That's why they've been good."

"Is the shutdown disagreement going to affect your schedule?"

"It might." She took a sip. "Hakon's team will have to redesign the emergency cut. But Isabel's right. I'd been thinking about the drive as a physics problem. She's thinking about it as a ship. I don't know which of us is more useful, but I know I wouldn't want to fly in a vessel that only one of us had designed."

"You're not going to be aboard for the trial, are you?"

"I'll be in the control room." She looked at me with some amusement. "You are, though, if you're coming."

"I'm observing."

"Observers still need a place to stand."

---

The control room was a windowless chamber on the frigate's third deck, packed with consoles and ringed by screens. A dozen people sat at stations, and four more stood behind them. At the center, in a chair that was bolted to the floor and didn't look comfortable, sat Commander Kastanie Drescher of the Systems Alliance Navy.

She had been sent to lead the Alliance's observer detachment for the duration of the trials, with three officers of her own, and she'd made it clear on her first day that she wasn't an employee, a guest, or a consultant. She was the officer responsible for finding out whether this ship could be run by a naval crew, and for deciding what the Alliance would be told about it. She'd said so to Strand, and then to Nwosu, and then, at length and without raising her voice, to me. She was in her late thirties, with close-cropped dark hair and a way of asking about reliability, maintenance, and crew safety in an order that made it plain which she cared about most.

"Ring is at thirty-two percent," a technician called. "Field is stable. Coherence at the edge is within tolerance."

"Understood," Drescher said. "Navigation, confirm your solution."

"Navigation solution confirmed. Target point eleven thousand kilometers ahead. Emergence tolerance is plus or minus fifty meters." The navigator was a thin young woman named Lena Hartmann, and she said the figure without looking up. "That's the number the drive team gave me. I don't believe it yet."

"Nobody does," Nwosu murmured, from the engineering station.

"Mr. Himura," Drescher said, without turning. "You may speak, but not on the command net."

"I'll stay quiet."

"Good." She raised her voice. "All stations. This is a test of the crew's ability to execute a short-range activation and an immediate controlled shutdown. I want to hear every deviation. Not after, now."

I stood against the back wall, between a rack of cabling and a console no one was using, and watched the screens.

The ring's power climbed. On the main display, a green line traced the field's coherence, wavering slightly as it rose. Beneath it, a column of numbers scrolled too fast to follow. The technicians didn't look at the numbers. They looked at their own displays, and spoke in short, flat sentences, and every few seconds Drescher said "Acknowledged," in the same tone, as if she were counting.

"Forty percent," said a voice. "Holding."

"Navigation, begin the sequence."

"Beginning." Hartmann's voice had tightened. "Ten seconds."

The deck didn't shake, and I'd been half expecting it to. The only change was a faint pressure in my ears and a shift in the light on the screens, as if a cloud had passed over the room.

"Field is shaped," Nwosu said. "Aperture is open. Drive is live."

"Five," Hartmann said. "Four. Three."

I found I was holding my breath, and I let it out.

"Two. One. Execute."

For an instant, nothing happened. Then the main display flickered, the star field on it swam sideways, and the numbers on the navigation panel froze, blinked, and began to count again.

"Emergence," Hartmann said, and her voice cracked slightly. "We're at the target point."

"Distance from solution?" Drescher asked.

"Nineteen kilometers."

There was a pause. I could feel it across the room, every person doing the same arithmetic. The tolerance was fifty meters. We'd missed it by nearly four hundred times.

"Understood," Drescher said. "Shut it down."

"Beginning shutdown." Nwosu's hands moved over her console. "Ring is ramping down. Field collapse in eighty seconds."

"Eighty seconds is too slow," Drescher said mildly. "I'm told there's been a discussion about that, and that someone is building a faster cut. Before anyone calls this ship ready, I'd like to see it tested at low power with a crew who didn't design it. But that's for later. Any fault indications?"

"Thermal spike in coil nine. Within limits." A technician spoke from the left. "No structural alarms."

"Navigation?"

"The drive emerged where the solution said it would, plus nineteen kilometers." Hartmann hesitated. "Ma'am, I'm not sure that's a navigation error. I think the drive itself drifted."

"Dr. Nwosu?"

"It might have." She hadn't looked up. "I'll need the raw data."

On the display, the field line fell to zero. The crew sat motionless. The ship had traveled eleven thousand kilometers in a time I couldn't have measured without the console, and nobody in the room said a word.

Then Drescher stood up. "That," she said, "was the first time a human crew has taken a vessel through a controlled FTL transition on this design. I'm told that's a milestone." She looked around the room. "It's also the first time a navigation officer has missed her target by nineteen kilometers and said so out loud. Lieutenant Hartmann, I want your written assessment by tonight. I don't care what it costs the program. I care that the next navigator reading it knows exactly what to expect."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Dr. Nwosu." She turned. "The drive works. Congratulations. I'd now like to know why I should trust it with my crew."

Nwosu looked up for the first time. "You shouldn't. Not yet."

"Good," Drescher said. "That's the right answer."

---

The debrief ran two hours, and I sat through all of it.

It wasn't a celebration. The engineers argued about drift, the naval officers argued about procedure, and Strand's team reported the thermal spike in coil nine with the calm of men who'd seen worse and expected to see it again. Drescher asked for a design change to the control layout. Nwosu said it would delay the next block by a week. Drescher said that was acceptable. A young engineer stood up and said, with some embarrassment, that he thought the shutdown button was in the wrong place. People looked at him, and then at the layout, and then at Drescher, who nodded slowly and said, "He's right."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing in that room I could have improved, and it was a quiet pleasure to realize it.

When the last of them had filed out, Drescher stopped beside me at the door. "Do you have a few minutes, Mr. Himura? I'd like the lounge. Captain Okonjo-Reyes, if you can spare them, I'd like you there too."

"I'd have come anyway," Okonjo-Reyes said. "I'd like to hear what you tell him."

---

The lounge was empty. Through the glass the _Akebono_ sat in her cradle under the work lights, the dark ring around her middle catching the glare. Drescher stood at the rail with her hands behind her back and looked at the ship the way I'd seen Marcus look at a stand that had just failed: with respect, and without any illusions.

"I'll tell you what I'll put in my report," she said. "The drive works. The crew can run it. The ship is not a warship, and she isn't close. Nineteen kilometers is a long way from a navigation solution I'd trust, and eighty seconds of shutdown is a long time to be wrong." She glanced at me. "None of that surprises you."

"No."

"It didn't surprise Dr. Nwosu either, which is why I believe her." She turned to face us. "What I don't know yet is what you think this is. A test article, or the first of something."

I'd been expecting the question for a month. "Both. The _Akebono_ is a step. She's five hundred meters because that's the smallest hull that could carry the drive ring and the instruments to learn from it. She isn't the largest ship we want to build."

"How much larger?"

"Preliminary goals, nothing more. We have no designs and no hulls under construction. If this program works, the next class up is a fast battlecruiser, around seven hundred and fifty meters. Faster than the frigate, with heavier armament. Above that, a line cruiser at a thousand meters, a heavy fleet combatant with the endurance to operate as a command ship." I nodded at the window. "The corvettes keep doing what they do. We'd build more of those, and more frigates, alongside the larger classes."

Okonjo-Reyes said nothing. She'd heard some of it before, in pieces, and I could tell she was watching Drescher more than me.

Drescher was quiet for a few seconds. "A thousand meters," she said. "That's twice the length of this ship and a good deal more than twice the problem."

"I know."

"Do you?" It wasn't hostile. "Let me tell you what I see, and you tell me where I'm wrong. Start with the yard. Your largest cradle holds this hull with about ten meters to spare, and it took you four years to build. A thousand-meter ship needs a cradle that doesn't exist, in a yard that doesn't exist. Then the material. The tonnage of structure alone is several times what you've handled, and every kilogram of it has to be moved, machined, and welded by someone. Your droids caught a bad weld on a cargo door this morning. I'd like to know how many doors a line cruiser has."

"A lot."

"Then power. The ring on this ship drew everything the yard could give it for ten minutes at forty percent. A heavier hull means a larger field, a larger field means more generator mass, and the generators have to be kept coherent at the edge, which is the one thing your people haven't yet solved at this scale." She shook her head. "I'm not saying it can't be done. I'm saying every one of those problems multiplies the others."

"That's fair," I said. "It's also why I'm not promising dates."

"Good. Now the part you haven't mentioned." She looked at Okonjo-Reyes. "Captain, how many people does it take to fight this ship?"

"Ninety-three, for trials. About sixty to run her operationally, if the automation holds." Okonjo-Reyes shrugged. "For something twice the length, I'd guess two hundred and fifty. I'd want a damage-control organization that doesn't exist yet, because no officer can walk a kilometer of hull in the time a fire gives her. I'd want a second command position that could take over if the first was gone. And I'd want a crew that had trained together for a year before anyone shot at them."

"That's the number I was afraid of." Drescher turned back to me. "It isn't the hull that limits you, Mr. Himura. It's the people who have to live in it. You can't automate a captain's judgment, and you can't buy a crew's experience. A line cruiser with a green crew is a very large target."

"I agree."

"Then why build it?"

I took a moment with the answer, because it deserved one. "Because the work we do now only matters if it can be scaled. A frigate protects a convoy. It doesn't protect a system. If something ever comes through that we can't handle with six corvettes and a testbed, I'd rather have spent the years learning how to build the next size up than discovering we should have started."

"Something like what?"

"Pirates with better ships. A rival power. Anything." I kept my voice even. "I don't have a specific threat in mind. I have a company that's good at building things and a feeling that a decade from now we'll want more than we have."

She studied me, and I couldn't tell whether she believed all of it. "That's more candor than I get from most contractors." She looked back at the _Akebono_. "I'll tell you what the Alliance cares about. It isn't the hull. A dozen governments will build larger hulls in the next twenty years, and most of them will be slower and less reliable than yours. What matters is whether human crews can maintain a gravitic ship at that scale, with spares and trained technicians and a supply line, in the middle of a deployment. If your ships can do that, you're offering something no other builder has. If they can't, you've built a very impressive piece of metal."

"Then that's what the trials are for."

"They are." She nodded once. "I'll report that the program is real, that the engineering is honest, and that the larger ambitions are exactly that: ambitions. Whether anyone acts on them is above my rank."

"That's all I'd ask."

"It isn't, but it's what I'll take." The corner of her mouth moved. "You'll want to start with the frigate's maintenance records, Mr. Himura. I'll be reading them."

She left, and her three officers fell in behind her at the door without being called.

Okonjo-Reyes lowered herself onto the arm of a lounge chair and rubbed her knee. "Nineteen kilometers," she said.

"It's a long way from fifty meters."

"It's a long way from nothing." She folded her arms. "A year ago the drive couldn't hold a field for ten seconds. Today it took a ship eleven thousand kilometers and put her down in the right neighborhood. I'd call that a good day, and I'd tell any captain who asked that it's still not a ship I'd take into a fight."

"Would you take her out of one?"

She thought about it. "Not yet. If I had a choice, I'd use the corvettes." She tilted her head toward the window at the far end of the lounge, where the _Tern_ sat in her cradle with her flank open to the dark. "They aren't pretty. They aren't fast. But I know what they'll do."

"That's what Commander Drescher said about the drive."

"She's a careful officer." Okonjo-Reyes pushed herself upright. "I like her. She'll make the frigate better, and she'll never say it was her idea. And she's right about the crew. I'll tell you now, I've never met a shipbuilder who thought about that before he thought about the hull."

"I'm thinking about it now."

"Good," she said. "Keep thinking."

---

I left the yard at nineteen hundred, on a shuttle that ran dark across the settlement's night side. Through the window I could see the cradles drifting past, one by one. The _Tern_'s work lights burned along her stripped flank. In Cradle Three, the _Kestrel_ was still taking on supplies, and a single tiny figure in a pressure suit moved along her hull with a handlamp, inspecting something I'd never see.

I thought about what the fleet was.

Six corvettes. Everything we had in the water, six hulls, each of them two hundred and fifty meters of metal and wiring and the people who lived inside. They guarded convoys and mining stations and the long, unglamorous routes between them. They'd been shot at. They'd lost people. They'd come home, been repaired, and gone out again, and none of it had been written up in a trade journal. It was a fleet, but a small one. A national navy would have laughed at it, and a pirate crew with a lucky shot could still cost us a ship.

And the frigate: five hundred meters of promise, with a drive that missed its mark by nineteen kilometers and a shutdown that took eighty seconds when it needed to take ten. Years of work lay between that ship and anything I'd have called operational. Drescher knew it, and so did Nwosu. Okonjo-Reyes had said it plainly in the lounge, and I trusted her more for saying it. The larger ships I'd described in that room weren't plans yet. They were a direction, and a sharp Alliance officer had spent twenty minutes listing everything that stood between us and it.

What we had was something real, and something unfinished, and the distance between them was made of work that other people would do. I'd supplied the money and the first sketch, and a few ideas that had turned out to be useful. The rest belonged to the engineer arguing with a logistics console, the captain who'd visited a gunner's family, and the navigator who'd said she didn't believe the number.

The shuttle turned, and the yard dropped out of the window.

On the console beside my seat, the Executive Assistant lit a line of text. _Chief of Staff reminds you that tomorrow's schedule begins at 0900. She has also noted that you did not eat at the yard._

"Tell her I had coffee."

_She anticipated that response. She asks me to tell you it does not count._

I laughed, tired and a little embarrassed, and asked the shuttle's galley for a sandwich. It was the second-worst I'd ever had. I ate every bite, watching the yard shrink behind us until it was a scatter of lights against the black, and then I opened the trial summary and began to read it, from the beginning, the way my mother had taught me to read a work order.
