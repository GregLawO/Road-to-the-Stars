**Chapter 10 — The Universe Just Got Bigger**

"Lower," Dolores said. "No, lower. You're lighting the ceiling."

I lay on the creeper under her Civic with the flashlight in one hand and a split CV boot in the other, and moved the beam six inches. Grease had already found its way down my wrist. Above me, from a stool she had dragged beside the lift, Dolores watched like an inspector.

"That's better. Now don't move it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to. You've got a restless arm." She shifted on the stool, and I heard the click of her hip, and neither of us mentioned it. "Your mother never held a light still either. She'd talk with it."

"I'm not talking."

"You're thinking at it. I can see the beam wobble."

I laughed, and the beam wobbled again. It was Friday, a little after four. This was the second Friday since the party, and I had arrived at one in the afternoon, ahead of the time I'd promised. Dolores had accused me of showing off. I had spent three hours learning that a boot clamp was a worse enemy than most of the competitors I'd ever faced, and I hadn't looked at a screen once.

The bay door rattled. I knew the footsteps before I rolled out from under the car. Helena Vasquez didn't walk onto shop floors in good shoes, and she did not come in person unless the call was one she didn't trust to a speaker.

"Nothing has happened," she said, before I could ask.

"That's a terrible way to start."

"Dr. Voss asked to see you today, in person. She asked me to say it isn't an emergency." Helena glanced at Dolores. "I told her you were occupied."

"That's what people say about emergencies," I said.

"That's what I told her. She said she knew, and that she'd chosen the words carefully, and that you'd understand when you saw it." Helena folded her arms. "I've worked with Elena Voss for six years. She has never once asked for the founder."

Dolores waved the grease rag at me. "Go. I'll find somebody with steadier hands."

"I'll finish the boot."

"You'll finish it next Friday." She turned her head and looked at Helena, then back at me, and her voice softened. "Wash your hands first. Nobody wants a scientist covered in axle grease."

---

Elena Voss ran Survey Sciences from a building at the north end of the campus, and she was waiting at the door when the car pulled up. She was in her fifties, tall and spare, with cropped gray hair and a habit of speaking as if each sentence had been audited. She had come to us from a national space agency's planetary program. Her team had spent four years building a single instrument package for a single probe.

"Thank you for coming," she said. "I'd like to apologize in advance. I'm going to be tiresome about what we know."

"Be tiresome."

"It's the only way I know how to do this."

The probe was called PLUMB, which was the survey consortium's acronym, and which our engineers had turned into the Plumb Line without asking permission. It was a mapper, an unglamorous workhorse in a long polar orbit around Charon, built in our Callisto yard under a contract with a consortium of university programs and three national agencies. The consortium wanted radar sounding and high-resolution imaging of a small, cold moon at the edge of the system. We had bid on it for a different reason. Under the dish, bolted to the spacecraft's spine, sat the first deep-space flight unit of our gravitic gradiometer, an instrument that measured the gradient of inertial fields rather than simple mass. It needed a place where the background was quiet, and almost nowhere was quieter than the outer system. The consortium had agreed to carry it as an experimental payload. They didn't get its data. It was ours.

The light lag from Pluto was a little over five hours each way. Voss's team had spent the last fortnight reading the probe's telemetry in the order it arrived.

"Kwame," she said, and a young analyst in a rumpled cardigan stood up from a console as though called to the front of a class. "Tell him how it started."

Kwame Asante cleared his throat. "Eleven days ago the sounder returned a flat-bottomed echo from a chasm floor on Charon's far hemisphere. It was about four hundred meters down through the ice, and it didn't look like stratigraphy. Stratigraphy is messy. This was sharp, with a sort of edge to it."

"Which meant what?" I asked.

"Which meant I assumed I'd broken something." He grinned weakly. "The analysis AI flagged the return as geometrically inconsistent with natural layering and declined to classify it. I ran it through the instrument checks. Nothing wrong. I ran it again."

"We considered five explanations," Voss said. She put them on the wall in a column. "A multipath artifact in the sounder. A buried impact-melt sheet. A dense metallic fragment. A cryovolcanic intrusion. An instrument fault. The first and last died quickly, because the echo persisted on the next orbit from a different angle. Melt sheets and intrusions are irregular. A metallic fragment would have a gravity signature much larger than a body that size should hide."

"And the gradiometer?"

"That's why you're here." She nodded at the screen. "It sees the same place. A weak, localized gradient, very steady, with a shape. A ring, and something along its axis. I don't have a word for it, and I distrust anything I can't name, so I asked someone who might."

Marcus Hale was sitting in the corner with his boots on a chair and a mug of coffee, which was the first time I'd seen him in a room that wasn't his own since the party. He lifted a hand.

"Mass doesn't make that pattern," he said. "A pile of rock, a pile of iron, a buried ocean, any distribution you like. The field's too clean. It has symmetry, and nothing in the ground makes symmetry for free." He rubbed his jaw. "That's an inertial gradient. I've built every kind of field source this company owns, and I've shaped fields on a bench for eighteen years. I know what ours look like. This isn't one."

"Could it be a natural gradient we haven't seen?"

"It could be anything we haven't seen. That's the problem with the word." He shrugged. "I'm saying the simplest explanation is that something made it."

The room was quiet. Voss let the silence run, and then did what I appreciated most about her. She didn't take the hypothesis any further.

"We don't have enough," she said. "The probe passes over the site again in nine days, and its orbit can be lowered for a closer pass. The sounder will have a resolution we've never used. The camera has not seen that ground at all." She looked at me. "I'd like your authority to do the lowering. It uses fuel we'd planned for the end of the mission."

"Spend it."

"I wanted to be sure. If I'm wrong, that's an expensive way to find out."

"If you're wrong, we've mapped a very interesting piece of ice." I paused. "Elena. Does anyone outside this room know?"

"The sounder returns go to the consortium on the usual schedule. The gradiometer data does not. The sounder anomaly is in the quality notes, flagged as unclassified, as we flag anything that fits no model." She hesitated. "I didn't want to hide it. I also didn't want to say anything I couldn't support."

"Good," I said. "Keep both."

---

The nine days passed the way they always do when you are waiting on a spacecraft: I did my job. I approved a procurement plan, argued with Ibrahim about a shipping delay, and signed off on a personnel review in the medical wing. At the edge of every meeting, I found myself looking at the time and doing the light-lag arithmetic. Helena noticed, and did not comment.

On the ninth day, Voss called me to the observation room instead of sending the data.

The wall showed Charon's far hemisphere in raw radar, then in optical, a pale gray wall of ice cut by a long chasm, lit at a low angle by a sun that was a very small, very bright point. The probe had dropped to a hundred and ten kilometers. The sounder had come back with returns that made Asante sit very still. The optical camera had given us something else.

"Here," Voss said. She enlarged an image. "The chasm wall has slumped, probably in the last few hundred million years. The overburden is thin along this edge. Part of the object is exposed."

It lay along the base of the cliff, half buried, half free of the ice. A long central spine, straight as a rule, ran back into the wall. At its near end, a ring of curved arcs surrounded the spine in a series of segments, each one separated by a gap. Beyond the ring, two long arms curved forward and outward from the spine, tapering, so that the whole thing looked like a tuning fork laid on its side.

The surface was dark, matte, without crater scars or erosion that I could see. The ice around it was clean.

"The material's reflectance doesn't match any ice, rock, or metal we have a spectrum for," Voss said. "Its albedo is low and uniform. There are no visible joins or fasteners. The spine is at least a kilometer in length. The ring is a bit under that in diameter." She paused. "The gradiometer says it's still doing something. The field's faint, steady, and it's centered on the ring."

"It's maintaining a field," Marcus said. "After however many years it's been sitting here."

"We can't date it," Voss said. "I'd like to be careful. We can say it's artificial. We can say it's not ours, and not like anything we've built. It's very large, very old, very well preserved, and it appears to be designed for something at scale. If I had to guess, I'd say infrastructure. I wouldn't put it in a report."

Nobody spoke. The ring on the wall hung there, patient and dark.

I was looking at the arms.

It took me longer than it should have. I'd been looking at the whole shape for a minute, the way you look at a thing you've decided to understand later. Then the two arms curved into a part of my mind that wasn't working on procurement or schedules, and something in me went very quiet.

I had seen that shape before. Not in this life.

Memory is usually a slow thing. It comes up in pieces, a smell or a phrase, and you assemble it. This came whole, as a word, and the word was _relay_.

Then another word followed it, and another, and I stood very still while they came. A relay, and a moon at the edge of the system, and a name that went with the moon. Charon. I knew that name. I knew it the way you know the first line of a song you haven't heard in a decade.

"Kenji?" Voss said.

"I'm here." My voice sounded ordinary. "Give me a minute."

I walked out of the observation room and down the corridor to a window at the end of it, a long pane that looked out over the campus. The convoy lanes were busy. A line of cargo droids was crossing the access road, and a human technician in a yellow vest was standing in front of the last one, inspecting something on its chassis, and the whole line waited.

I put my hand against the glass and felt the cool of it.

I knew what the object was. I did not know much else. That was the part that held me still. I could have told you it was a relay, one of many, that they moved things between stars. I could have told you there were others in the galaxy who used them, and a council, and a space station at the center of it all that I couldn't picture clearly. I remembered a war that humanity fought, and nearly lost. I remembered something worse behind all of it, something with a name I wasn't ready to think yet. I did not know the year the relay was found, or who found it, or what came after. I was almost certain it hadn't been a company's survey probe with a gradiometer bolted to its spine.

Everything I'd learned about my own world told me that my memories were not a map. They were a sketch drawn from a distance by a man who had only ever played the game.

Only it wasn't a game. Four hundred and twelve children in a school on Callisto. A woman named Teodora Marsh handing me a roll. A mother's ledger, and the pegboard in a shop, and fourteen thousand people on this campus who showed up each morning because a man had once been right about a transducer. All of it sat on a planet that was about to be found by something larger than itself.

For eleven years I had told myself I was building a company. I had been building it inside something I hadn't recognized.

I took my hand away from the glass. There was a faint print where my palm had been, and it faded while I watched. I breathed in, held it, and breathed out, and went back to the room.

"Sorry," I said. "I needed to think."

"Of course." Voss studied me a moment longer than politeness required, and then turned back to the wall.

---

"I want to be sure I understand the constraint," Kateryna Hrytsenko said.

We were in her briefing room, the one that had been swept twice that day. I had called her in the same hour, and she had arrived without asking why. I had told her only what Voss's team had told me, which was true, and left out the rest, which wasn't mine to share yet.

"The gradiometer data is ours," I said. "It stays in a compartment. The Survey group, Marcus's people for interpretation, Dr. Voss, Kwame Asante. Nobody else, and no one on a network that touches the corporate backbone."

"That's a day's work."

"Do it tonight."

"And the sounder data?"

"We deliver it to the consortium on schedule, the way the contract says, with the anomaly flagged in the quality notes, the way we've already flagged it."

Hrytsenko didn't move. "That's an unusual choice."

"What would you do?"

"I'd hold the delivery. A week, ten days. Citing processing delays. It's a defensible reason, and by then we'd know more." She said it without apology. "The moment a graduate student in a university lab reads that return, we have a rumor, and we don't control it. I'd rather not have competitors, governments, and a dozen news services guessing at our probe's data before we understand it."

"I understand the logic."

"But?"

"But I'm not going to doctor a scientific dataset for something we haven't verified, and I'm not going to tell a consortium that trusted us with a mission that we found a reason to be late." I kept my voice level. "If we delay, and the object turns out to be what Elena thinks, then we're the people who sat on a discovery. If it turns out to be a strange rock, we're the people who panicked over a strange rock. I don't want to be either."

"The rumor will still happen."

"Probably. So I want two outside reviewers looking at the raw data before it does. Independent. One planetary geologist from the consortium's own roster, one geophysicist with no ties to us. Under confidentiality, starting immediately. Dr. Voss will choose them, not me." I held her eye. "If a rumor starts, I want an answer waiting for it."

"The reviewers will make it more likely to leak."

"They'll make it more likely to be correct."

Hrytsenko was silent for a few seconds. She had a way of being silent that made you reconsider your last sentence, and I reconsidered it, and did not change it.

"I disagree," she said finally. "I'll note it. But I'll do it your way." She made a mark on her pad. "I'm going to place two people on the reviewers' travel and communications, and I'll tell Voss that's standard. I'd also like a standing file opened on this. Anyone who asks about the probe's data, anywhere, goes into it."

"Do that. Quietly."

"Quietly is my job." She almost smiled. "You look like a man who's just been told something."

"I'm a man who's just seen an image I can't explain."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

---

I did the remaining work in the order it needed doing, which was a relief, because it left no room for the other thing.

I told Ibrahim Nasser to pull one of the six corvettes off its convoy schedule and have it fueled and provisioned for a long run to the Pluto system. He listened without expression, asked what it was for, and was told that an observation platform would be useful. He said the convoy schedule would suffer. I said I knew. It would take the corvette weeks to arrive, and there was nothing in my plan that depended on the corvette arriving fast. I wanted a crew and a set of eyes of our own at the site, belonging to professionals who could say what they saw in their own words. He wrote it down, and I could tell he'd be drawing his own conclusions.

Then I sat alone in my office with the Strategic Analysis AI and asked it a question I'd never asked it before.

"Assume that Akatsuki has evidence of a second technological civilization. Not contact. Evidence. What is our exposure?"

"Insufficient data to assess exposure to an unobserved party," it answered. "I can provide scenario categories, not probabilities. Categories: commercial competition for technology access; regulatory or governmental interest in Akatsuki's capabilities; security risk to proprietary systems; strategic vulnerability of human spacecraft capability relative to an unknown standard. I cannot rank them without observations."

It was the right answer. I thanked it, which no one had told me was unnecessary, and closed the session.

Then I opened a private session with the Knowledge Management AI, in the room behind two locked doors, and began dictating.

It took forty minutes. I told it what I remembered about a structure at the edge of the system. I told it about relays and a station and a council, and the broad outline of a human war. I told it about the thing behind all of that, in the dullest words I could find. I told it what I did not remember, which was most of it. It sorted each statement without comment. When I was done, every item sat in a single category, with a label I had given it.

_Remembered. Unverified._

"No items are classified verified," it said. "The Charon structure is externally observed but its identity is not established by any evidence in the repository. Do you want a verification plan?"

"Not yet," I said. "Hold the category open."

"Category held."

I looked at the list for a long time. Verified was a very small word. Almost nothing I knew had earned it.

---

Helena came to the residence at ten that night, which she had never done without a reason. I opened the door in my socks, and she walked past me into the kitchen and put a plastic container on the counter.

"Soup," she said. "From the cafeteria. A person made it, not a machine, so it's got character."

"I'm not hungry."

"Okonkwo says you asked him to drive slower tonight." She took two bowls from the cabinet and set them down. "You've never asked him to drive slower in nine years. He thought something was wrong with the car."

"I wanted to think."

"I know." She ladled the soup. "Sit down."

I sat. She sat across from me, not at the end of the table where she usually put herself, and pushed a bowl in my direction. The old stove clicked as it warmed itself. On the camp burner beside it, my mother's pan sat where it always sat, out of principle.

"You asked me to clear your calendar for the next ten days," Helena said. "Everything that isn't Survey, ASID, or Ibrahim. You didn't tell me why."

"I'll tell you when I can."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I have." I picked up the spoon and put it down. "I think it changes what we should be building. I can't say how yet. I want to understand it first."

"The probe."

"Yes."

"Marcus told me he'd seen something that made him feel like a first-year student." She watched me. "He doesn't use that expression."

"It's a strange object. It's a very strange object. I don't think I can say more."

Helena was quiet. She had a way of listening that made her feel like a wall you could lean on, and I'd never decided whether to be grateful for it or afraid of it.

"You can keep a secret from me," she said. "That's your right. There are a lot of things you keep, and I've never asked about the ones that were yours. But you can't keep me from noticing that you have one, and I'm not going to pretend I haven't."

"I'm not asking you to."

"Good." She took a spoonful of soup. "Then let me say what I think. You came out of that observation room looking like someone had pulled the floor out. You walked straight past three people to a window. You called Kateryna within the hour and made two decisions I'd have expected you to sleep on. And now you're sitting at your own table in your socks, and you haven't asked me a single question about the schedule."

"I asked you to clear it."

"You told me. That's different." She set down the spoon. "I'm not worried about the decisions. They were sensible. I'm worried that you're about to turn something into a project and disappear into it, and nobody's going to be in the room when you do."

"I'm not going to disappear."

"You said that about the dinner with the procurement delegation." Her voice stayed level. "I'm not accusing you. I'm saying that I know what it looks like when you start building, and I'd like to be somewhere nearby when it happens."

"That's fair."

"It isn't fair. It's accurate." She looked at the bowl in front of me. "And I disagree with one thing. You wanted to move the corvette tonight. You wanted it out of the yard tomorrow. Ibrahim told me. I told him to take three days."

"Helena."

"Nothing about this requires a decision tonight. If the object is what Elena thinks, it's been there a very long time. It will be there on Tuesday." She said it gently. "If it isn't, you'll have spent a corvette's time on a rock. In either case the right sequence is the one where the reviewers look first and you act second."

I started to argue, and found I couldn't. It was the sequence I'd have chosen on any other day.

"Three days," I said.

"Thank you."

"You're annoyingly good at this."

"It's what I'm paid for." She picked up her spoon again. "Eat. Then, whenever you can, tell me what you're allowed to tell me. Not before."

I ate. The soup was good, and had character, and somewhere near the end of the bowl I realized I'd stopped being cold. Helena said nothing, which was the best thing she could have said. When I looked up, she had already begun stacking the dishes.

"I think we may not be alone," I said.

I hadn't meant to say it. It sat in the kitchen between us, plain and small.

Helena didn't blink. "Is that the thing you can tell me?"

"It's the thing I can tell you."

"Then I'll treat it as the thing." She took the bowls to the sink. After a moment, with her back to me and the water running, she said, "Tell me when you need someone to say it's real."

I didn't answer. She hadn't asked me to.

---

After she left, I went out to the yard.

My mother's garden had been mostly gravel and a lemon tree that had never fruited. I'd kept the tree. The night was clear and cold, with the city's light low along the horizon, and above it a field of stars that I'd seen ten thousand times and never once looked at properly.

I couldn't see Pluto. It was nothing you could find without an instrument, a faint dot among faint dots, and it was nothing at all to the naked eye. It didn't matter. I stood there with my hands in my pockets and looked at the place where it would be.

Everything I'd built was still true. The company was real, and so were the people in it. The shop, the settlement, and the six ships were real, and so were the schools and the clinics and the wages. I hadn't stopped caring about any of it. But the question that had organized my days for eleven years had been how to build something worth building, and that question had just acquired a much larger one standing behind it.

I knew enough to be afraid, and not enough to know what of.

That was something I could work with. My mother had taught me that. _First you look at what's in your way,_ she'd said. _Then you decide what to do about it._

I went back inside, took a pad from the kitchen drawer, and sat down at the table where Helena's empty bowl was still drying. At the top of the page I wrote a single word, and underlined it twice.

_Verify._

Then I started the list.
