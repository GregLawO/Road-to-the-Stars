**Chapter 1: Getting Started**

The truck had already been to two other shops, and it was still dying on the road.

"It runs fine for ten minutes," Mr. Okafor said, standing well back from the open hood with his arms folded. "Then it just quits. Not a sputter, nothing. Like somebody flipped a switch. The last place replaced the entire drive battery, and it did it again the next morning."

"How much did they charge you for the battery?"

He told me. I winced, and he nodded like a man who had already done his wincing.

I slid out from under the front of the truck and wiped my hands on a rag that had stopped being clean sometime last year. The diagnostic tablet on the workbench showed what it had shown the last four times I ran the test: no fault codes, a drive system reporting healthy. It was my mother's old tablet, the stylus tip wrapped in electrical tape where she'd chewed it. That, plus the way the truck failed (only when warm, only under load, and no differently with a brand-new pack in it) pointed away from the battery. The controller wasn't registering a failure. Something was making it _think_ it had lost a signal for a moment, and when it lost the signal, it cut power to be safe.

"Give me a minute," I said.

I put the truck on the lift, lowered the underbody shield, and went over the main harness with a flashlight, one hand following the wiring the way my mother had taught me when I was twelve: _Don't look for the broken thing. Look for the thing that's rubbing against something._ She'd written it in marker on the inside of the parts-room door, above the light switch, where she could read it every time she turned the light on. The harness ran from the controller along the frame rail, past the coolant pump bracket, toward the rear axle motor. Everything looked fine until I pressed on the loom near the bracket and felt it shift against the metal.

There. The outer sheath had worn thin where it touched a sharp edge. Under it, one of the signal lines was flattened, its insulation chafed to a shine. When the truck warmed up and the pump vibrated, the line pressed against the bracket and shorted for a fraction of a second, just long enough.

I called Mr. Okafor over and showed him with the flashlight.

He stared at it. "That's it? That little bit of wire?"

"That little bit of wire. The new pack didn't change anything because the pack was never the problem."

"I paid for a new battery."

"I know. I'd call them and ask for a refund, if I were you. I'll write you a note saying the fault didn't change after the replacement, and what the actual cause was." I was already cutting out the damaged section. "I'll splice and re-sheath the line, then file down the bracket and put a grommet on it so it doesn't happen again. Forty minutes."

"What do I owe you?"

I named a price that covered the parts, the time, and not much else. He raised his eyebrows. "That's it?"

"It's a splice and a grommet, Mr. Okafor. I'm not going to charge you for a battery I didn't install."

He laughed, the first time since he'd walked in, and his wristband chirped at him about a delivery window. He silenced it and went to sit in the waiting area with a cup of the terrible coffee my mother had left behind in a machine I never had the heart to replace. She'd bought it used the year I was born and claimed the taste was character.

The shop was the same shop she had run for twenty-two years: three bays, a lift in each, a parts room that smelled of rubber and cutting oil, and a sign out front that still said HIMURA AUTO in faded red letters. Outside the open bay door, a municipal bus slid past without a driver, and the street's traffic parted around it like water. It was the fall of 2137. I was twenty, I'd been running the shop alone since the spring, and I'd learned that the people who came through the door didn't care that I was young. They cared whether the truck stopped dying.

Forty minutes later, it didn't. I took it around the block twice with the load monitor on, pulled it back into the bay, and handed Mr. Okafor the keys along with the note.

"You're Linda Himura's boy," he said, taking them.

"Yes, sir."

"She would've found that in ten minutes."

"She would've found it in five," I said. "And she'd have told you off for letting someone replace the battery first."

He grinned and shook my hand. "I'll send people."

I watched him drive away. The truck ran fine down the whole length of the street.

Then I pulled the bay door down, locked the front, flipped the sign, and looked at the clock. Just past four. I had three hours before the parts order and the books. I took the stairs up to the loft above the parts room instead.

---

The loft had been storage for as long as I could remember. For the last few months it had been something else.

Most of what filled it had come from my mother: the steel worktable, the mill and lathe, the welding station, the good oscilloscope with a shelf above it where she kept her reference tables and a cast-iron surface plate under a dish cloth, and a pegboard where every tool hung inside an outline she'd drawn for it in marker. She'd never thrown away a tool in her life. The rest I'd added a piece at a time since the spring: a rack of salvaged electronics, two 3D printers, two desktop workstations, and a diagnostic server I'd pulled out of a dead dealership and talked back to life.

I sat down with a pad and a pen, because writing things by hand made me slow down and be honest. My mother had been the same way. Her ledger still sat on the corner of the table, a green cloth-bound book she'd kept in parallel with the shop's software for twenty-two years because she didn't trust anything she couldn't hold. Her last entry was dated in March: _shop towels, 2 boxes._ I'd started my own page directly below it.

First, the money.

The shop made enough. After parts, utilities, insurance, the property tax on the building and the house, and a modest amount for groceries and myself, there was a little left each month. The savings account she'd left was healthy for a twenty-year-old and pathetic for anything I had in mind. I wrote the numbers down anyway, because numbers don't care how you feel about them. I could fund a few hundred hours of experimentation and a few thousand in parts without touching the shop's operating cushion, and I wasn't willing to touch the cushion. The shop kept me independent. It wasn't going on the table.

Second, what I could do with the equipment. The loft was a good workspace for one person. It was not a laboratory. I didn't have precision sensors, sterile fabrication, medical-grade materials, real fluid-handling hardware, or enough computing power to run serious simulations without waiting a week for each one. I wrote _Need_ at the top of a column, and it filled faster than I liked.

The gap between what I knew and what I could build was enormous. I knew how a lot of impossible things worked, in the sense that I could have drawn the diagrams and explained the principles. But knowing how something works and having the tools to make it are different problems, and the second one has no shortcuts. I wasn't going to wake up with a gravitic drive on the workbench because I understood the physics. I was going to get there the way my mother had taken a failing garage and made it a three-bay shop: one finished job at a time.

So the question wasn't what I could build. It was what I could build _first_, with what I had, that would pay for building the next thing.

I started a list.

---

The list had four lines.

**Gravitic systems.** I wrote it, looked at it, and crossed it off before I'd finished underlining it. I'd need fabrication tolerances and power systems that a three-bay auto shop couldn't dream of. That was a decade away, and it would take a real industrial base.

**Robotics.** Closer, but not close enough. I could build a basic robotic arm for the shop tomorrow. What I wanted was something that could assemble and maintain itself, which meant precision manufacturing, specialized components, and a supply chain. A long road, and one that wouldn't pay for itself at the start.

**Specialized AI.** I circled it. It had no manufacturing problem, just hardware and a great deal of work, and everything else I wanted depended on it. But nobody was going to pay me for a research tool.

**Med-Pods.**

I looked at that last one for longer than the others.

The concept was simple to state and fiendishly hard to build: a self-contained unit that could assess a patient, diagnose what was wrong, and treat it with minimal human intervention. Scan the body, find the damage, repair it, monitor the results. The version I knew could make a trauma surgeon weep. Mine wouldn't, not for years. Mine would be crude and slow and limited to what I could actually test.

But it had everything the others didn't.

People needed it. Nobody on this planet was short of medical problems, and the people who dealt with them were short of time, hands, and equipment. Even a fraction of routine diagnostics and wound care, automated, would be worth a fortune to hospitals, clinics, and remote settlements.

It could also be built in stages. A sensor array first, then the diagnostic software, then the treatment modules, each useful on its own. A reliable diagnostic scanner was a product. A wound-closure module was a product. Every stage I finished could be sold or licensed, or could attract the people and money the next stage needed. And medical technology meant hospitals, researchers, regulators, and specialists, every one of them a path to a bigger lab and people who knew things I didn't.

I drew a box around _Med-Pods_ and sat back.

I wasn't going to pretend it was easy. Imaging, diagnostics, precision actuation, fluid and drug delivery, tissue repair, a sterile enclosure, patient monitoring, safety interlocks: each was its own project, and each would fail at least twice before it worked.

And none of it would go anywhere near a living person until it had earned that. Bench tests, phantoms, and simulations first, and enough of them to trust. After that, only through proper clinical trials: medical professionals in the room, safety procedures, oversight, and the authorization to run them. I wrote it at the top of the page and underlined it. A medical device that hadn't been validated wasn't a product. It was a liability with a power cord.

I broke the project into pages, one for each major system. Sensing came first, because everything else depended on seeing the problem. I labeled the first page _Scanner: imaging and vitals_ and started listing components. It took about an hour to get that page right. By the second, I'd stopped listing components and started listing _calculations_, and that was where the problem showed up.

Sensor fusion models. Tissue-density mapping. Thermal modeling for the actuators. Tolerance stacks for every moving part. A few hundred cross-references between systems that all depended on each other, and documentation exact enough that someone else could follow every decision.

I did a rough estimate of the hours. Then I did it again, because the first answer was ridiculous. Done by hand, between customers, it would take years. The work wasn't just hard. It was _wide_.

I put the pen down and looked at the two workstations in the corner. The answer had been sitting there for an hour.

---

I didn't need a bigger brain. I needed something tireless to organize the information, do the routine arithmetic, and keep the documentation straight, so my time went to the decisions only I could make.

I'd always preferred to automate anything a machine could do. My mother used to tease me about it, usually while I was rigging something to sort her bolts by size. _You'd build a robot to tie your shoes if it saved you five seconds._ She wasn't wrong.

What I wanted wasn't a person in a box. A thinking, wanting, deciding machine was a different kind of problem, with different consequences, and it had nothing to do with what I needed. I needed a tool, in the same sense as the lathe, only much better at arithmetic and filing. Tonight I could build only the groundwork for one: a place to keep everything I measured, a way to queue and track jobs, a documentation system that could turn my terrible handwriting into something searchable, and the simplest calculation routines to start with. The rest was a long way off. Engineering simulations, tolerance checking, and any real analysis of a design would need models I hadn't written, tested against results I hadn't measured yet, on hardware I didn't have. A specialized system that did a defined set of jobs well and stayed inside its lane was the destination. Tonight was the first layer of it.

The hardware was the catch. The workstations were respectable for their age and nowhere near enough. I could build the foundation with what I had, but anything involving serious simulation would crawl, and some of it wouldn't run at all. I wrote _Compute upgrade_ at the bottom of the _Need_ column, and under it, _used server blades?_ Salvaged hardware wouldn't cost much if I was careful where I bought it. So I wouldn't try to build the finished system. I'd build the first layer, make it good, and add to it as money allowed, the same way the shop had grown.

I drew the architecture on a fresh page. A core that managed tasks and data. Separate modules for each function, so I could improve one without breaking the others. Strict permissions, so each module touched only what it needed. A log of everything it did. And a rule in block capitals at the top: _NOTHING IT PRODUCES GOES INTO A DESIGN UNTIL I'VE CHECKED IT._

It was only a skeleton, but it was a skeleton I could build tonight.

My wristband buzzed. It had noticed I hadn't eaten since noon, which was more than I'd done. Through the loft window the sky had gone the color of a bruise. I went down to the kitchen of the house my mother had left me, and the stove started warming itself the moment I opened the fridge, as it had been doing for years. She'd hated that feature and kept a cast-iron pan on a camp burner beside it out of principle. I ate leftovers standing up beside the pan and went back to work.

---

The core came first. Before any of the clever parts, I needed something that could take in information, store it sensibly, and hand it back in useful form. I cleared the first workstation, set up a clean partition, and wrote the data structures before anything else, because if those were wrong, everything built on them would be wrong too. The second workstation I wired in as a dedicated worker, so heavy jobs wouldn't freeze my screen.

The dealership server fought me. It ran an ancient operating system on a stubborn storage controller, and I lost forty minutes arguing with it before it admitted it had disks. When it finally came up, I formatted it and gave it its first assignment: store everything. Nothing I produced would live on only one machine again.

Around ten, I stopped for coffee. The good coffee, from the kitchen, not the machine downstairs. I drank it on the stool at my mother's worktable, under her pegboard, with the radio she'd left tuned to a station that played nothing but old rock turned down to a murmur. I hadn't changed the station. I wasn't sure I would.

Then I opened a blank file and wrote the first real entry into what would become the project's knowledge base. Not a calculation. A requirement.

_Med-Pod project, scanner subsystem. Objective: noninvasive imaging and vital-sign assessment sufficient to localize and characterize injury._

Under it, I typed the first questions the system would eventually need to help answer. How to combine multiple sensor types into one picture. How to estimate tissue density from indirect measurements. How to know when an answer wasn't good enough and say so instead of guessing.

That last one mattered most. A system that was wrong and sure of itself was worse than no system at all. Whatever I built on this framework would have to keep _I measured this_ separate from _I think this_, and the place to start was the data structure. Every entry got a field for where it came from.

The framework compiled on the third try. I queued the first test job, a trivial calculation, and watched the task manager pick it up, hand it to the worker, log each step, and return the right answer in a fraction of a second. I queued ten more. Then fifty, a few of them deliberately malformed. It rejected the bad ones, logged why, and finished the rest in order, and every result was on the dealership server before the screen finished refreshing.

It was a machine adding numbers correctly. It couldn't design anything, or judge anything, or tell me whether a single result was worth trusting. But a job went in, a record of it came out, and none of it depended on my remembering anything. This was the part every later piece would hang from, and it had held on the first real try. I sat back on the stool and let myself have a moment with the idea. Then I reached for the pad, because the next thing needed doing.

I flipped back to the Med-Pod pages and started feeding the first of them in, one note at a time. The sorting routine filed them, tagged them, and flagged two entries under the same tag with values that didn't match. They were two of my own estimates, written a week apart. I'd been too tired to notice the conflict. I fixed it. It was exactly the kind of mistake I'd worried about, caught on the very first night.

The fans hummed. Downstairs, the shop was dark and quiet, and in the morning there would be a customer with a bad alternator and an invoice that needed sending. I'd do both. The shop still kept me honest and fed.

But tonight there was a long road ahead and a short list of things to build first. I turned the radio up a notch, pulled the keyboard closer, and went back to work.
