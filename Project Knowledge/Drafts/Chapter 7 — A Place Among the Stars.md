**Chapter 7 — A Place Among the Stars**

"You're early," Lucia Ferreira said.

"We had a good transit."

"You don't get good transits at this distance. You get shorter bad ones." She took my duffel before I could object and handed it to a cargo droid waiting at her elbow, which rolled off toward the guest quarters without being told where they were. "Nine days is the fastest I've seen a passenger run in, and your pilot is going to want to tell you about it."

"He already did. For about four of the nine."

"Then you've heard it." She started walking, and I fell in beside her. The arrivals hall was a long, low corridor of sprayed insulation and grated floor, lit in the warm white the settlement's lighting engineers had settled on after the first residents complained about blue. Strips of dosimeter badges hung in a rack by the inner door, one for each person who came and went from the surface, and a technician in a work suit was feeding hers into a reader. It beeped green. "Dr. Osei wants twenty minutes before your afternoon session. Baek wants you at the Ice Works while the plant is still open, because he says if you don't see it he'll never convince you. And I've put you in the ring tonight."

"The ring? I usually take the guest module."

"The guest module is full. Freight crews from the Ganymede run, six days stuck waiting for a tug." She glanced sideways at me. "And I'd like you to sleep in a place where your neighbors have children."

On the little display by the inner door, a message from Helena had been waiting since I docked. _Eight hours. I will know._ I'd never found out how.

---

Callisto had never been a pretty place, and the settlement hadn't tried to make it one. It sat on the broad cratered plain where the moon's gentler radiation environment had made it worth building, sixty kilometers from the nearest ridge. From the arrivals dome you could see the whole of it in one look: the squat pressurized halls of the industrial district, the steel lattices of the power field, the long white scar of the ice haul road, and beyond them the orbital yard's cradle lights burning in the black. Jupiter hung low over the horizon, enormous and banded and, after nine years of visits, unremarkable.

We took the tram. It ran in a buried tube, because everything on Callisto that people used ran in a buried tube. The surface was for machines, and for the humans who had reason to be among them with a dosimeter on their chest and a count of hours in their record. Two meters of regolith and ice over every habitat took the radiation out of the equation, or most of it. Solar storms were another matter, and every resident carried a storm-shelter assignment as automatically as a name. A drill was posted for Thursday.

The tram was crowded. A man in a pressure-suit liner sat with a sleeping child on his chest while his wife argued quietly with a dispatcher on a handset. A pair of teenagers in school jackets compared something on a tablet and fell silent when they noticed me, then resumed more quietly. Two maintenance droids stood against the far wall in standby, shoulder to shoulder with a woman eating a sandwich. Nobody spoke to them and nobody had to. It had taken me a long time to learn that the best thing automation could do was be unremarkable.

"Baek's tired," Lucia said, over the noise of the rails. "He's been up since the shutdown started. Be kind."

"I'm always kind."

"You're always direct. They're not the same."

---

The Ice Works occupied an enormous vaulted chamber at the end of the haul road, and it was quiet in a way no operating plant should be. The conveyor lines hung motionless, and the thermal loops that normally hummed through the walls were dark. Two of the plant's maintenance droids stood on a gantry above the main separator vessel, holding a section of cowling open like waiters.

Jun-ho Baek met us at the base of the stairs. He ran industrial operations for the settlement, an eighteen-year veteran of terrestrial mining who had been the third person to move to Callisto and had never once said he regretted it. He had a gray stubble, a coverall with the sleeves cut off at the elbow, and the expression of a man who has found something he doesn't like.

"The fix isn't the fix," he said.

"Good morning to you too," I said.

"Morning is a convention." He led us up the stairs. "Lucia told you about the seal."

"A recurring fault. Four days to replace the failed unit."

"That was the plan. My team opened the vessel on day one, pulled the failed seal, and found this." He pointed to a gleaming ring of polymer lying on a tray under a lamp. "That's the one that failed. And that's the one next to it, which hasn't." The second seal looked identical to my eye. Baek tilted the lamp. "Look at the surface."

I looked. A fine crazing ran across the face of the second one, like old paint.

"Thermal cycling," he said. "The compound degrades from the inside. The one that failed was the first to go. The rest are right behind it. The inspection software rated all fourteen healthy two weeks ago, because the software looks for deformation, and this stuff doesn't deform. It goes brittle." He set the tray down. "Nobody was wrong. The droids did the job they were assigned. The model that should have caught this didn't have it as a failure mode, because it's a failure mode we only get in ice-processing conditions, and nobody on Earth has run ice at our duty cycle."

"What do you want to do?"

"Replace all fourteen. Now. While the vessel is open." He crossed his arms. "I don't have fourteen. I have six. Freight has the rest, and the rest is on a tug that's two days out and held at Ganymede by a docking queue. If I wait for it, the plant stays dark for eight more days, and the ring's water reserve drops under the margin Lucia set. If I patch the worst eight and run on six new ones, I'm betting that the remaining eight last the thirty days until the next freight window."

"And if they don't?"

"Then I get to do this again at the worst possible time." He said it without heat. "I'm not asking you to choose, because you shouldn't. I'm telling you what I'm going to do, which is patch, and I'm telling you why I don't like it."

I looked at the tray for a while. The seal compound was the kind of thing that would have taken me a day to find with the instruments I had in the loft, and it had taken Baek's team an afternoon because they were looking at the thing itself. I didn't have anything to add to his analysis, and I had learned long ago not to pretend that I did.

"Your call," I said. "If I'm hearing you right, the water margin is the real constraint."

"It's the only one."

"Then you've already decided correctly. I'd add one thing." I turned to Lucia. "Can we get the tug moved up the docking queue?"

"I've asked twice," she said. "It's a Ganymede operator's queue, not ours."

"Then ask again, and tell them what's on the tug. Seals for a settlement water plant. That's a different category than ore." I looked at Baek. "And log the failure mode. Send the crazing data to Marcus's group, so the inspection model gets it before the next plant does."

"It's already logged," Baek said, and for the first time since I'd arrived, something in his shoulders eased. "I just wanted a human to read it."

---

Dr. Adaeze Osei had set up her clinic on the second tier of the ring's central concourse, in a space that still smelled faintly of the paint they'd used to turn a storage room into an exam room. She was a pediatrician who had come for a two-year contract nine years ago and stayed, and she had acquired, along the way, the habit of treating the settlement's administrators as patients who would not follow instructions.

"Sit," she said. "Not there. The chair, not the exam table. You're not my patient."

I sat. She sat across from me with a tablet and did not smile.

"The new ring," she said. "I've seen the engineering proposal. It specifies a spin gravity of thirty-eight percent of Earth's. The same as the first ring."

"That's what the first ring's bearings are rated for. Same design, lower cost."

"I know why it's the same." She turned the tablet around. A chart showed a cloud of dots, rising from left to right, with a shaded band across the middle. "Those are our children. Bone mineral density against age, for the ones born on Callisto or who arrived before the age of four. The band is the Earth reference. We started tracking the first cohort six years ago, and they have a trend I don't like." She traced the line with a fingertip. "They're not in danger. We have an exercise program, and the children who attend it stay close to the band. The ones who don't fall away. It's slow, and it's cumulative, and I don't have enough data to tell you what it means at age forty."

"What are you asking for?"

"Sixty percent." She said it flatly. "The new ring spins at sixty, and the school wing and the nursery sections are built there. The first ring stays as the adult habitat, and the families move to the new ring over the next decade. I'd prefer the transition to be faster. But I understand how construction works."

I looked at the chart for a long time. "Baek will say sixty percent changes the bearing design."

"Baek has already said so." She didn't blink. "Eleven weeks and a considerable amount of money. He sent me the number. I'm not ignorant of the cost, Mr. Himura. I'm asking you to weigh it against something that doesn't have a number yet."

"And if I choose thirty-eight?"

"Then I'll build a better exercise program, and I'll lose some of the children who don't come, and I'll tell you in ten years what it cost." She laid the tablet on her knee. "I'm not threatening to quit, if that's what you're wondering. I'm telling you what I'll see, and I'll see it either way."

I sat there and looked at the dots. I could have asked her a dozen questions about the sampling and the reference band, and she'd have answered them, and she'd have been right to be a little impatient. She'd done the work. What was left was mine to decide.

"I'll give you an answer at the meeting," I said.

"Fine." She stood. "Eat something first. You look like you've been awake for nine days."

---

The meeting was at 1700, in a conference room that had once been Lucia's office before the settlement outgrew it. Lucia sat at the head of the table, Baek on her left, Osei on her right, and I took the end, with a view of the screen on the wall where Baek had put up the bearing loads.

"The short version," Baek said, "is that thirty-eight percent works with the existing bearing design, and sixty doesn't. For sixty we need the heavy bearing set, which has a lead time. I can get it from Earth in forty days if I pay for the priority run, and the ring construction slips about eleven weeks while the heavy set is qualified. The cost is in the packet."

"What does eleven weeks do to the yard?" I asked.

He paused. It was the pause of a man who had been hoping not to be asked. "The yard's expansion shares its construction crews with the ring. We have two repair cradles, a corvette is in one of them now for hull repair, and the yard extension was supposed to give us a third by the end of the year. If the ring slips, the extension slips with it. That means two cradles for the whole fleet and a longer queue for every vessel that needs service."

"How long?"

"Two months, probably three."

"That's a real cost," Lucia said. She said it to the table in general. "I want it noted that it's a real cost. We're not choosing between a cost and no cost."

"Noted," I said.

"I'll add something." Lucia leaned forward. "I know Dr. Osei's data. I've read it. But the first ring has been running for nine years and the children in it are healthy. Every parent in the settlement could tell you that. If we delay the ring, the waiting list gets longer, and families who might have come don't. They go to a station in the inner system that has gravity at one-tenth and no school at all. I'm not saying the data's wrong. I'm saying the cost of waiting falls on people who aren't in this room."

"The cost of not waiting falls on people who aren't in this room too," Osei said. "Seven-year-olds."

"I know that."

"I know you know."

Neither of them looked away. Baek stared at the bearing charts as though the answer might be in the margins.

I let the silence run a little longer than was comfortable. Everyone at the table was right. Lucia was right that a longer waiting list had victims. Baek was right about the yard. Osei was right about the children. And all of them knew it, which was why none of them was trying to win.

"Sixty percent," I said.

Lucia breathed out, not quite a sigh.

"I want to be clear about why." I looked at Osei. "The data isn't proof. It's a trend with six years of history. But if I'm wrong and build for thirty-eight, I can't fix it later. A ring isn't something you rebuild. If I'm wrong and build for sixty, I've spent eleven weeks and some money I can recover." I turned to Baek. "I know what it costs you. I need the yard's third cradle delayed no more than eight weeks, not eleven. Find me three weeks. I don't care how. Reassign crews, buy outside contractors, whatever you need. If you can't, tell me, and we'll talk about it."

"I'll find them," Baek said, with the flat voice of a man who intended to be furious about it later. "It won't be pretty."

"I'm not asking for pretty." I looked at Lucia. "The waiting list. I'd like a bridging plan. Whatever you can do inside the first ring: converting storage, subdividing units, a temporary module. I don't want families turned away, I want them told the truth about the timeline."

"I'll draft one tonight," she said. "It won't make anyone happy."

"Then we've done it right."

Osei was writing something on her tablet. She didn't look up. "Thank you," she said, and I could tell she'd rather have been able to say it more simply.

---

By the time the meeting ended, the settlement had shifted into its evening. Lucia had been right about the guest module. A freight crew from the Ganymede run was camped in the concourse commons, loud and cheerful and four days overdue for a shower, and at the center of them a woman in a faded flight jacket sat on a table with her boots on a bench, telling the story of her docking queue to anyone who would listen.

"Eleven hours," she was saying. "Eleven hours in a holding orbit with a hold full of polymer seals, and the controller kept calling me _the tug_. I have a name. It's on the hull."

I stopped at the edge of the group. She saw me, gave me a once-over, and grinned. "You're the founder."

"Today I'm the guy who needs your seals."

"The ones in the crate? Those are yours?" She swung her boots down. "Marta Quist. Independent. I've been hauling to Callisto for six years, and nobody's ever told me what was in the crates, only where to put them." She raised her mug. "Seals for a water plant. That's a better cargo than ore."

"That's what I told Ganymede."

"They moved me up forty minutes ago." She tilted her head. "That was you?"

"That was Lucia. I made some noise."

"It worked." She looked around the commons, at the dozen conversations going on at once, the smell of something frying, the clatter of trays. "You know why I keep coming back? Most settlements out here, the freight crews eat in the cargo bay. Here they've got a place with chairs." She shrugged. "A place with chairs is rare."

I left her to her story and walked the length of the concourse. The ring turned under my feet with the faint, constant sensation that I'd stopped noticing years ago, a slight lean in the horizon when I turned my head too fast. The commons gave way to a row of shopfronts: a laundry, a repair counter, a barber with a hand-lettered sign, a tiny shop selling nothing but spare parts for household droids. The supply for most of it came in on the same freight lines as the polymer seals. The settlement's hydroponic bays fed the community perhaps sixty percent of what it ate, and the rest arrived in sealed pallets from places I had never visited. Callisto wasn't self-sufficient and had never pretended to be. The bakery at the end of the concourse made its flour from wheat grown in the bays, and its yeast from a culture someone had carried up from Earth in a coat pocket nine years ago. The oven was imported. The woman who ran it, a broad, quiet person in her fifties named Teodora Marsh, was closing for the night when I reached the counter.

"Mr. Himura," she said. "I wasn't expecting you."

"I wasn't expecting me either." I leaned on the counter. "Is there anything left?"

"A little." She boxed two rolls without asking what kind I wanted. "On the house. The administrator said you'd be hungry."

"Did she?"

"She said you'd skipped lunch." Marsh wiped her hands on her apron. "She said you always do when there's a decision."

I laughed, and she smiled, and for a moment neither of us said anything. A child's laughter echoed from somewhere down the concourse, high and bright, followed by a parent's voice calling a name.

"How long have you been here?" I asked.

"Eight years. My husband came first. I followed him with the yeast." She looked at the rolls in my hands. "We came for the pay. That's the honest answer. He's a power engineer, there was a signing bonus, and we had a daughter in college who needed it. We planned to stay three years."

"What changed?"

"Nothing, really." She considered. "The second year my daughter had a baby, and she sent me a picture, and I realized I'd have to learn her son's face through a screen. And I thought, well, I'll have to go back. And then I walked out into the concourse that morning, and Mrs. Adeyinka from three doors down saw me crying, and she didn't say a thing. She just put a cup of tea in my hands and sat down beside me. And I thought, I don't know anybody like this on Earth." She shrugged. "That's not an answer, exactly."

"It sounds like one."

"The walls are yours, Mr. Himura. I know that. The air, too, and the pay. But nobody sent Mrs. Adeyinka with that tea." She closed the register. "I don't think you can build that. I think you can only make room for it."

I looked at the rolls, still warm in the paper, and at the long curve of the concourse rising away on both sides of us, the lit windows and the people moving between them. I had signed the paper that put the walls there. I had never once been to Mrs. Adeyinka's door.

"Thank you," I said.

"Eat the roll," said Teodora Marsh. "Before it's cold."

---

I slept eight hours, and Helena would know.

In the morning the ring woke the way it always did, with the lights rising slowly from amber to white, and the sound of doors opening along the corridor. Someone's kettle whistled. A droid went by on the walkway outside my window, carrying a crate of something, and stopped at the corner to let a line of children cross.

They were on their way to school, a stream of them in bright jackets, bouncing a little as they walked the way children do when they've never known anything else. Somewhere ahead of them a bell rang, thin and clear, the same bell I'd heard behind Lucia's voice on a recording two weeks earlier, from seven hundred million kilometers and thirty-nine minutes away.

I stood at the window and watched them go. Then I went to find Baek, because he owed me three weeks, and I wanted him to know I thought he could find them.
