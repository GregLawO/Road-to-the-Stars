**Chapter 9 — Among Our Own**

Helena Vasquez took the tablet out of my hands at 5:40, and she did it the way she did everything, without ceremony.

"That's the procurement summary," I said.

"That's the procurement summary at twenty to six on a Friday." She tucked it under her arm. "Sofia has read it. The Strategic Analysis AI has read it. Ibrahim has read it twice, which means it is the best-read document in the company. It will survive until Monday."

"It flagged a variance in the actuator supply."

"It flagged a variance of point four percent." She held the office door open. "The Executive Assistant has cleared your calendar through Sunday morning. It was very efficient about it. I'd like you to be efficient about leaving."

"Sunday morning?"

"Dolores is seventy on Saturday. Ayesha is throwing the party tonight because Dolores refuses to be thrown a party on the actual day. You were invited three weeks ago." She looked at me over her glasses. "You said yes. You said it to Ayesha's face, at the shop, while holding a transmission filter."

I had a vague memory of the filter. "I remember saying I'd try."

"Okonkwo has the car running, and I have your jacket." She handed it to me. "If you'd like to bring something, there's a cake in the trunk. I picked it up myself. It is not from the company caterer."

"You bought a cake."

"I bought a cake," Helena said. "Because someone has to, and it was never going to be you."

I put on the jacket. Behind me, the ceiling said, "Your evening schedule contains no scheduled items. Do you want any messages held for review tonight?"

"Hold everything except security escalations."

"Understood. Security escalations will be delivered to the Chief of Staff first."

I looked at Helena. "Did you arrange that?"

"Obviously."

---

Himura Auto had its bay doors open and a string of work lights hung across the ceiling, and the place was louder than I had heard it in years. Somebody had dragged the mismatched waiting-room chairs into a horseshoe around the center lift, which had been lowered and covered with a sheet of plywood and a plastic tablecloth. The radio on the parts-room shelf was playing the old rock station at a volume my mother would have called disrespectful. Three pizza boxes sat on the plywood, already half-raided.

"He lives," Ayesha said, from behind a stack of paper plates. She was in her forties, short and quick, with grease permanently ingrained in the creases of her knuckles, and she had run the shop for nine years without once asking me what I thought of how she did it. "I had money on you cancelling."

"Who was holding the money?"

"Dolores. She said you'd come." Ayesha took the cake from Helena with obvious relief and set it down on the parts counter. "I said you'd send a droid with a card. She said I didn't know you. I'm paying her twenty dollars."

"I'd have sent a person with a card."

"That's worse."

The room was smaller than my memory of it. There were perhaps fourteen people. Ayesha's five mechanics stood in a loose knot near the pizza, and two of them were arguing about a football score. Gloria Castellanos sat in the best chair with a paper plate balanced on her knee, still in a gray coat, still neat, her hair now white and cut shorter. She raised two fingers to me without getting up. Marcus Hale leaned on the lift with a beer, talking to Naomi Reyes, and from the way Naomi's arms were folded I could tell they were fighting about something and enjoying it.

Okonkwo had followed me in and was hovering beside the door. "I'll wait with the car, sir."

"You'll eat," Helena said, without turning around. "There is a pizza with olives on it, and no one else will touch it. I ordered it for you."

Okonkwo looked at me. I shrugged. "She ordered it for you."

"Yes, sir." He took a plate. It was the first time I'd seen him smile in a room.

I went around the horseshoe and said hello to everyone, carefully. The first year after the enhancements started, I'd cracked a doorframe by shaking a surgeon's hand. I had learned to modulate, but I still held my handshakes about two-thirds of the way, like a man carrying something full to the brim. Gloria noticed. She always did.

"You're doing the thing with your hand," she said.

"I'm being gentle."

"You're being a stage magician. You may as well say 'watch closely.'" She tapped the plate on her knee. "Walter sent a card. It's on the counter. He enclosed an invoice for services rendered in 2137, itemized, with interest."

"How much is the interest?"

"He compounded it quarterly. He's retired in Tucson and he's still billing us." She smiled thinly. "I think it's the happiest he's ever been."

---

Dolores sat at the head of the horseshoe on a stool someone had padded with a folded moving blanket, in a clean flannel shirt and her good boots. She had a paper crown on her head that Ayesha's mechanics had cut from a shop towel. She wore it with the dignity of a woman who had decided that complaining would only encourage them.

"You came," she said.

"Ayesha said you had a bet."

"I always have a bet." She looked me up and down. "You look better than Monday."

"Helena took my tablet."

"Good for Helena." She reached up and patted my arm. It was a light pat, a pat she would never have given a hurting man. "Sit. You're blocking the light."

I sat on a stool near her. For about half an hour I did what I almost never get to do, which was nothing. Gloria told a story about a patent examiner in Munich who had once rejected an application because he disliked the font. Marcus told one about his daughter learning to drive in the test hall's parking area and clipping a coolant cart, and then the two of them argued over which of them had been the worse student driver. One of the mechanics asked if it was true that the corporate cafeteria had a robot that made omelets to order. Helena, from the parts counter, said yes, and that it made them better than anyone in the room, and a mechanic said that was a lie, and Helena said she had the data. Someone threw a paper plate at her. She caught it without looking up.

Marcus drifted over, still holding the beer. "Naomi says the night-shift coil winding was her idea."

"It was my idea," Naomi said, from across the circle, without turning her head.

"I proposed the night schedule in January."

"You proposed a night schedule that violated my clean-line protocol in four places. I proposed the version that worked."

"I proposed the version you then called your own."

"You see," Marcus told me, with immense satisfaction, "this is why I don't take her out in public."

"You took her to a wedding in March."

"She corrected the officiant."

"He misused the word 'cleave,'" Naomi said.

I laughed, loud enough that Okonkwo looked up from his pizza, and I realized I'd been waiting for it all week. Nobody in this room was trying to impress me. A man could be disagreed with on a plain Friday, about words and coil schedules, and nothing in the building needed him to decide.

---

It was after the cake, which Ayesha cut into pieces too large for the plates, that Dolores raised her fork.

"Before anybody gets sentimental," she said, "I'll say what I've told Naomi. I'm not doing it."

The room quieted, a little. Naomi, near the lift, closed her eyes.

"Doing what?" I asked.

"Naomi mentioned my hip." Dolores waved the fork. "She was supposed to mention it to nobody, and she mentioned it to you."

"I mentioned it to Dr. Sen, who is a doctor," Naomi said. "He mentioned it to Helena. Who mentioned it to Kenji."

"Everybody's a gossip," said Dolores. "I'm an old woman with a hip. It's got a click. It aches in the rain. When I retired, I told Ayesha I'd come in Fridays, and I told myself I'd take the stairs. I've taken the stairs."

"Dolores," I said. "It's a two-hour procedure. No incision. The pod maps the joint, regrows the cartilage, and you walk out. Naomi's team has done three hundred."

"I know how many they've done. I read the pamphlets."

"Then you know the outcomes."

"I know the outcomes. They're excellent." She set her fork down. "That isn't what I'm saying."

I tried again. "The company would cover it. It costs us almost nothing."

"There it is."

"There what is?"

"That voice. The one that thinks if the numbers are right, the argument's over." She wasn't angry. If anything she sounded tired, and a little fond. "You know what your mother used to say about favors? A favor you can't pay back is a debt that ties your hands. She never took one in her life. She'd have walked to the hospital before she took a ride from a man she couldn't repay."

"It isn't a favor."

"Then why are you looking at me like you're asking for something?"

Nobody spoke. Marcus studied his beer. I opened my mouth and found I had nothing in it.

"I'd rather have a hip that has a story," Dolores said, more quietly. "I fell off a loading dock in 'ninety-nine, and the click is where I landed. I'm not sure I want a hip that doesn't remember."

"That isn't how cartilage works," Naomi said, gently.

"I'm aware of how cartilage works. I'm talking about me."

Naomi let that sit. Then she turned to me, and her voice was the one she used in the loft eleven years ago, flat and steady. "She's right about one thing. It's her call, and a competent adult has the right to turn down a safe procedure. I'm her doctor's colleague, not her doctor. If she changes her mind, the pod is there. If she doesn't, we manage the pain the old-fashioned way." She raised an eyebrow. "You're allowed to think she's wrong. You're not allowed to keep pushing."

"I wasn't pushing."

"You were building a case," Gloria said, from her chair. "I've watched you do it for eleven years. It's very thorough. You'd do well in front of a judge."

"I can't help it if the facts are on my side."

"The facts are on your side," Dolores said. "They're always on your side. That's why you're hard to argue with and easy to refuse."

That one landed. I sat on the stool with my hands on my knees, and Ayesha, who had gone quiet beside the pizza, started stacking plates with great concentration.

"Okay," I said, finally. "I'll stop."

"You'll start again in a month."

"Probably."

"Ask me at seventy-one," Dolores said. She picked her fork back up. "I may be tired of the click by then. Today I'm not."

---

Ayesha brought out the dominoes after that, a battered double-nine set in a cigar box that had belonged to her father. We played at the parts counter, five of us, with a house rule that anyone who counted tiles out loud would be fed to the lift.

"That's cheating," Dolores said, ten minutes in.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're thinking it. I can hear it." She leaned across the counter and tapped my temple with one blunt finger. "It makes a noise."

"It does not make a noise."

"It's a quiet noise, like a refrigerator." She set down a tile. "Nobody counts at my table. You play what you hold."

I played what I held. I lost the first hand, and the second, and then a third in which I laid down a double six with a flourish and Ayesha, who had been waiting for it all night, went out with a play that made the whole room groan. Gloria insisted on keeping score, with a precision that suggested she did not trust any of us. By the fourth hand, I'd stopped doing arithmetic and started paying attention to Dolores's face, which turned out to be much more useful. She had a tell. When she held a strong tile, her mouth went flat.

"You're looking at me," she said.

"I'm admiring you."

"Admire the tiles."

I won the fifth hand, narrowly. Dolores accepted it with a grunt and announced that I'd earned a piece of the cake. The radio shifted to a song my mother used to hum, and one of the mechanics turned it up, and the room, without anyone saying anything, started to sing along badly. Okonkwo, I noticed, knew every word.

---

I found Marcus on the loading dock a little after nine, sitting on an overturned milk crate with his back against the cinderblock and a view of the dark lot. A single sodium lamp lit the pavement. The party's noise came through the wall in muffled waves.

"Take the other crate," he said. "It's cleaner."

I sat. For a while neither of us said anything. A truck went by on the road beyond the fence, and its headlights slid across the lot and went out.

"Mina turned eighteen in April," Marcus said.

"I remember. I signed the card."

"Helena signed the card. You wrote your name under it." He said it without bite. "She's leaving in September. She got into the engineering program at Michigan. She wants to build something to do with water." He rolled the beer bottle between his palms. "I realized last month that I haven't been to one of her recitals since she was twelve."

"Marcus."

"I'm not asking you to say anything. I want to tell you what I saw." He looked at the lot. "I've been in that test hall for eighteen months. Twelve hours a day, some weekends. I told myself it was the work. I told myself the coils needed me, and they did. But I'm fifty, and my daughter is going to be a stranger by Christmas, and I'm the one who let it happen."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. I chose it." He tipped the bottle toward me. "I'm saying it because you're doing the same thing."

"I'm not doing it with a daughter."

"No. You're doing it with everyone." He said it gently. "How long since you ate dinner with somebody who didn't work for you?"

I started to answer. I stopped. "Callisto," I said. "Teodora Marsh gave me a roll."

"Does that count?"

"It's the most recent one I can think of."

"That's eleven days ago, and she works for you." He laughed once, without much humor. "I'm not telling you to quit. I couldn't if I wanted to. I'm telling you that nobody built this place so you could be the only one in it who isn't allowed to leave."

I didn't have a reply. The song through the wall ended, and a different one began.

"I used to know what I'd do after," I said. It was the first time I'd put it into words, and I felt slightly foolish saying it to a man holding a beer on a milk crate.

"After what?"

"After it was built. When I started, I figured at some point it would be built. The company, the labs. I'd have a house with a real kitchen and a day with nothing on it."

"And?"

"And every time it's nearly built, there's a new part that needs building."

Marcus nodded slowly, as though I had confirmed a measurement. "It's never built," he said. "I thought you knew that. It's a shop. It's like your mother's. You don't finish it, you just keep it running, and you hope you've got the right people holding the wrenches." He finished the beer. "The trick is to be one of the people holding the wrench, not the guy who stands in the doorway with a clipboard."

"That's your advice?"

"It's my observation. I'm not that wise." He stood, joints popping. "Come to Mina's graduation party in August. She asked for you. I mean she asked for you specifically, because you once spent a whole afternoon showing her how a linear actuator worked, and she's never forgotten."

"I don't remember that."

"I know," Marcus said. "That's why she remembers."

---

By ten, the party had thinned. Gloria's car had come for her. Naomi left with Marcus, still arguing, about whether a particular ceramic was porous, and Okonkwo had been talked into driving one of the mechanics home. That left Ayesha stacking chairs, Helena folding the plastic tablecloth with military corners, and Dolores, still crowned, sitting on her padded stool beside the center lift, watching the lights.

I went to the parts counter and picked up the cake knife and the last of the plates. Dolores watched me stack them.

"You're good with your hands," she said.

"I had a good teacher."

"Two." She shifted her weight, and I heard the click, and neither of us mentioned it. "Ayesha's been talking about a second shop. Did she tell you?"

"No."

"She doesn't want your money." She said it flatly, a fact. "She went to the credit union. She'd like to get turned down first, so she's earned the right to be annoyed."

"I'd give her the loan."

"I know you would. That's why she didn't ask." She turned her head. "You're not the answer to every question, Kenji. You're allowed to be the second person somebody calls."

I thought about that while I wiped the knife. It was a good line, and I decided I would not tell her so, because she'd have said I was admiring it again.

"Can I ask you for something?" Dolores said.

"Anything."

"There it is again." But she smiled. "Come Friday. Not every one. I'm not greedy. The one after next. I've got a Civic in the back bay with a bad CV boot, and my hands won't hold the light the way they used to."

"You want me to hold a flashlight."

"I want you to hold it steady and not say a word about efficiency." She adjusted the crown, which had slipped. "Your mother liked company on the jobs she hated. I never told you that. She'd have you hold something. She'd say it was for the light, but it wasn't."

I put the knife down. For a moment the shop was just a shop: the pegboard with its marker outlines, the radio humming a station I'd never changed, Ayesha dragging the last chair into the corner and pretending not to listen.

"The one after next," I said.

"In the afternoon. Not five a.m."

"I'll be there."

"You'll be late," Dolores said. "That's all right. I'll start without you."

Helena appeared at my elbow, the folded tablecloth under one arm and her tablet, I noticed, nowhere in sight. She didn't look at me. She looked at Dolores, and Dolores looked back, and something passed between them that I wasn't meant to read.

"It's in the calendar," Helena said. "Non-movable."

"Who put it there?"

"I did. An hour ago." She handed me my jacket. "I assumed you'd say yes."

Outside, the lot was cool and still. Okonkwo had the car waiting at the curb with the engine off, and the interior light was on, a small yellow square in the dark. Behind us, Ayesha switched off the work lights one string at a time. The faded red sign over the door caught the last of the streetlight, and I stood there longer than I needed to, with my jacket on my arm.

"Home?" Helena asked.

"Home," I said. "Not the office."

"Not the office," she agreed, and held the door.
