# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (36)*

### *Houston, 2056*

**February 2056.** I squared the four reports on the table until their edges lined up. There was one for each person who had written one. Marchetti's was the thickest, and Vance's was two pages in pencil.

Senator Whitcombe had called on Monday. "The faction meets on Friday, Mr. Himura," she said. "Every one of them has been treated, or has a wife who has, and not one of them has been asked what happens on the day someone comes for it. I would rather bring them a plan than a worry. Mm. A plan with a job in it for each of them."

"You'll have it before they sit down. Do you want one page, or a page for each of them?"

"One," she said. "I will put it in front of them myself."

It was Wednesday night now, and I had read the four reports twice.

Marchetti wrote on the licenses. All three clinics held licenses she had negotiated before the doors opened, and every one was in force. None named an owner except the foundation and its trustee. Her last line read: *The paper is sound. The one name on it that can be found is mine.*

Strauss wrote on Oyama. The Cleveland plant was running full shifts, and the arrays took in every ton of scrap that reached the gate. He named January of 2058 as the month Oyama could begin selling Primitive pods to the public. His last line read: *Nine thousand people work here and none of them has been told who draws the designs.*

Vance wrote on the platoon. Forty fighters and the droid squads stood at the three clinics and the warehouse, and every post was covered. His last line read: *Every one of them goes home at night to somebody.*

Ashby wrote on communications. The lines between sites ran on data crystals that no one could tap. His last page was about November.

In November a physician had asked one of our nurses how the treatment worked. He had offered her money. His patient, a man we had treated that spring, remembered being treated and remembered the nurse, and nothing else. The doctor had gone to the only door he could find. The nurse reported it the same day, and the doctor left the country within the week. No one learned who he worked for.

I read that page again. Elite sales had been running since April. Someone had seen a man come back from a trip younger than he left and had started asking how. I had not expected anyone to ask this soon.

I washed my hands at the sink in the corner and came back to the table. Then I opened a clean sheet and wrote four words down the left margin: political, commercial, financial, press. Beside each I left a blank for a name.

**Friday.** The faction met in the conference room at Marchetti's firm after the staff had gone home. Eight people sat around the table. All of them had been recruited on the same terms as Whitcombe, and all of them could hear what I had to say and could not repeat it. My two guards stood on either side of the door, and nobody looked at them twice. I took the chair against the wall.

I laid the sheet in front of Whitcombe. "Four fronts, and each has one owner who decides. Political is yours, Senator. If a ministry moves on a clinic, your committee asks its questions before anyone locks a door. Is that enough authority, or do you need more?"

She read the line twice. "It is enough. Commercial is Mr. Strauss's."

Strauss turned the steel ring on his finger. "Oyama can bury a rival in orders by Monday. Or starve him of parts by Wednesday." He looked at me and not at the table. "Tell me which, and I'll have the trucks moving."

"Financial is Marchetti's. The foundation holds every term and every design."

Marchetti capped her pen. "And nothing moves without my signature. I would like that entered in the minutes of this meeting, with the date." She waited until the woman beside her had written it down. "Press goes to him," I said. A man across the table, who owned two newspapers and a wire service, lifted two fingers from the wood. "Give me a day and a story," he said, "and I'll give you the page."

A woman halfway down the table had not touched her pen. "If someone follows one of the clients home, they have us. Not one of them lives behind a locked gate."

"They can't follow. Every transport is random. The route and the day change each time."

"Who knows the route?"

"The client and Janus."

"Not the client's driver?"

"Not the driver, and not the client's wife." I kept my voice level. "Is that short enough for you?"

"It is short." She wrote it down and did not look up again.

Whitcombe had not looked at the sheet since I put it in front of her. She was looking at me. She pushed her reading glasses up onto her head.

"Well. I find nothing wrong with the paper, the money, the plants, or the newspapers, which is why I would like to ask about the one thing that sits on none of the four." She let a breath go by. "Your staff, Mr. Himura. The nurse the doctor offered money to in November, and the drivers, and the clerks. Who protects them when they go home at night?"

Nobody at the table spoke. I had four fronts and an owner for each of them. Not one of the four covered the people who worked for us.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Byron Pyrrus, as Anthony Strauss (68)*

### *Houston and the yard, 2057*

**February 2057.** Byron sat at Kenji Himura's side at the long table in the Houston office, with the drive drawings spread in front of them both, and turned his steel ring on his finger as he read. Across the top of the first sheet it said GRAVITIC DRIVE. Below that were a flat ring of coils around a core, a mounting plate, and a column of figures down the margin in Himura's square capitals.

"It lifts a hull and moves it," Himura said. "The sleds Oyama builds are the small version. They lift a load a short way and burn a great deal of power doing it. This is the one we keep. Which part do you want first?"

"Skip the sleds. How big a hull?"

"The shuttle first, fifty meters. A starship after that."

Byron followed the figures down the margin with one finger. In forty years he had never built anything that left the ground. He did not ask where Himura had learned this. He had stopped asking in the first week.

"What does each part cost?"

"The coils and the core come out of the arrays, so they cost power and time, not money. The reactor is a tokamak, and that's yours. The plates are the same materials NASA built its shuttles from, cast in sections. Gundarium Alpha comes later, when we have a yard in orbit."

"Cast where?"

"Cleveland. The keel sections are bigger than any press has cast, and yours is the closest." Himura squared the sheets against the table edge. "I can draw the section. I can't tell you what your press can cast. Can it?"

Byron knew the Cleveland press. It cast the pod lids, and it was the only one in the country big enough for those. "That's a drawing. How long is the section?"

"Longer than the bed."

"Then you're asking the wrong question. What has to change, the press or the section?" He did not wait for the answer. "The section. Cast it in three and join it. Can it be joined?"

"Yes."

"Then it's three castings back to back." The press shut for its rebuild on the first of July and would not be back until October. Any plate not cast by then would wait the autumn. "I need the patterns ordered in two weeks and the press booked for June. Tuesday for the patterns would be better than Wednesday."

"What do you need from me?"

"Your signature on the order. Today, not Friday."

Himura took the pen.

**June to December 2057.** The three keel castings came off the press in the third week of June, nine days before the shutdown. Byron stood beside the bed and watched the last one cool. He rode with them to the yard on the flatbed.

The yard lay on the Gulf behind a double fence. Himura's security droids walked the fence at night, loud and clanking, and the crews had stopped looking up at them by the second week. Cranes and robot arms moved plate along the slips. The keel of the starship went down on the long cradle, three castings joined, and the crews began to raise ribs. On the shorter cradle beside it the shuttle took shape, fifty meters of frame, then skin.

A foreman stopped him at the foot of the gantry in August. "What's it for, Mr. Strauss? The ship."

"It's a ship. You build it, I pay for it." The foreman went back to his crew. He came back in September with the same question.

"You asked in August."

"I did. Same answer?"

"Still good in September."

His knees did not hurt on the gantry ladder. He climbed it every morning to look at the work, and he came down at night to read the day's count. He had run Oyama for forty years and never watched anything he built leave the ground. The night the drive ring was set, he stood on the gantry with both hands flat on the rail. The ring hummed for the first time, and he felt it in the steel under his boots.

The shuttle was finished in December. A yard clerk brought him the registration card on a clipboard. Oyama's name stood at the top, and below it were lines for the builder and the owner. Byron signed on the builder's line: *Anthony Strauss.*

The owner's line was empty.

"The owner line is left blank on all of them, sir," the clerk said. "Standing instruction."

"Who signs it, then?"

"Nobody, sir. Not on any of them."

Byron looked at the empty line. In forty years he had never signed a card that did not say whose it was. He turned it over. The back was blank.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (26)*

### *Galveston, autumn 2057*

**Autumn 2057.** Hannah laid her father's tape along the lid of the crate and read the number twice before she would sign for it. The tape was steel and yellow, its brass tip worn flat, and it had measured every door in the clinic. The length matched the custody sheet. She measured the width, and it matched too.

The clinic had signed for every Primitive pod Oyama built, because the pods belonged to the foundation until they were sold. This crate was the first that would go on to a buyer. The sheet had to be signed before the seven o'clock whistle, or the crate would sit on the quay another day.

Last December three men had come over the dock gate at three in the morning. The gray frames had them on the concrete before she reached the quay, and Kenji's people took them away. She wrote the incident report herself and never heard back.

A steel plate on the crate's side carried Oyama's name and a line headed *Designer*. The line was blank. It was blank on every Oyama plate she had seen.

"Jefa." Rafa Ibarra stood at her elbow with a clipboard of his own. He wore a steel frame strapped over his coveralls, hinged at the hip and knee, and the joints ticked when he shifted his weight. "The whistle, jefa. Ya casi."

"Almost," she said in Spanish. "One minute."

She had known him since her first week on the job. He was the best rigger on the quay, and he had taught her half the words her father never would. On the next berth a gray Oyama arm on a steel rail was tying down a stack of containers. One man stood beside it with a controller in his hand. It had been there since September.

"That does the lashing?" she asked.

"A stack in forty minutes." He said it to the arm and not to her. "My crew used to do that in a day."

He tapped the flat steel sled at his feet. It floated a hand's breadth above the concrete and did not move. "This lifts two tons for twenty minutes, then it has to rest. Same as me, jefa."

She did not laugh, and he had not expected her to. "How many of these do you have?"

"Three sleds." He still did not look at her. "We were eight on the crew in the spring. Five went home in September, jefa. Their last shift was a Friday."

She signed the sheet and gave him the copy. The whistle blew while she was folding hers. She put the tape in her pocket and did not say anything.

**That evening.** Kenji was standing at the edge of the clinic roof. It was where she had expected to find him. The first question on her list was why he stood at the edge of every room, and there he was at the edge of a whole roof, with the Gulf behind him and his two quiet men at the stair door.

"Rafa Ibarra's crew was eight in the spring," she said. "It's three now. Where did the other five go?"

"I don't know."

"Oyama sells the arms and the frames. Somebody over there knows who it fired."

"Somebody should." He turned from the rail. "I'll find out."

She had come up the stairs ready for a smaller answer. She stood with her hands in her jacket pockets. "Do you want me to stop asking?"

"No."

She looked at him for a while. The wind off the water lifted the corner of the custody sheet in her pocket. "Have dinner with me. Somewhere that isn't my mother's table."

"Which night?"

"Thursday."

**Thursday.** The diner on Seawall Boulevard had six booths and a counter, and a cook who did not look up. He sat with his back to the wall and ordered what she ordered. When the plates came he began to eat, and he did not stop until the plate was clean. She watched him finish the parsley.

"Was it hard to leave the Navy?" he said.

It was the first question he had ever asked her about herself. She put her fork down. She had expected him to talk about the clinic, or the weather, or nothing.

"Not leaving," she said. "Okay, so. Leaving was easy. The hard part was the first week after, when nobody told me where to stand."

"Where did you stand?"

"In my father's yard, handing him wrenches." She picked her fork up again. "Then my father talked me into the clinic job."

She talked for forty minutes. She told him about her first ship, the *Kearsarge*, and her first commanding officer, a lieutenant commander named Reyes who would not let anyone sit down on watch. She told him about the three days she had sat in the clinic waiting room in front of a bare wall, with a folded uniform on the chair beside her and a napkin in her pocket. He listened. He did not look at the clock, and he did not square anything. When she stopped he asked one more question, which was what she had wanted to do before the Navy, and she had to think.

At a corner table two men in plain jackets sat with two cups of coffee. Neither man touched his cup. She took them for his security.

Outside, her car stood alone at the curb under a streetlight. He walked her to it with his hands in his pockets. She turned and kissed him once.

He stood with his hands at his sides.

"What do you need?" he said.

She laughed until she had to lean on the car.

"That," she said. "Again."

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (38)*

### *The Gulf coast, January 2058*

I squared the checklist, the flight log, and the pen against the edge of the shuttle's panel, then squared them again. The ring was in my jacket pocket. It had been there since Tuesday, and I had touched it often enough that the steel was warm.

The shuttle stood at the end of the pad on its cradle, fifty meters of dark plate with the hatch open. Strauss's crews had finished it in December, and I had flown it twice with Ashby's range crew. I knew it would hold, as I knew every weld in it. The weather window had opened forty minutes early. I logged that and went on. I had told no one the date, and the shuttle's transponder was off.

What I did not know was what Hannah would say when I told her where we were going.

She came across the pad at six in her flight jacket, with her father's tape in the pocket. She stopped at the foot of the ramp and looked at the shuttle for a long time. Then she looked at me.

"You said you wanted to show me something."

"This is it."

My two guards stood at the edge of the pad, where I had told them to stay. She nodded to them, as she did every day. "They're not coming?"

"No. They stay here. Do you want them to come?"

"I want to know why they're not." She waited. When nothing came she let it go. "Where are we going?"

"Around the Moon. We'll be back before dark."

She did not laugh. She looked up at the hull, then at the hatch, and the corner of her mouth moved. "Okay, so. Before I get on, tell me what you can."

"Three things. Janus owns the designs. I run the people who guard them. And this is the first of my places I have shown you. There are others, and you haven't seen them yet."

"Janus." She turned the name over. "How does it fly?"

"I can't answer that."

"Who drew it?"

"I can't answer that either."

"Then why show me?"

"You keep asking what I'm hiding. This is part of it."

She walked up the ramp.

She took the right seat and checked her harness twice, as she must have done a thousand times on the *Kearsarge*. I lifted the shuttle off the cradle. The drive made a low hum that I felt in the seat, and the pad dropped away. The Gulf became a flat sheet, then a curve. She watched the glass and said nothing.

For a long time the Moon was only a small gray disc. Then it filled the window. We went around the far side, and I brought us down to a few thousand meters over the surface. Craters ran under us in rows, and a long ridge threw a shadow across a plain. She put her palm flat on the glass.

I took the ring from my pocket and held it open in my hand. It was a plain band of steel with no stone. I had cut her name inside it in square capitals, so small she would need a lens to read it.

"I want to marry you," I said. "Before you answer, I have to tell you what I can't give you. I have no family. I can't tell you where I'm from. There are parts of my life you won't be shown for a long time, and some of them perhaps never."

"You've rehearsed that."

"Four times. To the lathe."

I kept my hand open. "I will never say a false thing to you. That I can promise."

"Is that the best you have?"

"It's the part I'm sure of."

She looked at the ring and not at me. The surface slid past below us. Then she closed my fingers over it. "I'll answer on the way home."

I took us up and around. The Earth came over the edge of the Moon, small and blue, and grew in the window. She did not speak until the Gulf was a curve again, and then she spoke without turning her head.

"Yes. And I'm going to keep asking."

"I'd be sorry if you stopped."

I opened my hand. She held out hers, and I put the ring on her finger. It fit. She turned her hand in the light from the window.

We landed before dark, as I had said. She walked down the ramp still looking at her hand.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · The Rival*

### *A study, early 2058*

A floor plan lay on the desk with one wing shaded in pencil and no door drawn. He had done the shading himself. The plan was of the Galveston clinic, bought from a city office, and the shaded wing was the east one, the only part of the building the plan gave no use for. Under it lay a log with his own signature on the last page, and three words in the watcher's hand: *Best guess. Unconfirmed.*

His security chief stood across the desk with the report on the third raid in her hands. She would close it on Friday. He had until then to say whether the matter closed with it.

"Read it," he said.

"You know it, sir." She did not open the folder. "Galveston, December of 2056. Three men through the dock gate at three in the morning. Singapore, August of last year. Six, by a side entrance. Galveston again this winter. Twelve."

"And?"

"None came out. Nothing in the police log, nothing in the papers. The clinic opened at eight every morning." She turned a page she did not need. "I don't know how far the twelve got, sir. I don't know if they are alive."

He looked at the plan. In November of 2056 he had told his man to find out who owned that clinic. Fourteen months later he had a charity foundation registered in Houston, a trustee no one could find, and a lawyer's mailing box for an address.

"A charity does not make three crews disappear," he said.

"No."

"What is behind that wing?"

"I never saw a door. Nobody has come back to tell me where one is." She set the folder on the desk, squared to the edge. "I came to say that it should close here."

He did not know what was in that wing, or how far any of his men had gone into it. Three of the men he had sat across from at dinner for twenty years had gone away on private checkups and come back ten years younger. He had asked each of them once where they had been. Each had smiled and said they had been looked after, and the second time he did not ask. Every car that took them there had gone somewhere that took cars at night. Something important was in that wing, and a year of raids had told him only that the people inside were not afraid of anyone coming through the dock.

"A fourth raid would end the same way," he said.

"Yes, sir. Whoever is inside deals with it themselves. They have never once called the police."

"Then I will not knock again."

He picked up the pencil. If they could not walk in, he would make it come out.

"Find me a gate guard who can be bought," he said. "A contractor who looks after the lamps in that car park. A driver on the Thursday delivery."

"They will take a year to place."

"Then it takes a year." He drew a line on the plan from the dock to the east wing. "A bomb at the handover, when the dock is open and the van is at the door. They bring out what is in that wing, or they move it. Either way I see it."

"And if the clinic doubles its guards afterward?"

"By then it won't matter. A lamp that is out. A stool that is empty. A van that isn't the usual van. Nobody looks until it is done."

She did not write anything down. "There will be people on the dock at the handover, sir. The night staff, going off."

He did not answer. He took the order sheet from the drawer and wrote three jobs down the left margin: *gate, lamps, van.* At the bottom was the line for his signature.

She waited for him to sign it or to tell her to close the file. He held the pen over the line and did not lower it.
